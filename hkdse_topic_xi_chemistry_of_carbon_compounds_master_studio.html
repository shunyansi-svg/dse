Recorded wrong answers: a chiral carbon "connected to four different molecules" (read: atoms or groups), and "the molecules rotate to different directions under plane-polarised light" (CP 2015 Q13). Syllabus Master Overview

Topic XI Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(e))

Every What students should learn / be able to strand of the Chemistry of Carbon Compounds, with HKEAA watch-outs from the mark-scheme keyword bank, the Annex to Part II, and Candidates’ Performance reports 2012–2025. Suggested duration: 25 hours — the largest organic topic in the curriculum.

Topic XI (a) Homologous series

Ten families, four formula languages, names up to eight carbons

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Homologous series: a family of carbon compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula, showing a gradation in physical properties and similar chemistry — members differ by a $\text{CH}_{2}$ unit.
  • Give systematic names, general formulae, condensed formulae and structural formulae for: alkanes, alkenes, haloalkanes, alcohols, aldehydes and ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, unsubstituted amides and primary amines — "with carbon chains not more than eight carbon atoms" (NTF-2018 II-B XI (a)).
  • Roots meth- (1C) eth- (2C) prop- (3C) but- (4C) pent- (5C) hex- (6C) hept- (7C) oct- (8C); suffix carries the functional group (-ane, -ene, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -oate, -amide, -amine), halogen branches are prefixes (chloro-, bromo-, iodo-).
  • Draw structures from names — including locant sets like but-2-ene, propan-2-ol, 3,3-dichloropropene and 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol (the NTF’s own worked examples of examinable naming) (NTF-2018 II-B XI (a)).
  • Name compounds with multiple functional groups of the same type — e.g. propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol); identify common trivial names: formaldehyde, chloroform, acetone, isopropyl alcohol, acetic acid.
  • Explain how functional groups and carbon-chain length govern boiling point, volatility and solubility — longer chain ⇒ stronger van der Waals’ forces; -OH/-COOH groups ⇒ hydrogen bonding; “G has a smaller relative molecular mass than F, so G has a lower boiling point” (ms bank).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Terminology is marked, not guessed: candidates who used the "'hydroxy group', 'alcohol' and 'alkanol'" trio interchangeably, or wrote "'keytone' instead of 'ketone'", were named in the report (CP 2012 Paper 2 context). Suffix discipline: prop-1-ene vs propane differ by one letter and one whole chemistry. For mixed-function compounds the ranking game is over — "the use of order of priority of principal functional groups is not expected" (NTF-2018 II-B XI (a)); chains beyond eight carbons never appear in names.

Topic XI (b) Isomerism

Same molecular formula, different structures — chains, positions, groups, and geometry

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different display/structural formula. Enumerate methodically — shorten the chain (butane/methylpropane, C₅: three), slide the group (butan-1-ol vs butan-2-ol, the four $\text{C}_{4}\text{H}_{9}\text{OH}$ alcohols), then move it between families ($\text{C}_{3}\text{H}_{6}\text{O}$ propanal vs propanone; $\text{C}_{3}\text{H}_{6}\text{O}_{2}$ propanoic acid vs methyl ethanoate).
  • Predict existence of structural isomerism "which includes isomers containing the same functional group and isomers containing different functional groups" — the syllabus’s own two-way split.
  • Cis-trans isomerism arises from restricted rotation about a C=C bond in acyclic compounds with one double bond; each double-bond carbon must carry two different groups (but-2-ene shows it; but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene do not — one C carries two H or two CH₃).
  • Enantiomerism: recognise it "in compounds with only one chiral carbon" — four different atoms/groups on one sp³ carbon (butan-2-ol, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid / lactic acid, CH₃CHClCH₂CH₃); show structures with models; know enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light "by the same extent, but in opposite directions" (NTF-2018 II-B XI (b)).
  • Use structural formulae and molecular models "to demonstrate the arrangement of atoms in isomers"; count carefully — CP 2015 Q26’s diene collapses to 3 geometrical isomers because symmetry merges two arrangements.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The report punishes sloppy counts and labels: "wrongly mentioned that the alkenes are positional isomers" (CP 2013 Q15); "gave incorrect answers such as 'positional isomers', 'geometrical isomers', and 'structural isomers'" when one exact relationship was asked (CP 2016 Q13(c)). Rotations of a drawn structure are not new isomers; ‘isomers’ must never be explained with ‘isotopes’ (CP 2013 Paper 2 context). Depth caps: "describing and explaining properties of specific examples of cis-trans isomers such as butenedioic acid are not expected", and "details of polarimetry and concepts related to racemic mixture are not expected" (NTF-2018 II-B XI (b)) — recognition and the one rotation sentence is all enantiomerism ever asks.

Topic XI (c) Ⅰ Hydrocarbons · halides · alcohols

Typical reactions Ⅰ — alkanes, alkenes, haloalkanes, alcohols (Annex chart, upper half)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Alkanes: substitution with halogens — $\text{CH}_{4}+\text{Cl}_{2}\xrightarrow{\text{UV light / sunlight}}\text{CH}_{3}\text{Cl}+\text{HCl}$; “X₂: $\text{Cl}_{2},\text{Br}_{2}$ … UV light, light or heat” (annex); excess methane favours the mono-substituted product (CP 2015 Q6).
  • Alkenes: addition of hydrogen ($\text{H}_{2}$, “platinum (Pt), nickel (Ni) or palladium (Pd)” (annex)), of halogens ($\text{X}_{2}$ in organic solvent — orange/brown → colourless) and of hydrogen halides ($\text{HX}$: HF, HCl, HBr, HI).
  • Markovnikov’s rule predicts the major product of unsymmetrical alkene + HX: “the hydrogen atom of hydrogen halide is added to the carbon atom of the carbon-carbon double bond that attached with a greater number of hydrogen atoms” (ms bank) — propene + HBr → 2-bromopropane as major product. “Prediction of major product by Markovnikov’s Rule is required” (annex).
  • Haloalkanes: substitution with $\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$ → alcohols (heat); the chart notes “Relative reactivity of different haloalkanes is not required” (annex).
  • Alcohols → haloalkanes with “HX: HCl, HBr, HI” or “$\text{PX}_{3}$: $\text{PCl}_{3},\text{PBr}_{3},\text{PI}_{3}$” — “the use of the notations HX and $\text{PX}_{3}$ as the reagents is acceptable … experimental details of how to produce $\text{HX}$ and $\text{PX}_{3}$ … are not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (c)).
  • Alcohols → alkenes: dehydration over “$\text{Al}_{2}\text{O}_{3}$, heat or conc. $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$, heat” (annex); oxidation of 1° alcohols to aldehydes and carboxylic acids and 2° alcohols to ketones with “$\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}\text{(aq)}/\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$, heat” (annex) — orange → green.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Product drawing is marked on bonds, not vibes: the addition of HBr/Br₂ to propenes produced three printed wrong structures — both halogens on one carbon, wrong carbon valences (CP 2016 Q3). 1° alcohol oxidation is a two-target row: distil for the aldehyde, reflux for the acid — CP’s apparatus swap (distillation drawn where reflux asked) cost marks (CP 2016 Q13(a)). A 3° alcohol has no C–H to lose: 2-methylpropan-2-ol with acidified dichromate gives no reaction — do not write propanone from it.

Topic XI (c) Ⅱ Carbonyls · acids · esters · amides

Typical reactions Ⅱ — aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters, amides (Annex chart, lower half)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Aldehydes → carboxylic acids with “$\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}\text{(aq)}/\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$, heat” (annex); ketones resist this oxidation — that gap is the chemical test pair, together with 2,4-DNPH: “yellow / orange / red precipitate … [Do not accept brown]” (ms bank).
  • Reduction of aldehydes and ketones → alcohols: “1. $\text{LiAlH}_{4}$, dry ether 2. $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$” or “$\text{NaBH}_{4}\text{(aq)}$” (annex) — carbonyl back to hydroxyl.
  • Esterification: “Carboxylic acid + Alcohol $\xrightarrow{\text{conc. }\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}\text{, heat}}$ Ester + Water” (annex); observation “fruit smell / pleasant odour substance formed” (ms bank); reversible — the acid catalyst is concentrated.
  • Ester hydrolysis both ways: $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$, heat → carboxylic acid + alcohol; $\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$, heat → carboxylate + alcohol (acidify afterwards to free the acid) — bank equation “$\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{COOCH}_{3}+\text{NaOH}\rightarrow\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{COONa}+\text{CH}_{3}\text{OH}$” (ms bank).
  • Amide formation: carboxylic acid → unsubstituted amide via “1. $\text{PCl}_{3}$ 2. $\text{NH}_{3}$” or directly “$\text{NH}_{3}$, heat” (annex) — and it is capped: “Amide formation from carboxylic acids is confined to unsubstituted amide” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (c)).
  • Amide hydrolysis: alkaline — “$\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$, heat → carboxylate + ammonia”; acidic — “$\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$, heat → carboxylic acid + ammonium salt” (annex); and reduction of carboxylic acids to 1° alcohols with $\text{LiAlH}_{4}$ then $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$ (annex).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Alkaline hydrolysis gives the carboxylate salt, never the free acid, while the mixture is basic — “the alkaline hydrolysis of ethanamide would give ammonia. If the reaction mixture is heated under reflux, the ammonia formed will be liberated as a gas” was the whole 2013 MC (CP 2013 Q32). Reduce selectively: "$\text{LiAlH}_{4}$ / $\text{NaBH}_{4}$ (reducing agents for carbonyls)" (ms bank) — but LiAlH₄ in dry ether first, never “in an acidic aqueous medium” (CP 2016 Q13(b) context). The distinguishing-test mark wants “correct reagent” plus “correct observations with comparison” — one colour alone scores nothing (ms bank).

Topic XI (d) Inter-conversions

The organic roundabout — convert any taught group into any other, then purify it

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Inter-conversions between the functional groups using only the (c)/Annex reactions: alkane → haloalkane → alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid → ester / amide — and backwards via hydrogenation, hydrolysis and reduction; “suggest routes to convert one functional group into another by using the reactions described in (c)”.
  • State reagents and conditions for every hop and predict major organic products “with given starting materials, reagents and reaction conditions”; multi-step schemes earn “step marks for multi-step organic synthesis” (ms bank).
  • Choose conditions for the target: distil the aldehyde off as it forms (its low b.p. lets it escape the flask) to stop a 1° oxidation at the –CHO; reflux with excess oxidant drives it on to the acid; aqueous $\text{OH}^{-}$ substitutes haloalkanes to alcohols; concentrated $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ and heat dehydrate alcohols to alkenes.
  • Describe laboratory preparations and purification of simple carbon compounds such as ethanoic acid and ester — reflux set-up, then separating funnel (“remove water (the lower liquid layer)”), drying (“anhydrous magnesium sulphate as the most suitable drying agent for ethyl ethanoate” by rejection logic (CP 2014 Paper 2 context)) and final “fractional distillation” (ms bank + CP 2014).
  • Calculate the percentage yield: moles of limiting reactant → theoretical moles of product → theoretical mass → % = actual ÷ theoretical × 100 — a listed (d) outcome.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Route discipline beats route length: examiners’ STSE note says “the best synthetic route may not be the one with the fewest steps or the lowest cost”; candidates lost marks by “including inappropriate steps” or by “stating inappropriate reagents for the steps” (CP 2016 Q12), and one proposed treating the intermediate with concentrated acid “to give a non-existing alkene before adding HBr” — a 1° benzylic alcohol cannot dehydrate (CP 2014 Q12 context). SBA reports twice blamed thin practical work on “isolating the product from the reaction mixture” (CP 2015, CP 2016 SBA comments) — rehearse the purification verbs: separate, dry, distil.

Topic XI (e) Important substances

Aspirin, soaps & detergents, nylon & polyesters — the examinable real molecules

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Aspirin = acetylsalicylic acid: identify its functional groups (carboxylic acid + ester on a benzene ring) and its uses — “relieve pain, reduce inflammation and fever, and the risk of heart attack”; the –OCOCH₃ link marks it as an acetylated phenol — structure recognition and hydrolysis products are the examinable angle (acetylation chemistry is not required in XI).
  • Soaps: salts of long-chain fatty acids (e.g. $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COO}^{-}\text{Na}^{+}$) made by alkaline hydrolysis (saponification) of fats/oils — “$3\text{NaOH} + \text{triglyceride} \rightarrow \text{glycerol} + 3\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COO}^{-}\text{Na}^{+}$” (ms bank); soapless detergents are made “from chemicals derived from petroleum”.
  • Explain wetting and emulsifying from structure: “The $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COO}^{-}$ ion has an ionic head ($\text{COO}^{-}$) and a hydrocarbon tail” — hydrophilic head in water, hydrophobic tail in grease; “The ionic heads of the grease droplets repel from each other” so the emulsion holds (ms bank).
  • Nylon and polyesters are condensation polymers: write their formation equations — nylon-6,6 from hexanedioic acid + 1,6-diaminohexane (amide links, $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ out; six $\text{CH}_{2}$ between the N's, four between the two C=O groups — count them: “only four, instead of six, -CH2 groups between the two C=O bonds” (CP 2016 Q31)); Terylene-type polyester from dicarboxylic acid + diol (ester links); state uses (ropes, fabrics, bottles).
  • Reason about biodegradability with bank logic: “There are many ester groups in PLA. These ester groups can be broken down in acidic / alkaline / bacterial conditions” (ms bank); distinguish thermoplastic (“becomes soft when heated, becomes hard when cooled”) from thermosetting (ms bank).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The ring never polymerises: addition polymerisation happens at vinyl $\text{C}=\text{C}$ bonds — “Bromination (addition of bromine) reaction occurs to the C=C bonds” (ms bank) — while benzene/phenyl rings stay out of it, and benzene stability arguments are “not expected” (NTF depth notes). Repeat units are drawn with brackets and a subscript n in the right place — “the omission of n” and stray extra bonds (“covalent bond between sodium and carbon”) are printed errors (CP 2016 Q5). And “analysing aspirin tablets by back titration is not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (e)) — that arithmetic belongs to IV/XV.

Homologous Series Inspector — Ten Families of Carbon Compounds

One functional group, one general formula, one naming pattern per family — and an examiner who checks all three. Names run to eight carbons, never further (NTF-2018 II-B XI (a)).

Pick a family

XI (a)

Homologous series — what makes a family a family

$$\text{one general formula} + \text{one functional group} + \text{a } \text{CH}_{2} \text{ step between neighbours}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition to state: a homologous series is a family of carbon compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula, whose members share chemical properties and show a gradation in physical properties as chain length grows.
  • Neighbours differ by exactly one $\text{CH}_{2}$ unit; every member’s name keeps the family’s suffix (-ane, -ene, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -oate, -amide, -amine).
  • Four formula languages, one substance: general ($\text{C}_{n}\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{OH}$), structural (all bonds shown), condensed ($\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH}_{2}\text{OH}$), molecular ($\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{OH}$) — the syllabus demands the first three explicitly.
  • Physical-property trend inside a family: more/longer chains ⇒ stronger van der Waals’ forces ⇒ higher boiling point; the functional group decides chemical behaviour, the chain modulates physical behaviour.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Compare volatility with the bank’s grammar: “G has a smaller relative molecular mass than F, so G has a lower boiling point / weaker van der Waals’ forces” (ms bank) — size first, force named, boiling point stated.
  • The suggested teaching list is a hint at what gets compared: propane, butane, pentane vs ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol — alkanes low b.p., alcohols far higher at similar mass because of hydrogen bonding (Topic VI language).
  • Solubility phrasing from the bank: “Both cinnamaldehyde and ethyl ethanoate are relatively non-polar compounds. Their molecules are attracted by weak intermolecular forces” (ms bank) — like dissolves like.
XI (a)

Alkanes — saturated hydrocarbons

$$\text{C}_{n}\text{H}_{2n+2}\qquad \text{functional group: none (all C–C / C–H single bonds)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • First members: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀, pentane C₅H₁₂, hexane C₆H₁₄, heptane C₇H₁₆, octane C₈H₁₈ — memorise the first eight; the root never changes, only -ane.
  • Saturated = only single bonds, maximum hydrogen; this is why alkanes do substitution, not addition, and why they lack the bromine/permanganate decolourisation tests of alkenes.
  • Properties: non-polar, insoluble in water, unreactive at room temperature (no polar bond to attack); good fuels — complete combustion gives $\text{CO}_{2}+\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$.
  • Hexane’s inertness is examinable: candidates who “thought that hexane would have reaction with metals” lost the 2012 corrosion MC (CP 2012 Q23) — alkanes do not attack iron or copper.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Combustion equations must balance and carry state symbols: $\text{CH}_{4}\text{(g)}+2\text{O}_{2}\text{(g)}\rightarrow\text{CO}_{2}\text{(g)}+2\text{H}_{2}\text{O}\text{(l)}$; omitting state symbols on fuel equations was a named error (CP 2014 Q6).
  • Cracking keeps the family story: “$\text{C}_{8}\text{H}_{18} \rightarrow \text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{6}+2\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH=CH}_{2}$” (ms bank) — one octane, three products, atoms conserved; CP flagged candidates who fumbled “the correct molecular formula of octane” and the coefficients (CP 2016 Q3).
⚠️ Safety line

C₁–C₄ alkanes are gases, C₅–C₈ volatile liquids — all highly flammable; the topic overview warns of “the hazards associated with the use and disposal of carbon compounds … their combustibility and toxicity” (syllabus, Values and Attitudes).

XI (a)

Alkenes — one C=C does everything

$$\text{C}_{n}\text{H}_{2n}\qquad \text{functional group: } \text{C}=\text{C (one, acyclic members)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • First members: ethene $\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{4}$, propene $\text{C}_{3}\text{H}_{6}$, but-1-ene and but-2-ene $\text{C}_{4}\text{H}_{8}$, pent-1-ene … — from four carbons the double bond needs a locant: but-2-ene, not “butene”.
  • Locant style: name as but-2-ene (number inside the name); the NTF itself prints both eras — “3,3-dichloropropene” and “2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (a)) — either is understood, the modern form is safest.
  • Unsaturated: the C=C takes addition (H₂, X₂, HX, steam) and decolourises bromine — the definition of the family’s chemistry in strand (c).
  • Flat around the double bond (Topic VI shape logic) ⇒ the restricted rotation that births cis-trans isomerism in strand (b).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • prop-1-ene is a real name, but propane is a different molecule — one letter “n”-vs-nothing errors were the classic suffix slip; the CP printed candidates who couldn’t write “propenoic acid” at all (CP 2016 Q5).
  • Boiling-point questions on alkene mixtures want chain length/molecular size, and separation wants boiling point, not melting point — “stated that the separation was based on the melting point of the alkanes” (CP 2014 Q6).
XI (a)

Haloalkanes — halogen as prefix, never suffix

$$\text{R–X (X = Cl, Br, I; F taught via HF)}\qquad \text{e.g. 2-bromopropane, 1-chlorobutane}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Name with fluoro-/chloro-/bromo-/iodo- prefixes plus the alkane root: bromomethane, 2-chloropropane, 1-bromo-2-methylpropane; number the chain to give the halogen the lowest locant set.
  • The polar C–X bond is the reaction site: substitution with $\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$ gives alcohols (strand c iii).
  • Trivial member to recognise: chloroform = $\text{CHCl}_{3}$ (listed trivial name family) — and CFC-style memory: haloalkanes’ ozone chemistry is Topic I/V link, not a XI reaction.
  • No reactivity ranking inside the family is needed: “Reactivity of haloalkanes in their substitution with hydroxide ions is not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (c)) — write the reaction, don’t race the halogens.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Structure drawings of halogen addition/substitution products carry wrong-valence errors (two Br on one carbon, five-bonded carbon) — check every carbon has four bonds (CP 2016 Q3).
  • The 2015 substitution item punished calling the reacting bromine species a bromide ion when the atom/radical sense was meant (CP 2015 Q6) — particles named wrongly score zero.
XI (a)

Alcohols — the -ol family and its 1°/2°/3° census

$$\text{C}_{n}\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{OH}\qquad \text{functional group: hydroxyl, –OH}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Names: methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol, butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol; multiple -OH keeps the chain name: ethane-1,2-diol, propane-1,2,3-triol (NTF-2018 II-B XI (a)).
  • Classify by the carbon carrying –OH: 1° RCH₂OH, 2° R₂CHOH, 3° R₃COH — the classification that decides oxidation products in strand (c iv).
  • Hydrogen bonding ⇒ high b.p. vs same-mass alkanes; small alcohols are water-soluble (–OH H-bonds with water; long chains defeat it).
  • Trivial names to recognise: isopropyl alcohol = propan-2-ol; wood spirit = methanol (toxic — “Methanol is toxic” (ms bank)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Diol drawing + solubility explanation bit twice: candidates could draw ethane-1,2-diol but “failed to explain why the molecule is soluble in water in terms of ‘intermolecular hydrogen bond’” (CP 2014 Q2) — structure AND the force’s name.
  • Say hydroxyl group for –OH as a functional group; don’t swap “alcohol” and “alkanol” (CP 2012 Paper 2 context).
⚠️ Safety line

Ethanol/methanol burn with nearly invisible flames and methanol is poisonous if ingested — microscale use, no open flames near spirit-rationed benches; “combustibility and toxicity” are named values-and-attitudes outcomes (syllabus).

XI (a)

Aldehydes — the carbonyl that can’t hide

$$\text{R–CHO}\qquad \text{suffix -al; the CHO carbon is always C-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Members: methanal HCHO, ethanal CH₃CHO, propanal CH₃CH₂CHO; write the group as –CHO (never –COH, which reads as an alcohol on carbon).
  • Terminal carbonyl ⇒ no locant (never “ethan-1-al”), and always one carbon in the count: propanal is C₃.
  • Terminal C–H on the carbonyl is why aldehydes oxidise easily to acids (dichromate orange → green, or purple → colourless permanganate) while ketones sit still (ms bank).
  • Trivial: formaldehyde = methanal; cinnamaldehyde (the cinnamon aldehyde of the 2012 bank solubility line) shows the –CHO group on a bigger skeleton (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Testing for carbonyl group is the same as testing for ketone” was a recorded misreading — 2,4-DNPH finds both carbonyls; to split them, oxidise with acidified dichromate (aldehyde only) (CP 2014 Paper 2 context; ms bank).
  • 2,4-DNPH observation: “yellow / orange / red precipitate … [Do not accept brown]” (ms bank) — precipitate, not colour-alone, and “brown” is worthless.
XI (a)

Ketones — carbonyl with a partner on both sides

$$\text{R–CO–R}'\qquad \text{suffix -one; propanone is the three-carbon minimum}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Members: propanone CH₃COCH₃ (no locant possible), butanone CH₃COCH₂CH₃, pentan-2-one / pentan-3-one — a ketone needs two carbons flanking the C=O, so C₃ is the floor.
  • Make-and-break: secondary alcohol oxidation gives ketones (c iv); ketone reduction gives 2° alcohols back (LiAlH₄ / NaBH₄, c v) (annex).
  • Resists acidified dichromate and permanganate — the clean chemical divider between the carbonyl siblings.
  • Trivial: acetone = propanone — listed in the syllabus’s own trivial-name examples.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Spelling is graded: “wrongly wrote ‘keytone’ instead of ‘ketone’” (CP 2012 Paper 2 context); the bank demands “condensation (†)”-style exactness on keyed terms.
  • For the 2016 pair acetophenone vs its reduction product, the winning reagent was again 2,4-DNPH (carbonyl yes/no) — “less than half of the candidates were able to suggest an appropriate chemical test” (CP 2016 Q13(e)).
XI (a)

Carboxylic acids — the -oic acid family

$$\text{R–COOH}\qquad \text{ethanoic acid }\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOH};\ \text{propanoic, butanoic, … octanoic}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The carboxyl group –COOH combines C=O and –OH on one carbon; the carbon is C-1, so no locant: butanoic acid, not butan-1-oic acid.
  • Typical-acid chemistry (Topic IV carry-over): weak acid, reacts with carbonates/metal/alkali; esterification and amide formation are its strand-(c) jobs.
  • Dicarboxylic variants appear in real exam molecules: ethanedioic acid (bank caution: “ethandioic acid”-style name errors (CP 2013 Q4)) and adipic (hexanedioic) acid, the nylon-6,6 diacid “HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH” (ms bank).
  • Trivial: acetic acid = ethanoic acid; benzoic acid ($\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{COOH}$) shows up via its salts in the bank’s ester-hydrolysis equation (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “$-\text{COOH}$” vs “$-\text{COO}$”: the acid carries its H; the ester/carboxylate does not — IR-style logic in the bank separates them (“does not react with $\text{NaHCO}_{3}\text{(aq)}$ (no -COOH)” (ms bank)).
  • Vinegar register: labelling ethanoic acid’s formula $\text{CH}_{3}\text{CO}_{2}\text{H}$ is fine; writing $\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOH}$ without the second O loses structure marks nowhere — keep one consistent style per answer.
XI (a)

Esters — two fragments, name them in the right order

$$\text{R–COO–R}'\qquad \text{CH}_{3}\text{COOC}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{ = ethyl ethanoate}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Name = alkyl (from the alcohol) + alkanoate (from the acid): ethyl ethanoate comes from ethanol + ethanoic acid; the acid-side carbon carries the C=O.
  • Read condensed formulae from the carbonyl side: $\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOC}_{2}\text{H}_{5}$ (acid CH₃COO–, alkyl –C₂H₅) is not the same drawing as $\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{COOCH}_{3}$ (methyl propanoate) — same-atom swaps are the examiner’s favourite trap.
  • Fresh esters smell sweet: “$\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOH}/\text{H}^{+}/\text{heat}$: fruit smell / pleasant odour substance formed” (ms bank) — a standard identification observation.
  • Esters of the syllabus’s own examples: methyl benzoate $\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{COOCH}_{3}$ (bank equation) and flavour esters made in microscale practicals (“preparing esters from different alkanoic acids and alcohols using microscale apparatus”, suggested activity).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • In cyclic molecules the ester group is read last and missed most: candidates “were not able to recognise the presence of an ester group and wrongly pointed out that the compound contains a ketone group” (CP 2012 Q28) — look for O inserted in the chain, not just =O hanging off it.
  • Hydrolysis direction matters for products: alkaline gives carboxylate + alcohol, acidic gives acid + alcohol (annex) — mix them up and the whole line is wrong.
XI (a)

Amides & primary amines — the nitrogen families

$$\text{R–CONH}_{2}\ \text{(unsubstituted amide)}\qquad \text{R–NH}_{2}\ \text{(primary amine)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Amide names: root + -amide — methanamide HCONH₂, ethanamide CH₃CONH₂, propanamide; the C=O carbon is part of the chain count.
  • Only unsubstituted amides (both N–H intact) are examinable from acids: “Amide formation from carboxylic acids is confined to unsubstituted amide” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (c)); N-substituted amides appear only as the annex’s hydrolysis chart rows.
  • Primary amines: root + -amine — methylamine, ethylamine, phenylamine aniline is trivial recognition only; the N carries two H for primary.
  • Keep amide ≠ amine: $\text{CONH}_{2}$ (carbonyl attached) vs $\text{NH}_{2}$ (bare) — IR distinguishes them too: N–H absorbs “3350-3500 cm⁻¹” (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The classic amide question is alkaline hydrolysis: “the alkaline hydrolysis of ethanamide would give ammonia” — under reflux the gas escapes and no equilibrium is reached (CP 2013 Q32); warm the solution and test the gas with damp red litmus.
  • Amide bonds also define the condensation polymers: “functional groups at the two ends of the monomers react repeatedly to form amide bonds / Small molecules are eliminated” (ms bank) — nylon is a polyamide.

Find the Family from the Formula — the four-question route

Given any condensed formula within the topic, run the questions in order. The first “yes” ends the search.

Q1 · What atoms are present?

Only C and H ⇒ hydrocarbon: all single bonds = alkane ($\text{C}_{n}\text{H}_{2n+2}$); one $\text{C}=\text{C}$ = alkene ($\text{C}_{n}\text{H}_{2n}$). Halogen present (no O, no N) ⇒ haloalkane. One N with no C=O beside it ⇒ primary amine $-\text{NH}_{2}$.

Q2 · Oxy-compound? Find the C=O.

No oxygen at all ends this branch. With O: is there a carbonyl C=O? Terminal C=O with an H (–CHO) = aldehyde; internal C=O flanked by carbons (–CO–) = ketone; no C=O but a lone –OH = alcohol.

Q3 · Both oxygens on the same carbon?

$-\text{COOH}$ (C=O and –OH on one carbon, at a chain end) = carboxylic acid. $-\text{COO}-$ with the second O continuing into another carbon chain = ester — read which side keeps the C=O: that side is the acid fragment ($\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOC}_{2}\text{H}_{5}$: ethanoate ← ethanoic; ethyl ← ethanol).

Q4 · O and N on the same carbon?

$\text{C}=\text{O}$ directly bonded to $\text{–NH}_{2}$ = unsubstituted amide ($-\text{CONH}_{2}$). Separate them: C=O missing, N present = amine; N on a carbonyl of a polymer repeat unit = a polyamide (nylon, Kevlar — strand (e)).

Multi-blank Quiz — Families, Formulae & Names

1. A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula; successive members differ by a CH₂ unit and show a gradation in physical properties.
2. Give the general formulae: alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, alkenes CₙH₂ₙ, alcohols CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH.
3. CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂ is but-1-ene (locant needed from four carbons upward), while CH₃CH=CHCH₃ is but-2-ene — which additionally shows cis-trans isomerism.
4. Name these: CH₃CHBrCH₃ is 2-bromopropane; (CH₃)₂CHOH is propan-2-ol (trivially, isopropyl alcohol).
5. The NTF’s own mixed-function examples: 3,3-dichloropropene has two Cl on C-3, and 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol puts –OH on C-1.
6. Systematic names for multiple same-type groups are expected — glycerol is propane-1,2,3-triol; ranking different groups by “order of priority” is, however, not expected (NTF XI (a)).
7. CH₃CH₂CHO is propanal (aldehyde group –CHO) and CH₃COCH₃ is propanone (trivial name acetone) — both C₃H₆O, functional-group isomers.
8. CH₃COOC₂H₅ is ethyl ethanoate: the alkyl part comes from the alcohol (ethanol) and the -oate part from the acid (ethanoic acid).
9. Trivial names on the syllabus list: formaldehyde = methanal, chloroform = trichloromethane (CHCl₃), acetic acid = ethanoic acid.
10. HCONH₂ is methanamide and CH₃CH₂NH₂ is ethylamine (ethanamine) — the amide carries a C=O next to the N, the amine does not.
11. Only unsubstituted amides are examinable from carboxylic acids; amide names end in -amide, and the group written on the page is –CONH₂.
12. Boiling-point logic bank-style: butan-1-ol (Mr 74) boils far above butane (Mr 58) because –OH allows hydrogen bonding, while within one family the rise with chain length is due to stronger van der Waals’ forces from the larger molecular size.
13. The complete combustion of pentane: C₅H₁₂ + 8 O₂ → 5 CO₂ + 6 H₂O — every organic equation answer should show the state symbols too (CP 2014 Q6).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Families & Naming

propane vs prop-1-ene vs propanal vs propanoic

Four different molecules, one root. The suffix decides the family: -ane saturated, -ene carries C=C, -al is –CHO, -oic acid is –COOH. CP printed candidates who could not even name propenoic acid (CP 2016 Q5) — read suffixes aloud until they bite.

Suffix vs prefix: -ol vs hydroxy-, halo- vs -halide

The chain’s principal taught group sits as a suffix (propan-2-ol); halogens and extra groups ride as prefixes (2-chloropropane, 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol). Halogen “substituent” never becomes a suffix — no “propan-2-ol bromide”.

Ethyl ethanoate vs methyl propanoate

Same atoms (C₄H₈O₂), different ester. $\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOC}_{2}\text{H}_{5}$: the C=O side (CH₃COO) is the ethanoate, the lone C₂H₅ is the ethyl. Flip it — $\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{COOCH}_{3}$ — and it is methyl propanoate. Name-reading follows the carbonyl, not the alphabet.

Alcohol vs alkanol (and carbonyl vs aldehyde)

“Alcohol” is the family/compound class, “alkanol” the specific homologues; hydroxyl names the –OH group. CP recorded these pairs being swapped at random — “‘hydroxy group’, ‘alcohol’ and ‘alkanol’” / “‘carbonyl group’, ‘aldehyde’ and ‘alkanal’” used interchangeably (CP 2012 Paper 2 context).

Locant zero and locant one

Methanal, propanone and propanal need no locant — there is nowhere else the group can go; writing “propan-1-al” or “1-propanone” invites a wrong-name mark. But but-2-ene must carry its 2, and pentan-2-pentan-3 pairs differ. The rule: locants only where position is possible.

Amide vs amine vs ammonia smell

–CONH₂ (amide, neutral, from acid + NH₃ logic) vs –NH₂ (primary amine, basic). Alkaline amide hydrolysis releases ammonia gas (litmus blue) (CP 2013 Q32); amines are the ammonia-like bases. Three nitrogen species, three different marks.

Molecular size vs branching in b.p. talk

Same family, longer chain ⇒ higher b.p. (stronger van der Waals’); same formula, more branching ⇒ lower b.p. (smaller contact surface) — but never say “more branches break hydrogen bonds” for alkanes: there are no H-bonds to break, only van der Waals’ forces, as the bank’s comparison grammar demands (ms bank).

XI (b)

Isomerism — the definition and the census plan

$$\text{structural isomers: same molecular formula,\ \underline{different structural formulae}}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition wording: isomerism occurs “when two or more compounds have the same molecular formula but different structures” (syllabus (b)).
  • Three search moves for structural isomers: (1) shorten the carbon chain, (2) slide the functional group along it, (3) change the family (same atoms, different group — aldehyde↔ketone, acid↔ester).
  • Then the stereo layer: does a drawn C=C allow cis-trans? Does a carbon carry four different groups? Only these two, per strands (b)’s exemplars.
  • Always verify each candidate with a real formula count — a proposed isomer whose H-count drifts is not an isomer (CP 2012 Q28).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Answer structure earns: list the names, then draw one displayed formula each; CP punished drawings “with a smaller/larger number of carbon atoms in comparing with the correct one” (CP 2013 Q15).
  • Explaining anything with “isomers” or “isotopes” when the question wants structure was recorded as irrelevant (CP 2013 Paper 2 context).
XI (b)

C₄H₁₀ — the two-chain starter

$$\text{butane }\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH}_{2}\text{CH}_{2}\text{CH}_{3}\qquad \text{2-methylpropane }(\text{CH}_{3})_{3}\text{CH}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Exactly 2 structural isomers. Method: four in a row → three in a row plus a methyl on the middle carbon only (a terminal “methyl” is just the straight chain again).
  • Common name to recognise: 2-methylpropane = isobutane; its two carbons types (9 equivalent H on the three CH₃) preview substitution-product counts.
  • Same trick generates the C₄H₉X family (haloalkanes): halogen on either skeleton, lowest-locant positions only.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never “find” a third isomer by rotating or flipping a drawing — a bent-looking chain that still reads C–C–C–C is butane; rotations are not isomers, a standing CP theme behind over-counting (iso engine trap set).
XI (b)

C₅H₁₂ — three chains, counted systematically

$$\text{pentane}\quad \text{2-methylbutane}\quad \text{2,2-dimethylpropane}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Exactly 3. Run the ladder: 5-carbon chain → 4 + methyl (positions 2 only, since position “3” is 2 seen from the other end) → 3 + two methyls, both on the centre carbon (2,2).
  • Reading names is the same skill in reverse: “2,2-dimethylpropane” says three-carbon chain, both branches on C-2 — draw it and count back to C₅H₁₂.
  • Boiling-point side-note for property questions: more compact (2,2) ⇒ smaller contact area ⇒ lowest b.p. of the three — van der Waals’ wording only.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “2-ethylpropane” does not exist — renumber and it is 2-methylbutane, a duplicate. Duplicates, not rotations, are where 5-isomer answers for C₅H₁₂ come from.
XI (b)

C₄H₉OH — the four butanols and their classes

$$\text{butan-1-ol,\ butan-2-ol,\ 2-methylpropan-1-ol,\ 2-methylpropan-2-ol}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Two skeletons × two −OH positions each = 4 structural isomers; classify: butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol are 1°; butan-2-ol is 2°; 2-methylpropan-2-ol is the syllabus’s example 3° alcohol (strand c iv payoff).
  • Branching and −OH location are independent slides — enumerate chain first, then group; never mix the two passes.
  • Link to the annex: 1° → aldehyde/acid on oxidation; 2° → ketone; 3° → no oxidation — the four drawings predict four different reaction answers.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “butan-1-ol” not “1-butanol” style-mix within one answer is sloppy but readable; what is worth zero is an alcohol named on the wrong skeleton (2-ethylpropan-1-ol = pentan-1-ol). Re-derive every name from your drawing.
XI (b)

C₄H₈ — three alkenes, four when geometry counts

$$\text{but-1-ene\quad cis-but-2-ene\quad trans-but-2-ene\quad 2-methylpropene}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Acyclic alkenes C₄H₈: 3 structural (but-1-ene, but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene); but-2-ene then splits into cis + trans → “draw all isomers” = 4 once the stem names stereoisomerism, 3 when it says structural only — read which is asked.
  • But-1-ene cannot show cis-trans: C-1 carries two identical H; 2-methylpropene: one carbon carries two CH₃ — the test is per-carbon, not per-molecule.
  • These C₄H₈ counts feed naming: add HBr to the set and Markovnikov decides major products (Engine C).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Cyclic isomers (cyclobutane/methylcyclopropane) are outside the taught series list — the strand says “acyclic carbon compounds containing one C=C bond” (syllabus (b)); do not gift yourself extra species the chart never teaches.
  • “Some wrongly mentioned that the alkenes are positional isomers” when the pair was chain-vs-position (CP 2013 Q15) — name the exact relationship (chain / position / functional group).

C₃H₆O — the aldehyde/ketone fork

$$\text{propanal }\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH}_{2}\text{CHO}\qquad \text{propanone }\text{CH}_{3}\text{COCH}_{3}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The syllabus’s “isomers containing different functional groups” in its purest exam form: same C₃H₆O, one –CHO, one >C=O — 2 isomers.
  • Their divider is chemistry: propanal oxidises on with acidified dichromate (orange → green) to propanoic acid; propanone does not — yet both give the 2,4-DNPH yellow/orange/red precipitate (ms bank).
  • Extension for C₄H₈O: butanal / 2-methylpropanal / butanone = 3 carbonyl isomers.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Don’t “discover” an –CH₂–O–CH₃ cyclic/ether-style isomer: ethers are not a taught family here, and CP’s ester/ketone confusion shows how recognition slips happen on ring compounds (CP 2012 Q28) — stay inside the ten families of strand (a).
XI (b)

C₃H₆O₂ — acid versus the two esters

$$\text{propanoic acid}\quad \text{methyl ethanoate}\quad \text{ethyl methanoate}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • 3 isomers. The acid CH₃CH₂COOH, then split the ester around –COO–: 2+1 (acid side CH₃CO–, alcohol side –CH₃) gives methyl ethanoate $\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOCH}_{3}$; 1+2 (acid side HCO–, alcohol side –C₂H₅) gives ethyl methanoate $\text{HCOOC}_{2}\text{H}_{5}$ — enumerate by carbon count on each side, never by eyeball.
  • Verify by counting atoms each time: propanoic acid C₃H₆O₂ ✓; methyl ethanoate CH₃COOCH₃ → 3 C, 6 H, 2 O ✓; ethyl methanoate HCOOC₂H₅ ✓.
  • Property divider: only the acid fizzes with $\text{NaHCO}_{3}\text{(aq)}$ and the bank’s IR logic: “does not react with $\text{NaHCO}_{3}\text{(aq)}$ (no -COOH)” (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Ester enumeration is where “missing isomers” marks die: fix the acid-fragment length (1, then 2…) and the alcohol fragment must absorb the rest — every pair checked, nothing repeated.
XI (b)

cis-trans isomerism — restricted rotation, per-carbon test

$$\text{cis-but-2-ene: two CH}_{3}\text{ same side}\qquad \text{trans: opposite sides}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Origin sentence: cis-trans isomerism results from “restricted rotation about a C=C bond” in acyclic compounds with one double bond (syllabus (b)) — the groups cannot swap sides without breaking the π bond.
  • Eligibility test, carbon by carbon: each C of the C=C must carry two different groups. but-2-ene ✓ (H/CH₃ on both); but-1-ene ✗ (CH₂= end: two H); 2-methylpropene ✗ (=C(CH₃)₂ end).
  • Draw the pairs honestly: keep the chain lengths identical in cis and trans — CP’s wrong drawings changed the molecule (CP 2013 Q15).
  • Counting geometry across several double bonds (2015 Q26): each eligible C=C gives a 2-way choice, then merge symmetric duplicates — CH₃CH=CH–CH=CHCH₃: cis-cis, trans-trans, cis-trans (= trans-cis) ⇒ 3 (CP 2015 Q26).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Relating cis/trans structures to properties uses Topic VI force language (polarity, packing) — but the specific-butenedioic-acid property essay is “not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (b)); count and draw, don’t lecture.

Enantiomerism — one chiral carbon, recognition depth only

$$\text{chiral carbon: C bonded to FOUR DIFFERENT atoms / groups}\ \ (\text{e.g. butan-2-ol})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Recognise enantiomerism “in compounds with only one chiral carbon” (syllabus (b)): butan-2-ol C carries –H, –OH, –CH₃, –C₂H₅; the teaching models are butan-2-ol and 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid) — build them with kits, show non-superimposable mirror images.
  • The single physical fact: enantiomers “rotate the plane of plane-polarised light by the same extent, but in opposite directions” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (b)); bank wording “rotate plane-polarised light to opposite directions to the same degree / extent” (ms bank).
  • Everything else about them is identical — same b.p., same m.p., same reactions with achiral reagents; claiming property differences is the recorded wrong answer (CP 2016 Q13(d)).
  • Scope: do not assign R/S names, do not discuss polarimeters or racemic mixtures — “details of polarimetry and concepts related to racemic mixture are not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (b)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Definition wording is graded word-by-word: “chiral carbon atom which connected to four different molecules” loses — the groups are atoms/groups, not whole molecules (CP 2015 Q13); “superimposable” must be spelled and meant (CP 2015 Q13).
  • Screen candidate “chiral” carbons by listing all four attachments: 2-chloropentane is genuinely chiral (–H, –Cl, –CH₃, –C₃H₇) while 3-chloropentane is not (two ethyl groups) — only four different attachments qualify (CP 2013 Q15(d) logic).

The Isomer Tree — one formula, four branches, then the stereo layer

Root = molecular formula. Grow structural isomers first (each branch is a different family of drawings), then ask the stereo questions about each finished structure. A list is only finished when every branch is empty.

Branch 1 · Chain

Longest chain down: $n \rightarrow n-1$ + methyl (middle positions only) $\rightarrow n-2$ + two methyls. For C₅H₁₂: 5 → 4+1 (2-methylbutane) → 3+2 (2,2-dimethylpropane). Renumber every candidate — “2-ethylpropane” is pentane in disguise.

Branch 2 · Position

On each skeleton, slide the group: butan-1-ol / butan-2-ol; pentan-2-one / pentan-3-one. Stop at the mirror of the start — positions read the same from the other end.

Branch 3 · Different functional group

Spend the atoms differently: C₃H₆O → aldehyde or ketone; C₃H₆O₂ → acid or ester; C₄H₈O₃ could even be a hydroxy-aldehyde — but only combos of taught families count.

Branch 4 · Stereo layer (applied last)

For each finished drawing: (i) any C=C with two different groups on both carbons? → add the cis/trans twin; (ii) any carbon with four different groups? → mirror-image pair exists (recognise; no R/S).

Multi-blank Quiz — Isomerism

1. Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures; structural isomers differ in the arrangement (displayed formula) of their atoms.
2. C₄H₁₀ has 2 structural isomers: butane and 2-methylpropane; C₅H₁₂ has 3.
3. The four alcohols of formula C₄H₉OH are butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol; the last is a tertiary (3°) alcohol.
4. cis-trans isomerism comes from restricted rotation about the C=C bond; eligibility requires each double-bond carbon to carry two different groups — so but-2-ene shows it, but but-1-ene (or 2-methylpropene) does not.
5. All acyclic C₄H₈ alkene drawings: but-1-ene, 2-methylpropene, and the two geometric forms of but-2-ene — cis and trans, 4 structures in all (structural only: 3).
6. Propanal and propanone are functional-group isomers of formula C₃H₆O; the aldehyde carries the –CHO group, the ketone a C=O flanked by two carbon (alkyl) groups.
7. Three C₃H₆O₂ isomers: propanoic acid, methyl ethanoate (CH₃COOCH₃) and ethyl methanoate (HCOOC₂H₅); only the acid reacts with NaHCO₃(aq) (ms bank).
8. A chiral carbon is bonded to four different atoms or groups — in butan-2-ol they are –H, –OH, –CH₃ and –C₂H₅ (ethyl); the pair of mirror-image forms are called enantiomers (optical isomers) (CP 2015 Q13 wording rule).
9. Enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light by the same extent but in opposite directions; their melting and boiling points are the same (NTF XI (b); CP 2016 Q13(d)).
10. The symmetrical diene CH₃CH=CH–CH=CHCH₃ has 3 geometrical isomers, not 4, because cis-trans and trans-cis are the same geometry (CP 2015 Q26).
11. A compound with two –COOH (carboxyl) groups and formula C₄H₄O₄ has 3 isomers: the cis and trans C=C-acids and CH₂=C(COOH)₂ — sixty percent missed one (CP 2016 Q29).
12. Out of scope while counting: polarimetry details, racemic mixture concepts, and property essays about specific cis-trans pairs such as butenedioic acid (NTF-2018 II-B XI (b)).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Isomerism

Rotation vs isomer

A chain drawn bent, upside-down or backwards is the same compound. Only a different connectivity (or a locked geometry) is a different isomer — over-counting by rotating drawings is the most common enumeration disease.

“2-ethylpropane” = pentane disguise

Renumber any chain you invent: find the longest chain first, then name. If the longest chain is 5, “ethylpropane” is illegal — a duplicate dressed as a new isomer.

Positional vs chain vs functional-group isomers

but-1-ene/but-2-ene differ in position; but-2-ene/2-methylpropene differ in chain; propanal/propanone differ in functional group. Naming the relationship exactly was worth marks and was got wrong in 2013 and 2016 (CP 2013 Q15; CP 2016 Q13(c)).

Geometrical ≠ optical

Geometrical (cis-trans) is locked-around-a-double-bond; optical is mirror-image-around-a-chiral-carbon. Offering “geometrical isomers” for a chiral pair (or vice versa) reads as guessing — the 2016 report lists exactly that spray of labels (CP 2016 Q13(c)).

Same formula ≠ same chemistry

Propanal and propanone share C₃H₆O yet split on oxidation (aldehyde → acid with acidified dichromate; ketone unreactive) — isomer questions love to chain a count into a test.

“Both are chiral” claims

Before claiming chirality, list the four attachments; two matching groups (or a CH₂) kills it. Candidates “wrongly mentioned that compound Y also has a chiral carbon” (CP 2012 Q14(a)), and giving 3-chloropentane (two ethyl groups on the C–Cl — not optically active) was printed (CP 2013 Q15(d)).

Cis/trans “different properties” vs “different b.p./m.p.” for enantiomers

cis/trans isomers genuinely differ in polarity and packing (different b.p.s possible); enantiomers do not differ in any achiral physical property — only rotation direction. Swapping these two sentences is a marked contradiction (CP 2016 Q13(d); ms bank).

2 · Cracking ↔ addition polymerisation

$$\text{C}_{8}\text{H}_{18}\rightarrow \text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{6}+2\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH=CH}_{2}\qquad n\text{CH}_{2}\text{=CH}_{2}\rightarrow [\text{-CH}_{2}\text{-CH}_{2}\text{-}]_{n}$$
🎯 Learning Goals

  • Cracking is a chemical change, fractional distillation is physical: “A few candidates were confused about catalytic cracking and fractional distillation, and wrongly stated that this reaction can separate useful fractions from petroleum” (CP 2016 Q3).

3 · Alkane + halogen — photochemical substitution

4 · Alkene + halogen — addition and the C=C test

$$\text{CH}_{2}\text{=CH}_{2}+\text{Br}_{2}\rightarrow \text{BrCH}_{2}\text{CH}_{2}\text{Br}\ \ (\text{1,2-dibromoethane})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Reagent: “X₂ (in organic solvent)” (annex) — bromine solution; conditions: room temperature, no light needed; observation: “Orange / brown $\text{Br}_{2}$ solution turns to colourless” (ms bank); type: addition.

5 · Alkene + hydrogen halide — Markovnikov’s rule

$$\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH=CH}_{2}+\text{HBr}\rightarrow \text{CH}_{3}\text{CHBrCH}_{3}\ (\text{2-bromopropane, major})$$
🎯 Learning Goals

Multi-blank Quiz — Reagents · Conditions · Products · Observations

1. Methane + chlorine react in the presence of UV light / sunlight by substitution, giving CH₃Cl + HCl; use excess methane to favour the mono-substituted product (CP 2015 Q6).
2. Ethene + Br₂ (in organic solvent): the reaction type is addition, the product is 1,2-dibromoethane, and the observation is the orange/brown solution turning colourless (ms bank).
3. Propene + HBr: by Markovnikov’s rule the H joins the double-bond carbon bearing the greater number of H atoms, so the major product is 2-bromopropane (CH₃CHBrCH₃).
4. Ethene + H₂ needs catalyst Pt, Ni or Pd (annex); industrial ethanol from ethene uses steam over a phosphoric catalyst — a Topic-V route, not an annex arrow.
5. Bromoethane → ethanol: reagent NaOH(aq) / OH⁻(aq), condition heat, type substitution (hydrolysis); comparing haloalkane reactivity is not required (annex).
6. Ethanol → bromoethane: use HBr (or PBr₃); balance: C₂H₅OH + HBr → C₂H₅Br + H₂O; shorthand HX/PX₃ is acceptable (NTF XI (c)).
7. Ethanol → ethene: heat with concentrated H₂SO₄ (or vapour over hot Al₂O₃) — the reaction type is elimination (dehydration).
8. To turn propan-1-ol into propanal, oxidise with acidified $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$ and distil immediately; to get propanoic acid instead, heat under reflux with excess oxidant; colour line: orange → green.
9. Propan-2-ol oxidises to propanone; 2-methylpropan-2-ol (a 3° alcohol) gives no reaction — the mixture stays orange.
10. Reduce ethanal to ethanol with either “1. LiAlH₄, dry ether 2. H⁺(aq)” or aqueous NaBH₄ (annex); the carbonyl test that is positive for both carbonyl families uses 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine giving a yellow/orange/red precipitate (ms bank).
11. Ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water: catalyst conc. H₂SO₄, condition heat, observation sweet / fruity smell (ms bank); dilute acid or no heating both cost marks (CP 2015 Q12; CP 2016 Q12).
12. Ethyl ethanoate + NaOH(aq), heat → sodium ethanoate (carboxylate) + ethanol; the same ester + H⁺(aq), heat gives ethanoic acid + ethanol — alkali can never return the free acid while basic (annex).
13. Ethanamide heated with NaOH(aq) releases ammonia (gas: alkaline to damp red litmus); with acid it yields ethanoic acid + an ammonium salt (annex; CP 2013 Q32).
14. Combustion balancing drill: 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O — halves are legal mid-work (6½ O₂ per butane) but the final equation uses whole numbers; schemes may stay unbalanced, ordinary equations may not (ms conventions).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Reactions & Conditions

Reflux vs distillation

Reflux = boil with the condenser vertical, vapour returns — everything stays hot together for long (amide/ester hydrolysis, full oxidation to acids). Distillation = condenser angled to a receiver — product escapes as it forms (stop at aldehyde). Swapping the drawings was a majority error (CP 2016 Q13(a)).

Sunlight for substitution vs no light for addition

Alkane + X₂ needs “UV light, light or heat” (annex); alkene + X₂ needs neither — it goes at room temperature, and light isn’t the point. Importing “sunlight” into an addition answer is a giveaway of memorised fragments, not understood charts.

Bromine solution vs “bromine liquid”

The test reagent is a bromine solution (annex: “X₂ (in organic solvent)”; accepted register “Orange / brown Br₂ solution” (ms bank)) — “bromine liquid or bromine” alone was rejected (CP 2014 Q3), and colour change must include the start colour (CP 2016 Q3).

Conc. H₂SO₄ vs dilute / aqueous

Esterification and dehydration want concentrated acid — it removes water; “$\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}\text{(aq)}$” (dilute, full of water) hydrolyses esters instead — the same molecule, opposite job, decided by the word concentrated (CP 2015 Q12; CP 2016 Q12).

Excess methane vs excess chlorine

Big excess of methane ⇒ mostly CH₃Cl; big excess of chlorine ⇒ the staircase runs to CH₂Cl₂/CHCl₃/CCl₄. “Failed to correctly state that excess amount of methane was needed” (CP 2015 Q6) — control the ratio, control the product.

LiAlH₄ (dry) vs NaBH₄ (aqueous)

Lithium aluminium hydride reacts violently with water/acid — its annex row is “1. LiAlH₄, dry ether 2. H⁺(aq)”; borohydride is used “(aq)” (annex; ms bank). Candidates wrongly stated LiAlH₄ used “in an acidic aqueous medium” (CP 2016 Q13(b)).

OH⁻(aq) on haloalkanes vs “elimination conditions”

The XI chart gives haloalkanes exactly one arrow: OH⁻(aq) → alcohol. Alkenes come from alcohols via Al₂O₃ / conc acid. Any “hot ethanolic KOH” answer is from another syllabus’s playbook and doesn’t exist in this annex.

Markovnikov for unsymmetrical alkenes only

Ethene and but-2-ene are symmetrical across the double bond: one product, nothing to predict. Dropping the rule’s name onto every addition reads as keyword-spamming; cite it only where two different additions compete.

XI (d)

Route-writing method — read the target, walk the chart

$$\text{compare C-skeleton \& group} \rightarrow \text{find the LAST arrow that makes the product} \rightarrow \text{write back-step by step}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Only reactions “described in (c)” build routes: “suggest routes to convert one functional group into another by using the reactions described in (c)”, stating reagents and conditions at every step.
  • Retrosynthesis ladder: ester ← acid + alcohol; acid ← 1° alcohol (reflux) or aldehyde; aldehyde ← 1° alcohol (distil); alcohol ← haloalkane ($\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$); haloalkane ← alkene (HX) or alkane ($\text{X}_{2}$/UV); alkene ← alcohol (dehydration).
  • Carbon count and the 1°/2° class are fixed at step one — you cannot change which alcohol-class a skeleton gives without changing the skeleton; the 2014 “non-existing alkene” answer failed exactly this check (CP 2014 Q12 context).
  • Sanity check against the annex before leaving the step: every reagent you write must be a chart arrow — no invented oxidations, no “$\text{LiAlH}_{4}$ then acidify to an aldehyde” (it goes to the alcohol).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Missing a whole step is the top failure: 2015 “omitted either the oxidation … or reduction … step”, and in the esterification step “gave the incorrect catalyst” (CP 2015 Q12).
  • Extra steps lose too: “including inappropriate steps” — extra dichromate oxidations and hydride reductions inserted where the route needed none (CP 2016 Q12) — the mark scheme pays step marks, not step counts.
XI (d)

Worked multi-step routes — three classics

$$\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH}_{2}\text{Br}\xrightarrow{\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq), heat}}\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH}_{2}\text{OH}\xrightarrow[\text{reflux}]{\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}/\text{H}^{+}}\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOH}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Route 1 (haloalkane → acid), 2 steps as printed above: substitution then full oxidation — conditions on both arrows.
  • Route 2 (alkene → ester), 4 steps: alkene $\xrightarrow{\text{HX}}$ haloalkane $\xrightarrow{\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}}$ alcohol $\xrightarrow[\text{reflux}]{\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}/\text{H}^{+}}$ acid $\xrightarrow[\text{heat}]{\text{R'OH, conc }\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}}$ ester — the last step needs the alcohol you choose.
  • Route 3 (amide → ester), 2 steps — the 2016 paper itself: amide $\xrightarrow{\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq), heat; then H}^{+}}$ carboxylic acid (hydrolysis; acidify to free the acid) then intramolecular/ordinary esterification with conc. acid catalyst (CP 2016 Q12).
  • Backwards planning example: propanone is made from propan-2-ol; propan-2-ol is made by Markovnikov hydration-substitution of propene — so propene → (HBr) 2-bromopropane → (OH⁻ aq) propan-2-ol → (Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺) propanone.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Format that scores: numbered arrows, reagent above, condition below, product formula after each arrow — “step marks for multi-step organic synthesis” (ms bank) reward exactly this layout.
XI (d)

Percentage yield — the (d) calculation outcome

$$\%\ \text{yield}=\frac{\text{mass actually obtained}}{\text{theoretical mass from the equation}}\times 100$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The chain: mass → moles ($n=m/M$) → equation mole ratio → moles of product → theoretical mass → % yield — “calculate the percentage yield of a product obtained from a reaction” is a listed (d) outcome.
  • Esterification yields sit below 100 % by design — the reaction is reversible; removing water (conc. $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$, dehydrating role (ms bank)) or excess of one reactant pushes it up.
  • Loss during purification (transfers, distillation cuts, drying) also caps the real number — exam answers name reversibility first, practical losses second.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Yield ≠ atom economy: “Many candidates were unable to differentiate between the terms ‘atom economy’ and ‘reaction yield’” (CP 2013 Paper 2 context) — yield is actual ÷ theoretical, atom economy is a property of the equation.
  • Keep the units: the bank hammers “the unit must be correct”; % needs the sign, masses need g.
XI (d)

Laboratory preparation Ⅰ · ethanoic acid (and benzoic by hydrolysis)

$$\text{CH}_{3}\text{CH}_{2}\text{OH}+2[\text{O}]\xrightarrow{\text{acidified }\text{K}_{2}\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}\text{, reflux}}\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOH}+\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mix ethanol with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) in a round-bottom flask, heat under reflux (vertical condenser; vapour condenses back — full oxidation proceeds); then distil the purified acid (b.p. 118 °C) from the mixture — two apparatus, two purposes.
  • Colour book-keeping: orange → green as dichromate is spent; the reflux keeps the aldehyde intermediate in the flask instead of letting it escape (its b.p. is only 21 °C).
  • Alternative (d)-route from the syllabus activities: benzoic acid by alkaline hydrolysis of ethyl benzoate (bank equation pattern $\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{5}\text{COOCH}_{3}+\text{NaOH}$ (ms bank)) then acidify to precipitate the acid.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Describe how to carry out” questions want anti-bumping granules, thermometric position if distillation follows, and the named apparatus parts — CP has marked down “wrong position of the thermometer and set-up of a closed system” in distillation drawings (CP 2012 Q12(c)); a reflux drawing must NOT be a distillation one (CP 2016 Q13(a)).
⚠️ Safety line

Ethanol vapour is highly flammable; dichromate is toxic and an oxidiser — gloves, microscale quantities, never heat to dryness.

XI (d)

Laboratory preparation Ⅱ · ethyl ethanoate — make, separate, dry, distil

$$\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOH}+\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{OH}\xrightleftharpoons[\text{}]{\text{conc. }\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}\text{, heat}}\text{CH}_{3}\text{COOC}_{2}\text{H}_{5}+\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Make: warm ethanoic acid + ethanol with a few cm³ of conc. $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ (catalyst + water remover); ester distils off as it forms (low b.p. 77 °C).
  • Separate: pour the distillate into a separating funnel with water/saturated $\text{Na}_{2}\text{CO}_{3}$ solution — the ester is the upper, non-aqueous layer; the aqueous wash removes acid/ethanol; run off the lower aqueous layer first: “Use separating funnel to remove water (the lower liquid layer)” (ms bank).
  • Dry: add anhydrous $\text{MgSO}_{4}$ (or $\text{CaCl}_{2}$) until powder flows freely — “anhydrous magnesium sulphate … the most suitable drying agent for ethyl ethanoate” (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3 context); purify: “Carry out fractional distillation on the upper layer” (ms bank) and collect the 77 °C cut.
  • Confirm: sweet/fruity smell; neutral pH; (d)-level yield = actual mass ÷ theoretical × 100.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The purification verbs are marks: separate → dry → distil; “did not mention the use of a separating funnel and the subsequent use of fractional distillation” (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3 context) — write the full chain, and open the funnel tap to vent when shaking with carbonate.
  • SBA reality: “not many schools carried out preparative experiments (in particularly those involving organic reactions)” — this four-verb chain is exactly what those reports say students had not seen (CP 2014/2015/2016 SBA comments).
XI (d)

Laboratory preparation Ⅲ · 2-chloro-2-methylpropane

$$(\text{CH}_{3})_{3}\text{COH}+\text{HCl(aq)}\rightarrow (\text{CH}_{3})_{3}\text{CCl}+\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The syllabus’s own haloalkane practical: shake 2-methylpropan-2-ol with concentrated HCl in a separating funnel; the tertiary alcohol reacts readily at room temperature (chart reagent HX, Card 7 of Engine C).
  • Two layers form — this chloroalkane is the upper layer (2-chloro-2-methylpropane, ρ ≈ 0.85 g cm⁻³, lighter than the aqueous acid; check by adding a drop of water and seeing which layer grows): run off the lower aqueous layer, wash the organic layer (water → carbonate with venting → water), dry over anhydrous $\text{MgSO}_{4}$, then distil/purify — same four verbs as the ester.
  • Yield arithmetic lands here: moles of alcohol (limiting reagent), theoretical mass of the chloroalkane, then % = actual ÷ theoretical × 100 on what you actually pour off.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • If a question asks how to prove the product is the haloalkane: hydrolyse a sample with $\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$, acidify with nitric acid, then acidified $\text{AgNO}_{3}$ — white precipitate = Cl present (Topic IV/V chain); writing tests “with comparison” is the bank’s mark pattern (ms bank).
⚠️ Safety line

Conc. HCl is corrosive and the chloroalkane is volatile — stopper the funnel, vent frequently away from flames, work in a ventilated bench.

XI (e)

Aspirin — acetylsalicylic acid at recognition depth

$$\text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{4}(\text{COOH})(\text{OCOCH}_{3})\ :\ \text{carboxylic acid + ester groups on one benzene ring}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Identify the functional groups of the molecule: a carboxyl group (–COOH) and an ester link (–OOCCH₃, written $\text{OCOCH}_{3}$ on the ring) — and note the ester points the O to the ring: it is a phenol-ester, so hydrolysis returns 2-hydroxybenzoic acid + ethanoic acid.
  • Medical uses verbatim: relieve pain, reduce inflammation and fever, and reduce the risk of heart attack (blood-thinning) (syllabus (e)).
  • Ester hydrolysis is the examinable chemistry inside the molecule: warming with aqueous alkali cleaves the –OOCCH₃ link (giving the sodium salts of the phenol and the acid, plus ethanoate); acidify and the products reappear as 2-hydroxybenzoic acid + ethanoic acid.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Calling the –OCOCH₃ a “ketone” is the ring-ester misread the reports love (CP 2012 Q28) — the giveaway is the extra single-bonded O bridging to the ring.
  • “Analysing aspirin tablets by back titration is not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B XI (e)) — aspirin questions stay on structure, groups, uses and simple hydrolysis.
XI (e)

Soaps & soapless detergents — head, tail, cleanse

$$\text{triglyceride}+3\text{NaOH}\rightarrow \text{glycerol}+3\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COO}^{-}\text{Na}^{+}\ \text{(saponification)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Soap = sodium/potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid, made by alkaline hydrolysis of a fat/oil (the bank equation above, its exact form; strand (d) ester hydrolysis in the laundry) (ms bank).
  • Structure sentence for marks: “The $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COO}^{-}$ ion has an ionic head ($\text{COO}^{-}$) and a hydrocarbon tail ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}$)” — head hydrophilic, tail hydrophobic; detergents can also be “made from chemicals derived from petroleum” (syllabus (e); ms bank).
  • Cleansing action: the hydrophobic tails burrow into grease, the ionic heads stay in the water — grease lifts as tiny emulsified droplets, and “The ionic heads of the grease droplets repel from each other”, so they won’t rejoin and the fabric rinses clean (ms bank).
  • Wetting: the detergent lowers water’s surface tension so it spreads and soaks into fibres instead of beading — compare soap vs soapless detergent cleansing ability, the syllabus’s own practical.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Head-and-tail must appear explicitly — “many candidates were not able to point out that a detergent molecule consists of an ionic ‘head’ and a hydrocarbon ‘tail’”, and both -philic/-phobic words were mis-spelled (CP 2012 Q14(b)).
  • Triglyceride structure is assumed: “a very high proportion of the candidates did not have any idea about the structure of a triglyceride” (CP 2014 Q14) — glycerol triester of three long-chain acids; learn to spot the three ester links.
XI (e)

Nylon-6,6 — a condensation polyamide, counted honestly

$$n\,\text{H}_{2}\text{N}(\text{CH}_{2})_{6}\text{NH}_{2}+n\,\text{HOOC}(\text{CH}_{2})_{4}\text{COOH}\rightarrow[\text{-NH}(\text{CH}_{2})_{6}\text{NHCO}(\text{CH}_{2})_{4}\text{CO}-]_{n}+2n\,\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Monomers: 1,6-diaminohexane (six CH₂ between the N’s) + hexanedioic (adipic) acid — “HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH (adipic acid monomer)” (ms bank); both bifunctional: “Monoacenes should be bifunctional … functional groups at the two ends of the monomers react repeatedly to form amide bonds” (ms bank).
  • Type: condensation polymerisation — “Small molecules are eliminated” ($\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$) (ms bank); the repeat unit holds one diamine + one diacid residue joined by two amide links.
  • Counting: six CH₂ between the two N atoms, four CH₂ between the two C=O groups — the 2016 MC’s exact distinction (CP 2016 Q31).
  • Uses & structure–property: strong fibres for ropes, fabrics, bristles (statement (1) of that MC); inter-chain hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O explain strength; Kevlar is stronger “because the benzene groups in the Kevlar molecules have a more rigid structure” (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The formation of nylon is never “a hydrolysis process” — that claim was the rejected option of 2016 Q31 (CP 2016 Q31); hydrolysis is what amide links undergo (Engine C card 13), not what builds them.
  • Deducing monomers from a repeat unit: cut at each amide link; C=O side → diacid, N side → diamine — “Many candidates were not able to give the correct structural formulae of the monomers” (CP 2012 Paper 2 context).
XI (e)

Polyesters — Terylene/PET, PBT, and why PLA biodegrades

$$n\,\text{HOCH}_{2}\text{CH}_{2}\text{OH}+n\,\text{HOOC}\text{-C}_{6}\text{H}_{4}\text{-COOH}\rightarrow[\text{-OCH}_{2}\text{CH}_{2}\text{OOC}\text{-C}_{6}\text{H}_{4}\text{-CO}-]_{n}+2n\,\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Diol + dicarboxylic acid (e.g. benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid + ethane-1,2-diol) → polyester + water: condensation again; ester links hold the chain.
  • Monomer deduction from the polymer runs through the ester link: –O–CH₂… from the diol, –CO– from the acid — the PBT 2016 item asked exactly this: “not able to … draw the structures of the two monomers of PBT” (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q2(c)).
  • Uses: fibres (clothing, ropes), bottles, films; properties: strong, flexible thermoplastics — “becomes soft when heated, becomes hard when cooled” (ms bank).
  • Biodegradability from link chemistry: “There are many ester groups in PLA. These ester groups can be broken down in acidic / alkaline / bacterial conditions” (ms bank) — polyester chains can be hydrolysed; addition polymers (C–C backbone) can’t, hence persistence.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Type discipline per polymer: styrene (vinyl C=C) → addition — calling it “condensation polymerisation” was the common wrong answer (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q2 context); PBT/diol-diacid → condensation — the reverse swap was 2016’s error (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q2(c)). Read the linkage (ester/amide + small molecule?) before naming.
  • Nylon ≠ polyester links: amide vs ester — the hydrolysis products differ (amine/ammonium vs alcohol) and that is the (c)(e) bridge question.

Real-Life & Green Map — where the cycle meets the world

Strands (d)/(e) are assessed as living chemistry: fuels, drinks, medicines, fibres. Each panel is a link the syllabus itself prints.

Ethanol’s two faces (V ↔ XI)

Fermentation of sugars (yeast, ~30–40 °C, anaerobic): renewable, slower, dilute product needing distillation. Hydration of ethene (steam, acid catalyst, high T/P from crude): fast, continuous, pure. Green argument per CP: fermentation uses biomass energy rather than depleting crude — and 2015’s candidates failed to say exactly why it is “a green process” (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q1 context).

Choosing a synthetic route (syllabus STSE)

“The best synthetic route may not be the one with the fewest steps or the lowest cost” — weigh safety, waste, energy and hazard of reagents (syllabus STSE). Bank tool: atom economy vs yield (CP 2013 Paper 2); low-yield steps × many steps = waste.

Everyday functional-group spotter

Nail-polish remover = propanone (ketone). Vinegar = ethanoic acid. Whistle/flavour esters = ethyl ethanoate & friends. Antiseptic “methylated spirits” = ethanol + toxic methanol — “Methanol is toxic”, and 2012 candidates wrongly sold methanol as a wine/food ingredient (CP 2012 Paper 2 context). Breathalyser = orange→green dichromate chemistry (Engine C card 10).

Materials ladder (polymers in life)

Addition plastics from cracked alkenes: PE/PP/PVC/PMMA — “PMMA is not a thermosetting plastic because its molecules are held by weak van der Waals’ forces” (ms bank). Condensation fibres: nylon (amide), Terylene (ester), Kevlar (rigid aromatic amide, stronger “because the benzene groups … have a more rigid structure” (ms bank)). Biodegradable: PLA, PHB — hydrolysable ester links.

Multi-blank Quiz — Conversions & Substances

1. Bromoethane → ethanoic acid in two steps: first OH⁻(aq), heat (substitution) to ethanol, then excess acidified Cr₂O₇²⁻, heat under reflux — distilling instead would stop at the aldehyde.
2. Propene → propan-1-ol? The chart path: propene + HBr gives mainly 2-bromopropane (Markovnikov), so to place Br on C-1 you must avoid HX addition — the chart offers no direct C-1 route from propene; the taught move is plan backwards from the real target instead.
3. The 2016 route asked: amide → carboxylic acid by hydrolysis (H⁺(aq), heat or OH⁻ then H⁺), then the acid + its own –OH chain → cyclic ester (intramolecular esterification); using dilute H₂SO₄ for the ester step was the recorded slip (CP 2016 Q12).
4. Ethyl ethanoate purification: separating funnel to remove the lower aqueous layer → dry with anhydrous MgSO₄ → purify the upper layer by fractional distillation (ms bank; CP 2014).
5. % yield chain: mass → moles (÷ Mr) → mole ratio from the balanced equation → theoretical mass → (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100; reversible esterifications rarely exceed 100 % — usually far below.
6. Aspirin’s two oxygen-groups: carboxylic acid and ester; its uses: relieve pain, reduce inflammation, fever and the risk of heart attack; back-titration analysis of tablets is not expected (NTF XI (e)).
7. A soap ion’s two halves: the ionic (hydrophilic) COO⁻ head and the hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail (C₁₇H₃₃); cleaning works by emulsifying grease into droplets whose heads repel each other (ms bank; CP 2012 Q14(b)).
8. Soap is made from a fat/oil + NaOH(aq), heat (saponification); the co-product is glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol); soapless detergents come instead from petroleum-derived chemicals (ms bank; syllabus (e)).
9. Nylon-6,6 is built from hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diaminohexane; between the two C=O bonds of the repeat unit sit four CH₂ groups, and between the N’s six (CP 2016 Q31).
10. Condensation polymerisation always eliminates a small molecule (H₂O); addition polymerisation eliminates nothing — all atoms are kept; styrene polymerises by addition (its vinyl C=C only — never the ring) (CP 2015 Paper 2 context).
11. PLA biodegrades because “there are many ester groups” that break down in acidic, alkaline or bacterial conditions (ms bank); polyethene persists because its C–C backbone has no hydrolysable link.
12. To tell nylon-6,6 from Terylene chemically: hydrolyse both and test — nylon gives a diamine (as its ammonium salt in acid) plus the dicarboxylate / diacid, Terylene gives a diol + dicarboxylic acid, no nitrogen; repeat-unit language: amide link vs ester link (Engine C cards 12–13).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Conversions & Substances

Reflux to make · distil to catch

Reflux = reaction conditions (everything hot, nothing lost): full oxidation, hydrolyses. Distil = isolate a volatile product: aldehyde mid-oxidation, the ester from its mixture. One drawing swapped for the other and the experiment described is the wrong one (CP 2016 Q13(a)).

Ester link vs ketone in a ring

C=O with a second single-bonded O hanging off the same carbon = ester (or acid with –OH). The extra O is the whole diagnosis — missing it turned a cyclic ester into a “ketone” for most of a cohort (CP 2012 Q28).

Addition vs condensation polymerisation

Addition: one alkene monomer type, nothing lost, C–C backbone. Condensation: two bifunctional monomers, small molecule out, heteroatom link (ester/amide) in the chain. Decide from the monomers; the 2015/2016 papers split candidates exactly on this swap (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q2; CP 2016 Paper 2 Q2(c)).

Yield vs atom economy vs purity

Yield = actual ÷ theoretical ×100 (this batch). Atom economy = % of atoms ending in product (the equation, 100 %-complete ideal). Purity = how clean the sample is. Three %’s, three questions — mixing them was a named confusion (CP 2013 Paper 2 context).

“Hydrolysis” as a universal excuse

NaOH(aq) was proposed for a reaction that wasn’t ester hydrolysis “possibly mistaking the reaction would involve the hydrolysis of ester” (CP 2013 Q14 context) — and in the 2014 reaction-type item “addition, hydrolysis, saponification and esterification” were sprayed as guesses (CP 2014 Q14). Match the reagent to the bond being made or broken.

Soap vs detergent — where they overlap, where they split

Both clean by head-tail emulsifying; but soap is a natural fatty-acid salt (biodegradable, works with soft water; textbook note: forms insoluble scum with hard-water Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺), soapless detergents are petroleum-made sulphonate-type salts that keep working in hard water. The syllabus asks structure ↔ cleansing action — keep the water-hardness line as an applied comparison, not a chart arrow.

The ring on the page vs the bond in the reaction

Aspirin, styrene, Kevlar, terephthalate all print benzene rings — that does not license ring addition reactions. Bromine addition “occurs to the C=C bonds” (vinyl ones) (ms bank); the ring’s stability is not to be argued (“stability of benzene NOT expected”); styrene’s polymer is poly(styrene) through the side C=C.

6 · Master Trap Checklist (cross-cutting, 20)▾
☐ “Acidified” + “heat” ride with every dichromate/permanganate line — orange → green; purple → colourless.
☐ 1° alcohol: distil stops at the aldehyde, reflux runs to the acid; apparatus name = product name.
☐ Concentrated H₂SO₄ for esterification & dehydration; aqueous/dilute acid means hydrolysis instead.
☐ LiAlH₄ = dry ether then H⁺(aq); NaBH₄ = aqueous. Never “LiAlH₄(aq)”.
☐ Haloalkane → alcohol = OH⁻(aq) + heat; no Cl/Br/I reactivity ranking anywhere in XI.
☐ Markovnikov: H joins the double-bond carbon with more H; cite only for unsymmetrical alkenes; say “major product”.
☐ Bromine test: name a solution, state the decolourisation from orange/brown to colourless.
☐ Esters read alcohol-first: CH₃COOC₂H₅ = ethyl ethanoate; the C=O side is the acid fragment.
☐ Esterification and every condensation loses H₂O; addition polymerisation loses nothing.
☐ Repeat units: brackets + subscript n; nylon = 4 CH₂ between C=O’s, 6 between N’s.
☐ Isomer lists: renumber longest chain, delete duplicates-by-rotation, then count; name the exact relationship.
☐ cis-trans needs two different groups on each C of the C=C; symmetrical dienes merge duplicate geometries (count 3).
☐ Chiral carbon = four different atoms or groups; enantiomers differ in rotation direction only.
☐ Naming caps: ≤ 8 carbons, same-type multiples fine (propane-1,2,3-triol), group priority not expected.
☐ Balance ordinary equations; state symbols on combustion; schemes may run unbalanced — the rest may not.
☐ Yield ≠ atom economy ≠ purity; % needs “÷ theoretical × 100” shown with units.
☐ Purification verbs: separating funnel → anhydrous MgSO₄ → fractional distillation; say which layer you keep.
☐ Observations need comparison (positive AND negative tube) — one bare colour is not a test result.
☐ Benzene/phenyl rings never addition-polymerise and their stability is not to be argued — styrene polymerises at the vinyl C=C.
☐ Mechanisms stay out: reagents, conditions, products, observations — nothing about “how”. Amide formation: unsubstituted only.
7 · Famous Misconceptions — flip your answer before an examiner does▾

The left column paraphrases wrong-idea wordings the reports and the keyword bank record (quotations there are condensed student phrasings, not verbatim report text); the citations vouch for each correction. Sources: HKDSE Candidates’ Performance 2012–2025 (2018–2025 via HKEAA briefing slides), the mark-scheme keyword bank, and the Notes-for-Teachers depth rulings.

The wrong ideaExam-true correctionSource
“Isomers are the same compound drawn differently.”Same molecular formula, different arrangement of atoms — different substances with different names, b.p.s and test results.syllabus (b); CP 2013 Q15
“Chains can rotate freely like a snake — every twist is a new isomer.”Single-bond rotations give the same molecule; only C=C rotation is restricted (and that restriction is what cis-trans isomerism is).syllabus (b); isomer ledger
“A carbon with four bonds is chiral.”Four different atoms or groups — CH₂ and C(CH₃)₂ centres are never chiral.CP 2015 Q13; CP 2013 Q15(d)
“Optical isomers boil/melt at different temperatures.”Enantiomers share all achiral physical properties; they rotate plane-polarised light equally but oppositely.CP 2016 Q13(d); NTF XI (b)
“H always adds to the left carbon when HX meets an alkene.”H goes to the double-bond carbon already carrying more H (Markovnikov); halogen lands on the more substituted carbon → the major product.annex; ms bank rule
“Esters smell fruity, so smelling is a proper test — one colour word is enough.”Waft gently (aldehyde too); and observations score with comparison between tubes — positive and negative.ms bank; CP 2012 Q2
“K₂Cr₂O₇ oxidises any alcohol to an acid.”1° needs two controlled stages (distil vs reflux); 2° stops at the ketone; 3° does not react at all.annex rows A8–A9; CP 2012 Q12
“Water is a by-product so I may leave it out.”Esterification/amidation are condensations — the eliminated small molecule is part of the equation and of the type name.ms bank; CP 2013 Paper 2
“The benzene ring in styrene/Kevlar polymers is where polymerisation happens.”Addition happens at the vinyl C=C; rings don’t polymerise (and benzene stability is off-syllabus to discuss).ms bank; NTF depth notes
“NaOH(aq) fixes any organic synthesis step.”Aqueous OH⁻ substitutes haloalkanes (and hydrolyses esters/amides) — it is not a dehydrator or an oxidant; pick the arrow from the chart, not the bottle.annex; CP 2013 Q14 context
“Markovnikov’s rule is decoration — any addition product scores.”For an unsymmetrical alkene + HX the rule decides the answer: H joins the double-bond carbon carrying the greater number of H, the halogen lands on the more substituted carbon, and the MAJOR product is what is named and drawn — stating the rule itself was only a quarter-correct in 2021, and 2024 schemes still produced the minor product.annex (major product “required”); CP 2021 Q11(a); CP 2024 Q11(d)
“Detergents dissolve grease into the water.”They emulsify: tails embed in grease, heads face the water, droplets repel each other and rinse away — grease stays as separate droplets.ms bank; CP 2012 Q14(b)
