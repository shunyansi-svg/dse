Syllabus Master Overview

Topic IV Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(f))

Every Students should learn / be able to strand of Acids and Bases, with HKEAA watch-outs from mark-scheme language and Candidate Performance reports.

Topic IV (a) Acids & alkalis

Acids: H⁺(aq) in water, typical reactions, the role of water, basicity — and alkalis with OH⁻(aq)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Common laboratory acids (hydrochloric, sulphuric, nitric, ethanoic) and common alkalis (sodium hydroxide, aqueous ammonia, potassium hydroxide, limewater); household examples of each.
  • Acidic properties come from H⁺(aq): acids react with active metals, carbonates, hydrogencarbonates, metal oxides and bases — write the chemical and ionic equations.
  • Water is essential: dry hydrogen chloride gas shows no acidic properties on dry litmus — the H⁺(aq) ions only exist in aqueous solution.
  • Basicity = number of ionisable H⁺ per molecule: HCl 1, H₂SO₄ 2, H₃PO₄ 3, CH₃COOH 1 (four hydrogens, only one ionisable).
  • Alkaline properties come from OH⁻(aq): alkalis turn litmus blue, react with ammonium salts to release NH₃ and with metal ions to give hydroxide precipitates.
  • Concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive — hazard labels and safe handling / dilution practice.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

An “acid” answer must mention water: “HCl contains hydrogen” alone scores nothing — say it ionises in water to give H⁺(aq). CP 2012 Q11: candidates missed that 2.0 M H₂SO₄ beats 2.0 M HCl on [H⁺(aq)] because sulphuric acid is dibasic. CP 2016 Q6: “quite a number” wrote the carbonate equation (2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻) where HCO₃⁻ was asked; separately (syllabus (a) / ms bank §4), CH₃COOH is monobasic despite four H atoms.

Topic IV (b) Indicators & pH

Litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein, universal indicator — and pH as a measure of [H⁺(aq)]

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Colour table: litmus red (acid) / blue (alkali); methyl orange red / orange / yellow; phenolphthalein colourless (acid & neutral) / pink (alkali); universal indicator full rainbow → approximate pH.
  • $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+(\text{aq})]$ — the scale measures hydrogen-ion concentration; one pH unit = a ten-fold change in [H⁺(aq)].
  • pH < 7 acidic, = 7 neutral (at 25 °C), > 7 alkaline; calculations only for strong acid solutions (and strong alkalis via [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴).
  • Test for acidity / alkalinity with a suitable indicator — describe method, observation and conclusion separately.
  • Measure pH with universal indicator (approximate) vs pH meter / data-logger (accurate, instrumental).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

End-point wording is policed: methyl orange “red to orange” — “red to yellow” means the titration was over-shot (CP 2012 Q7); phenolphthalein “from colourless to pink” — “clear to red” or a bare “pink” is rejected (CP 2013 Q4). Dilute a strong acid ten-fold and pH rises by exactly 1 — approaching but never reaching 7; a “pH above 7 after diluting acid” answer is marked wrong.

Topic IV (c) Strength ≠ concentration

Strong vs weak, concentrated vs dilute — THE confusion pair of this topic

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Strong acid/alkali = completely ionised in water (single arrow); weak = partially ionised, equilibrium with the molecules (⇌). HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, NaOH, KOH strong; CH₃COOH, H₂C₂O₄, aqueous NH₃ weak.
  • Concentrated / dilute describe how much acid is dissolved per unit volume — a physical amount, not the ionisation extent.
  • Concentrated weak ≠ dilute strong: “ethanoic acid is concentrated, so it is strong” is the classic false inference — the two words live on different scales.
  • Compare strengths at the same concentration and temperature: measure pH (stronger → lower pH for acids), rate of H₂ with magnesium, or conductivity (stronger → more mobile ions).
  • Same-pH challenge: distinguish a strong and a weak acid of equal pH — dilute both and the weak acid’s pH changes less (reserve of unionised molecules keeps ionising).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

CP 2014 Q5: candidates “wrongly related the strength of acid to some irrelevant factors like basicity and pH”. CP 2015 Q5: “comparing the amounts of HCl needed to neutralise” a weak vs strong alkali fails — equal moles consume equal acid regardless of strength. CP 2013 Q4 recorded “ionisation/dissociation” swapped — for acids the mark-scheme word is ionisation.

Topic IV (d) Salts & neutralisation

Neutralisation H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, and the three salt-preparation routes with their choice rules

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Bases (metal oxides / hydroxides) are the chemical opposites of acids; alkalis are the soluble bases. Neutralisation = acid + base/alkali → salt and water only: $\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})$.
  • Neutralisation is exothermic — temperature rise is measured (same-volume, same-concentration comparisons keep it fair).
  • General solubility rules: all nitrates, all group-I and ammonium salts soluble; chlorides except AgCl / PbCl₂; sulphates except BaSO₄ / PbSO₄ (CaSO₄ slightly); carbonates and hydroxides insoluble except group I / NH₄⁺ (Ba(OH)₂ and Sr(OH)₂ soluble; Ca(OH)₂ sparingly soluble).
  • Method choice: soluble salt from acid + excess insoluble solid (metal/oxide/carbonate) — filter, crystallise; soluble salt from acid + alkali — titration then mix without indicator; insoluble salt — precipitation, filter, wash, dry.
  • Name common salts; applications of neutralisation: antacids, lime treating acidic soil / lake water, excess ammonia treated with acid in industry.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

CP 2012 Q6: PbSO₄ was treated as soluble — it is not. CP 2014 Q12: candidates “confused the proper procedure of obtaining an insoluble substance” (filter, wash, dry) “with obtaining a soluble compound from a solution” (evaporate / crystallise). A method-justification mark needs the words insoluble / soluble + excess / measured volumes, not a recipe alone.

Topic IV (e) Concentration

mol dm⁻³ (molarity), g dm⁻³, converting between them, dilution and making up solutions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ with V in dm³; “0.5 M” = 0.5 mol dm⁻³. Convert cm³ by ÷ 1000 once.
  • Interconvert: $\text{concentration (g dm}^{-3}) = c \times M_r$; from % by mass + density: mass of solute per dm³ = density × 1000 × % (then ÷ Mᵣ) — multiply by 1000, never divide (CP 2014 Q7).
  • Dilution: solute moles conserved → $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$; water added = V₂ − V₁.
  • Prepare a solution of required concentration by dissolving a solid (weigh, dissolve in beaker, transfer, make up to the mark in a volumetric flask) or diluting a stock solution (for a DILUTE stock, pipette the stated volume and make up to the mark; for CONCENTRATED acids, measure into a beaker of water — acid into water, never the reverse — never in a volumetric flask, never pipetted — CP 2015 Q4).
  • Titration scaling: moles in the pipetted aliquot only; × (total ÷ aliquot) to recover the whole sample.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

CP 2014 Q7: dividing 1.18 g cm⁻³ by 1000 instead of multiplying lost the concentration mark; “the unit must be correct” — mol dm⁻³ vs g dm⁻³ mix-ups score zero. CP 2015 Q4: pipette / volumetric flask suggested for diluting concentrated sulphuric acid — heat and apparatus misuse; CP 2016 Q6: the apparatus answer is volumetric flask, not beaker/conical flask.

Topic IV (f) Volumetric analysis

Standard solutions, acid–alkali titration technique, concordant titres — and back titration (solvable with sufficient info)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Standard solution = solution of accurately known concentration; anhydrous Na₂CO₃ serves as a primary-standard base salt, while NaOH(s) (deliquescent, absorbs CO₂) and concentrated HCl (HCl(g) escapes) cannot be primary standards.
  • Apparatus & craft: burette (rinse with the solution to fill it; remove air bubble below the jet), pipette + filler (rinse with the solution to be measured), conical flask rinsed with distilled water only, white tile under the flask, swirl while adding drop-wise near the end point.
  • Readings at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus, to the nearest 0.05 cm³; record to two decimal places.
  • Rough titre first, then repeats; average only concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other).
  • Calculations: $c_bV_b$ with the acid:base mole ratio (mind dibasic / tribasic acids); apply to vinegar, drain cleaner, tablet neutralisation.
  • Back titration: add a measured excess of standard reagent, titrate what remains; solvable “with sufficient information given” NTF-2018 II-B IV (f) — aspirin is the set example.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

SBA 2014/2015/2016 all recorded burette readings written to one decimal place — wrong granularity. CP 2013 Q4(e) recorded candidates saying the residual acid “affects the concentration … instead of its volume” — it adds extra moles of H⁺(aq), so MORE alkali is needed; distilled water left in the flask changes no moles (only the volume/speed) → titre unaffected. CP 2012 Q7: most failed multi-step calculations — mark schemes award step marks (*), so write every line.

🚫 Out of scope for Topic IV (Notes-for-Teachers depth caps — do not test here)

Numeric pH of weak acids/bases (no Kₐ work) — only strong electrolytes are calculated; Kw/pOH vocabulary and buffer calculations — the water ion product appears only as the given [H⁺(aq)] × [OH⁻(aq)] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C; hydrolysis of salts — never explain a salt solution’s pH from K_b logic; titration curves and error-propagation calculations — indicator choice and qualitative error reasoning only; systematic naming of organic acids beyond the named examples (ethanoic, citric, ethanedioic); the aspirin-tablet analysis practical itself is not expected NTF-2018 II-B XI (e), although back-titration problems are solvable with sufficient information NTF-2018 II-B IV (f). Equilibrium detail of ionisation continues in Topic X / Module 9A.

IV (a)

Common acids and alkalis — daily life and laboratory

$$\text{acids: } \text{HCl},\ \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ \text{HNO}_3,\ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \qquad \text{alkalis: } \text{NaOH},\ \text{KOH},\ \text{NH}_3\text{(aq)},\ \text{Ca(OH)}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Laboratory acids: dilute hydrochloric, sulphuric, nitric acids; the everyday weak acid ethanoic acid (vinegar), citric acid (lemons), carbonic acid (drinks).
  • Laboratory alkalis: sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, aqueous ammonia, limewater; household alkalies: drain cleaner (NaOH), baking soda (basic salt), toothpaste, antacid.
  • Recognise which household substances are acidic vs alkaline from indicator behaviour, not from memory alone.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Learn the names in this exact spelling: sulphuric (the American “f” variant is not HKEAA style), nitric, hydrochloric, ethanoic, citric.
  • “Baking soda” items recur: baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate) is a basic salt, not an alkali — answer with its reaction with acid, not “OH⁻”; the cached raising-agent item is potassium hydrogencarbonate (CP 2012 Q8(a)) — and soda ash is the CARBONATE Na₂CO₃, not the hydrogencarbonate.
IV (a)

What makes an acid acidic — H⁺(aq)

$$\text{HCl(g)} \xrightarrow{\ \text{water}\ } \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{Cl}^-(\text{aq})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • An acid is a substance that ionises in water to produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq); all common acidic properties (sour taste, litmus red, reactions below) come from these ions.
  • Relate any observed acidic property back to “presence of H⁺(aq) ions” — that phrase is the mark.
  • All aqueous solutions contain H⁺(aq) — even alkaline ones (in low concentration): CP’s MC key for exactly this wording (CP 2015 Q1).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Write H⁺(aq) with the state symbol; “hydrogen” or “H₂” is NOT ACCEPT. The ion is produced from the acid by water, not already present in the pure liquid acid.
  • “All acidic compounds contain hydrogen” was chosen by 19% of candidates — false: CO₂ is acidic (ionises water to give H⁺) without containing hydrogen (CP 2015 Q1).
IV (a)

The role of water in exhibiting acidic properties

$$\text{dry HCl(g)} + \text{dry litmus} \to \text{no change};\qquad \text{HCl(g)} + \text{water} \to \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{Cl}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{red}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Design evidence: test dry hydrogen chloride gas with dry litmus paper (no change, no fizzing with carbonate) — then with wet paper (turns red): the acid behaviour needs water.
  • Explain: without water the H–Cl molecules stay unionised; no free H⁺(aq) exists to give the properties.
  • Same logic for solid citric acid vs its solution: the solid does not conduct / shows weak surface properties until dissolved.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Three-part answer for full marks: observation with dry paper, observation with wet paper, conclusion naming H⁺(aq) produced in water.
  • Do NOT say “water makes the acid stronger” — water lets the acid ionise; strength is a property of the acid itself (Engine C).
IV (a)

Basicity of an acid — how many H⁺ per molecule

$$\text{HCl (monobasic)}\quad \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{ (dibasic)}\quad \text{H}_3\text{PO}_4\text{ (tribasic)}\quad \text{CH}_3\text{COOH (monobasic)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Basicity = number of ionisable hydrogen atoms per molecule, i.e. how many H⁺ one molecule releases in water.
  • Mark-scheme sentence to own: “HCl is a monobasic acid, while H₂SO₄ is a dibasic acid”.
  • Consequence for arithmetic: same molarity → H₂SO₄ has twice the [H⁺(aq)] of HCl; 1 mol H₂SO₄ neutralises 2 mol NaOH; citric acid is tribasic (3 ionisable H) (CP 2016 Q6).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Basicity ≠ strength ≠ concentration. CP 2014 Q5 recorded candidates tying “strength of acid” to basicity — a weak acid can be monobasic, a strong acid dibasic: different words, different scales.
  • CH₃COOH has 4 hydrogen atoms but basicity 1 — only the –COOH hydrogen ionises. CP 2016 Q6 asked exactly this for citric acid (3 of 8 ionisable).
IV (a)

Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen

$$\text{Mg(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \to \text{MgCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)} \qquad \text{ionic: } \text{Mg(s)} + 2\text{H}^+\text{(aq)} \to \text{Mg}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Observations: effervescence, metal dissolves / heat may be felt; gas gives a pop with a lighted splint (H₂ test).
  • Salts formed are named from the acid: chlorides from HCl, sulphates from H₂SO₄, nitrates from HNO₃.
  • Rate comparisons at equal [H⁺(aq)] belong to Topic IX — here just know the products and tests; acid + Mg is also the strength-comparison probe (Engine C).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Not every metal: copper, silver, gold do not release H₂ from dilute acids (their position in the electrochemical series, Topic VII).
  • Concentrated nitric / sulphuric acids with metals give not hydrogen but sulphur dioxide / nitrogen dioxide — CP 2014 Q5: candidates wrongly said H₂ with copper and the oxidising concentrated acids.
IV (a)

Acid + carbonate / hydrogencarbonate → CO₂

$$2\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{CO}_3^{2-}(\text{aq}) \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} \qquad \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{HCO}_3^{-}(\text{aq}) \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Effervescence of a gas that turns limewater milky (CO₂ test); salt solution left behind.
  • Carbonate needs 2 H⁺, hydrogencarbonate only 1 — the two ionic equations above must not be swapped.
  • Applications: baking powder (potassium hydrogencarbonate raising agent — CP 2012 Q8), marble chips + acid (CO₂ preparation), antacid action of hydrogencarbonates.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2016 Q6: “quite a number” wrote 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ where the substance was a hydrogencarbonate — wrong ion equation, zero.
  • Give the answer as an ionic equation when the question asks ionic: CP 2012 Q7 logged “complete equation instead of an ionic equation” as a losing slip.
IV (a,d)

Acid + metal oxide / metal hydroxide → salt + water

$$\text{CuO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \to \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \qquad \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Metal oxides and hydroxides are bases; with acids they give salt + water only — the essence of neutralisation (Engine D).
  • Colour cues worth owning: black CuO → blue solution; brown Fe₂O₃ → yellow/brown iron(III) solution.
  • This is the “acid + excess insoluble base” route to making soluble salts — the same chemistry, applied (Engine D card).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2015 Q10 required seeing that Cl₂O’s aqueous solution is acidic (and NOT hydrochloric acid alone — check the equation the question offers); the ms bank carries P₂O₅ + 3H₂O → 2H₃PO₄ — non-metal oxides give acidic solutions, while CuO is a base — classify before predicting.
  • MS accepts “silicon dioxide can neutralise alkalis to form salt and water” — basic oxides are not the only bases; SiO₂ is insoluble in water.
IV (a)

Alkalis — bases that dissolve: OH⁻(aq)

$$\text{NaOH(s)} \xrightarrow{\ \text{water}\ } \text{Na}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \qquad \text{NH}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_4^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • A base reacts with acid to form salt and water only; an alkali is a base that dissolves in water giving OH⁻(aq). All alkalis are bases; NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ (sparingly), aqueous NH₃.
  • Alkaline properties from OH⁻(aq): litmus blue, slippery feel, pH > 7, reactions with metal ions and ammonium salts.
  • Syllabus defines bases “in terms of their reactions with acids” — keep the definition operational, not “pH > 7”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • NaOH(s) is deliquescent and absorbs CO₂ from air (ms bank) — that is why it cannot be a primary standard and why solid NaOH pellets are coated/caked.
  • Aqueous ammonia is the only common weak alkali you must name; CP 2015 Q5: “NH₃ is a weak alkali because it ionises slightly in water”, about two thirds got it — with the equilibrium arrow ⇌ in the equation.
IV (a)

Alkali reactions — hydroxide precipitates and the ammonium test

$$\text{Cu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{Cu(OH)}_2\text{(s)} \qquad \text{NH}_4^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{NH}_3\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Dilute alkalis on aqueous metal ions: Cu(OH)₂ blue, Fe(OH)₂ green → brown, Fe(OH)₃ reddish-brown, Al(OH)₃/Ca(OH)₂ white precipitates — the identification set.
  • Alkali + ammonium compound (warm): ammonia — pungent gas turning moist red litmus blue; the standard NH₄⁺ test.
  • Al(OH)₃ dissolves in excess NaOH (amphoteric) while Mg(OH)₂ does not — the classic separation hint.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • For precipitate answers include colour + state: “blue precipitate forms”, not “blue solution” — CP 2014 Q9 logged the wrong copper(II) hydroxide colour.
  • Write “moist red litmus” for the ammonia test; NH₃ only changes the paper when water dissolves it (the role-of-water idea again).
IV (a)

Corrosive concentrated acids & alkalis — hazard and handling

$$\text{“corrosive” label} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{concentrated acid / alkali} \qquad \text{dilution: add ACID to WATER, never water to acid}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive — burn skin, destroy fabric; hazard warning label and safety spectacles / gloves / tongs.
  • Neutralise small spills: dilute acid spills with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (weak base), alkali spills with dilute ethanoic / boric acid, then wash with plenty of water.
  • Diluting concentrated sulphuric acid: measure acid, slowly add it with stirring to a beaker of water — the process is strongly exothermic.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “A corrosive label is required on all reagent bottles containing acid solution” is FALSE — very dilute acids need no label (CP 2015 Q1); the label tracks concentration + corrosiveness, not the mere word “acid”.
  • CP 2015 Q4: a pipette or volumetric flask used to dilute concentrated H₂SO₄ was a common wrong procedure — heat can shatter graduated glassware; use a beaker.

The four acid audiences — what H⁺(aq) reacts with

Any “describe the reaction of dilute hydrochloric acid with …” stem is one of these four routes; each ends at a salt plus a signature small molecule.

+ metal → salt + H₂

Mg/Zn/Fe only (not Cu). Effervescence; pop test. $\text{Zn(s)} + 2\text{H}^+\text{(aq)} \to \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$. Also the strength-comparison probe.

+ carbonate / hydrogencarbonate → salt + H₂O + CO₂

Effervescence; limewater turns milky. Carbonate: $2\text{H}^+ + \text{CO}_3^{2-}$; hydrogencarbonate: $\text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^{-}$ — never swap (CP 2016 Q6).

+ metal oxide (base) → salt + water

Insoluble black CuO / brown Fe₂O₃ disappear, solution takes the ion’s colour. Route to soluble salts with excess solid — Engine D.

+ alkali (hydroxide) → salt + water

Clear solutions, no visible sign — needs an indicator or a thermometer: neutralisation is exothermic. Route to soluble salts by titration — Engine D.

Alkalis mirror the acids

OH⁻(aq) attacks ammonium salts → NH₃ (pungent, moist red litmus blue) and metal ions → insoluble hydroxides (Cu²⁺ blue ppt, Fe³⁺ reddish-brown ppt, Al³⁺ white ppt dissolving in excess NaOH). Both routes are the identification tools Paper 1B loves — the same two ions, H⁺ and OH⁻, run the whole topic.

Multi-blank Quiz — Acids & Alkalis

1. An acid is a substance that ionises in water to produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq) as the only positive ions in its aqueous solution.
2. Dry hydrogen chloride gas leaves dry blue litmus unchanged, but turns it red on moist / wet paper — proof that water is needed to make H⁺(aq).
3. Basicity is the number of ionisable hydrogen atoms per molecule: HCl is monobasic (1), H₂SO₄ is dibasic (2), H₃PO₄ is tribasic, and CH₃COOH is monobasic — only the –COOH hydrogen ionises.
4. 2.0 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄ has a higher [H⁺(aq)] than 2.0 mol dm⁻³ HCl because sulphuric acid is dibasic — this is what CP 2012 Q11 blamed.
5. Magnesium + dilute hydrochloric acid gives magnesium chloride + hydrogen; the gas is tested with a lighted splint (pop), and the ionic equation is Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g).
6. Sodium carbonate + acid releases CO₂ — test: turns limewater milky; for a hydrogencarbonate the ionic equation is H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) + CO₂(g), not the carbonate equation (2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻).
7. Copper(II) oxide + dilute sulphuric acid: the black solid dissolves and the solution turns blue, forming copper(II) sulphate + water.
8. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water to give hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq); copper(II) oxide is a base but not an alkali — it is insoluble.
9. Warming ammonium chloride with sodium hydroxide solution gives a pungent gas that turns moist red litmus blue — the gas is ammonia, NH₃; ionic equation NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O (ms neglects states here).
10. Adding dilute sodium hydroxide to copper(II) sulphate solution gives a blue precipitate of Cu(OH)₂; to iron(III) chloride a reddish-brown precipitate; to aluminium chloride a white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH.
11. Concentrated sulphuric acid is diluted by adding the acid to the water slowly with stirring, because dilution is strongly exothermic — never pipette or volumetric-flask the concentrated acid (CP 2015 Q4).
12. Reagent bottles containing very dilute acid need no “corrosive” label — CP 2015 Q1: 13% chose the “label on every acid bottle” option.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Acids & Alkalis

Hydrogen vs hydrogen ions

Acids contain hydrogen atoms, but only H⁺(aq) after ionising in water causes acidic behaviour. “Ethane contains hydrogen, so it is an acid” is NOT ACCEPT — and CO₂ is acidic without any hydrogen.

Basicity vs strength

Basicity counts ionisable H’s per molecule (H₂SO₄ = 2); strength measures how completely those H’s ionise (H₂SO₄ = strong, CH₃COOH = weak). Both words can describe the same molecule independently — CP 2014 Q5 shows they get merged wrongly.

Base vs alkali

Base = reacts with acid to give salt + water only. Alkali = a base that dissolves in water giving OH⁻(aq). CuO, Fe₂O₃ are bases but not alkalis; NaOH, NH₃(aq) are alkalis.

Carbonate vs hydrogencarbonate equation

CO₃²⁻ needs 2H⁺; HCO₃⁻ needs 1H⁺. Both give H₂O + CO₂, but the balancing H⁺ differ — the exact error CP 2016 Q6 recorded for antacid-style hydrogencarbonate items.

Ionic vs complete equation

If the stem says “ionic equation”, giving Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ instead of Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g) loses the mark — CP 2012 Q7 “complete equation instead of an ionic equation”.

Dilute acids vs “no hazards”

Concentrated → corrosive label + burn risk; very dilute → no corrosive label (CP 2015 Q1 distractor). Safety marks hinge on reading the concentration stated in the stem, not assuming “acid = dangerous”.

NH₃(aq) vs NH₄⁺ vs NH₄OH “molecules”

Aqueous ammonia is NH₃(aq) ionising slightly to NH₄⁺ + OH⁻; there is no “NH₄OH molecule”. And with alkali added, it is the NH₄⁺ ion that releases NH₃ — keep all three particles straight in the ammonium test.

Calculations: [H⁺(aq)] and pH of strong acids

IV (b)

pH of strong alkalis — via the water ion product

$$[\text{H}^+(\text{aq})] \times [\text{OH}^-(\text{aq})] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{mol}^2\ \text{dm}^{-6}\ (25\,{}^{\circ}\text{C})$$
🎯 Learning Goals

💡 Examiner Tips
  • Use the product as given — the studio does not name it Kw or teach pOH: those are beyond Topic IV scope.
  • Ba(OH)₂ / Ca(OH)₂ give 2 OH⁻ per formula unit — the same “multiply by basicity” trap as H₂SO₄, on the alkaline side.

Multi-blank Quiz — Indicators & pH

1. Litmus turns red in acidic solutions and blue in alkaline solutions; it gives no numeric pH, only the acidic/alkaline verdict.
2. Methyl orange is red in acid, orange around the transition and yellow in alkali; the accepted end-point wording is “changes from red to orange”.
3. Phenolphthalein stays colourless in acidic and neutral solutions and turns pink in alkali — CP 2013 Q4 rejected “clear to red”.
4. $\text{pH} = $ −log₁₀[H⁺(aq)]; a solution with [H⁺(aq)] = $2 \times 10^{-3}$ has pH ≈ 2.7, and going from pH 3 to pH 1 means [H⁺(aq)] is 100 times larger.
5. 0.020 mol dm⁻³ HCl has pH = 1.7; 0.050 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄ has [H⁺(aq)] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³ (dibasic!) and pH = 1.0.
6. At 25 °C a neutral solution has pH 7 because [H⁺(aq)] = [OH⁻(aq)], and $[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = $ 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.
7. 0.10 mol dm⁻³ NaOH: [OH⁻] = 0.10 → [H⁺] = 1.0×10⁻¹³ → pH = 13.0; the MS chain even records a pH of 14.2 for a 1.6 mol dm⁻³ [OH⁻] mixture.
8. Diluting a strong acid by a factor of 10 raises the pH by exactly 1; repeated dilution makes pH approach 7 but never reach or exceed it.
9. To test for acidity: take a small sample, add a few drops of indicator (or use moist litmus), then state the colour change and the conclusion — never dose the stock bottle.
10. The approximate pH comes from universal indicator + chart; the accurate reading comes from a pH meter / data-logger.
11. A 1.0 mol dm⁻³ strong monobasic acid has pH = 0 — so “the pH of all acid solutions is greater than zero” was the 22%-chosen wrong statement of CP 2015 Q1.
12. For a strongly coloured solution the indicator end point is hard to see (CP 2013 Paper 2 §C: the red-wine colour masked it) — switch to a pH meter / data-logger instead.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Indicators & pH

End point vs over-shot

Methyl orange red → orange is the stop; “red → yellow” is what the flask looks like after you blew past it — CP 2012 Q7’s named error. Same idea for phenolphthalein: pale permanent pink, not magenta.

Colourless ≠ acid

Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and neutral. A colourless phenolphthalein solution cannot be ranked more acidic than a pink one’s counterpart — say what the indicator can and cannot tell you.

pH difference vs [H⁺] ratio

pH 2 vs pH 4: the [H⁺(aq)] ratio is 100, not 2. Inverse and logarithmic: bigger pH number → smaller [H⁺]. Questions like “how many times more acidic” always want 10^(ΔpH).

Acidic solution vs pure acid

Concentrated ethanoic (glacial) acid barely ionises without water; dilute solutions carry H⁺(aq). “All aqueous solutions contain H⁺(aq)” was the MC key — even NaOH(aq) does, just at low [H⁺] (CP 2015 Q1).

Universal vs pH meter

If a stem asks for the “most accurate” pH or a pH–volume record, the answer is pH meter / data-logger; universal indicator is for quick approximate classification of many samples (domestic substances activity).

pH 0 is possible; pH “above 14” is possible

1 M strong acid → pH 0; [OH⁻] = 1.6 → pH 14.2 (MS chain). The 0–14 window is the common school range, not a hard wall — the definitions are [H⁺] and [OH⁻] through the 10⁻¹⁴ product.

Engine C · Strands (c) + (e)

Strength vs Concentration Inspector

Two scales that every DSE acid question keeps apart — and the molarity arithmetic that rides on both.

Pick a term

IV (c)

Strong acids — 100 % ionised

$$\text{HCl(aq)} \to \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{Cl}^-(\text{aq}) \qquad \text{one-way arrow}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Strong = completely ionised in aqueous solution: no HCl molecules left, only ions — the definition is about extent, nothing else.
  • The strong set to name: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, plus the strong alkalis in the next card. H₃PO₄ appears in the syllabus as the tribasic example and is a weak acid at this level — proof that basicity and strength are independent labels.
  • [H⁺(aq)] = basicity × concentration → low pH at any decent concentration.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Use “ionised / completely ionised”, not “dissolved” or “dissociated” — CP 2013 Q4 recorded the wording drift; mark-scheme sentences for acids speak of ionisation.
  • “A strong acid is one with a low pH” is circular and rejected — pH is an effect of both strength and concentration.
IV (c)

Weak acids — a small equilibrium ionisation

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH(aq)} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-(\text{aq}) + \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) \qquad \text{mostly molecules, few ions}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Weak = partially ionised: an equilibrium sits mostly on the molecule side, so [H⁺(aq)] ≪ concentration.
  • Name the syllabus weak acids: ethanoic (vinegar), citric, ethanedioic — organic nomenclature stops at these named examples.
  • Consequences at equal concentration: higher pH, slower reaction with Mg / carbonates, dimmer conductivity bulb than a strong acid.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The MS-grade sentence: “H₂C₂O₄ is a weak acid. It undergoes incomplete ionisation in water. As pH = −log₁₀[H⁺(aq)], its pH is thus greater than 1.” — give this shape, never a numeric weak-acid pH (out of scope).
  • The ⇌ arrow is part of the mark in equation questions; single arrow for a weak acid = conceptual error flagged by examiners.
IV (c)

Strong and weak alkalis

$$\text{NaOH(aq)} \to \text{Na}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \qquad \text{NH}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_4^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Strong alkalis: NaOH, KOH (Group-I hydroxides) fully ionised; aqueous ammonia is the classic weak alkali — ionises only slightly.
  • “NH₃ is a weak alkali because it ionises slightly in water” — the exact reason two-thirds of candidates could give in CP 2015 Q5.
  • Ca(OH)₂ / Ba(OH)₂: the part that dissolves ionises completely (strong but sparingly soluble — concentration stays low).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not write NH₄OH as the ionising species; start from NH₃(aq) + H₂O with ⇌.
  • Solubility ≠ strength: “limewater has low pH so Ca(OH)₂ is weak” is the wrong inference — it is strong but only slightly soluble.
IV (c,e)

Concentrated vs dilute — an amount of solute, not a strength

$$\text{concentration} = \frac{\text{amount of acid dissolved}}{\text{volume of solution}} \quad(\text{mol dm}^{-3}\ \text{or}\ \text{g dm}^{-3})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Concentrated = large amount of solute per dm³; dilute = small amount. You can change either by adding water; you cannot change strength by dilution.
  • Concentrated acids often react differently for other reasons (conc. H₂SO₄ / HNO₃ are oxidising — CP 2014 Q5; conc. is corrosive — strand (a)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The sentence to memorise: “concentrated ethanoic acid is still a weak acid; dilute hydrochloric acid is still a strong acid.” The words describe different quantities — one is a label on the bottle, one on the ionisation.
  • When comparing “reactivity with Mg” of two acids, state both strengths and concentrations are controlled — CP 2014 Q10 punished missing fair-test conditions.
IV (c)

The two scales side by side — pick each word on purpose

$$\underbrace{\text{strong} \leftrightarrow \text{weak}}_{\text{extent of ionisation (what it IS)}} \qquad \underbrace{\text{concentrated} \leftrightarrow \text{dilute}}_{\text{amount dissolved (how MUCH you put in)}}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Any of the four adjectives may combine freely: strong+concentrated (conc. HCl), strong+dilute (0.001 M HCl), weak+concentrated (glacial CH₃COOH), weak+dilute (vinegar).
  • Describe acids and alkalis “with the appropriate terms: strong and weak, concentrated and dilute” — that is the syllabus able-to, so use the pairs, never “powerful / weak solution”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • If a solution’s pH is quoted, the pair “stronger acid” may simply mean higher [H⁺(aq)] — do not read strength from pH without matching concentration (CP 2014 Q5’s “irrelevant factors like basicity and pH”).
  • In data questions, “0.0010 M HCl, pH 3” vs “0.1 M CH₃COOH, pH 3” proves both scales exist at once — write the explanation with the words ionisation extent and concentration separately.
IV (c)

Comparing strengths — the valid methods and their controls

$$\text{same } c,\ \text{same } T:\quad \text{pH}\downarrow \; / \; \text{rate with Mg}\uparrow \; / \; \text{bulb brighter} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{stronger}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Method 1: measure pH of equal concentrations — lower pH (more H⁺ per mole) → stronger acid; higher pH → stronger alkali.
  • Method 2: same concentration + same excess Mg ribbon (same surface area, same T), compare initial rate of bubbles (or gas volume in fixed time).
  • Method 3: conductivity lamp with equal concentrations — stronger electrolyte → more mobile ions → brighter bulb.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Invalid for CP 2015 Q5: “compare the amounts of HCl needed to neutralise” equal volumes of NH₃ / NaOH — equal moles neutralise equal acid whatever the strength; the comparison proves nothing about ionisation.
  • “About a third of candidates were not able to state the appropriate conditions for a fair test” — write same concentration, same temperature, same surface area, same volume (CP 2015 Q5; CP 2014 Q10).
IV (c)

The same-pH challenge — distinguishing strong from weak

$$\text{equal pH, } \times 10 \text{ dilution: strong } \Delta\text{pH} = 1,\qquad \text{weak } \Delta\text{pH} < 1$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Listed syllabus experiment: distinguish a strong and a weak acid having the same pH.
  • Route A — dilute both ten-fold and measure pH: strong rises exactly 1 unit; weak rises less, because more molecules ionise to top H⁺(aq) back up.
  • Route B — add equal excess Mg (same surface area): the weak acid keeps fizzing longer: its reserve of unionised molecules refills the H⁺(aq) as it is used.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not argue “weak has less acid” — at the same pH the total acid content of the weak solution is larger; the explanation is “reserve of unionised molecules”, the CP 2013 ionisation vocabulary.
  • Both routes need the words “same volume / same pH” at the start — the control clause earns a mark by itself.
IV (e)

Molarity — concentration in mol dm⁻³

$$c = \frac{n}{V} \quad (V \text{ in dm}^3) \qquad 25.0\ \text{cm}^3 = 0.0250\ \text{dm}^3 \qquad 1\ \text{mol dm}^{-3} \equiv \text{“1 M”}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Number of moles in a volume: $n = c \times V$ (with V in dm³) — the engine of every titration calculation (Engine D).
  • Convert cm³ → dm³ by ÷ 1000 exactly once; keeping units as printed (mol dm⁻³) avoids CP 2012 Q11-type unit losses.
  • How much water to dissolve 5.3 g Na₂CO₃ for 250 cm³ of 0.2 M? Work: n = 5.3/106 = 0.05 mol; c = 0.05/0.250 = 0.2 M ✓.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “the unit must be correct” is mark-scheme language — “0.2 mol” for a concentration earns nothing.
  • Molarity is a property of the whole solution: pipetting 25 cm³ from 250 cm³ of 0.2 M gives 0.2 M in the aliquot (but fewer moles) — CP 2013 Q4(e)’s “concentration instead of volume” confusion dies on this line.
IV (e)

Converting g dm⁻³ ↔ mol dm⁻³ (and from % + density)

$$\text{g dm}^{-3} = c\,(\text{mol dm}^{-3}) \times M_r \qquad c = \frac{\text{density}\times1000\times w\%}{M_r}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mass concentration (g dm⁻³) ÷ Mᵣ = molarity; × Mᵣ back again — the syllabus “convert the molar concentration to g dm⁻³” able-to.
  • Labelled-bottle route (CP 2014 Q7): 1.18 g cm⁻³ HCl, 36 % by mass → one dm³ weighs 1180 g → HCl mass = 1180 × 0.36 → ÷ 36.5 = c.
  • Express final answers in the unit the stem demands (mg dm⁻³ in CP 2013 Paper 2 §C; mol dm⁻³ in Engine D titles).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2014 Q7: candidates divided 1.18 by 1000; dm³ is a thousand times bigger than cm³, so density is multiplied by 1000 to get g dm⁻³.
  • Check the answer’s size sense: “0.0012 mol dm⁻³” from a 36 % bottle of acid means you divided somewhere you should have multiplied.
IV (e)

Dilution — c₁V₁ = c₂V₂ and doing it safely

$$c_1V_1 = c_2V_2 \quad(\text{moles of solute conserved}) \qquad V_{\text{water added}} = V_2 - V_1$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • To make 250 cm³ of 0.5 M from 2.0 M: pipette V₁ = 0.5×250/2.0 = 62.5 cm³ of the stock into water, then make up to the mark — measuring pipette + volumetric flask (or beaker route for concentrated acids).
  • Dilution changes concentration, pH, [H⁺] — never strength; the ionisation fraction of a weak acid even creeps up, which is why its pH shifts less than 1 unit per 10×.
  • Concentrated acids dilute acid into water, in a beaker, with stirring — graduated glassware is not the vessel (heat!).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2015 Q4: “pipette / volumetric flask” written for diluting concentrated sulphuric acid — procedure mark lost; the beaker-and-stir answer is the safe one.
  • CP 2014 Q10: dilute the given 1 M HCl with water to get 0.5 M / 0.25 M — inventing “2 M or 4 M” bottles is not an answer when only the 1 M is supplied.

The 2×2 grid — four corners, four different acids

Rows ask “how much did you dissolve?”; columns ask “what fraction ionises?”. A molecule cannot escape its column by being poured into less water.

Strong + concentrated

≈ 12 M HCl, ~18 M H₂SO₄. Fully ionised (before saturation) at a high load: very low pH, violently corrosive, oxidising behaviour appears for hot conc. H₂SO₄ / HNO₃ with metals.

Strong + dilute

0.001 M HCl. Still 100 % ionised — pH 3 comes purely from the low concentration. This corner demolishes “higher pH therefore weaker acid”.

Weak + concentrated

Glacial ethanoic acid: lots of molecules packed in, but each ionises only slightly — still a weak acid, though its pH is low because c is huge.

Weak + dilute

Vinegar (~0.8 M CH₃COOH): partial ionisation of a small load — highest pH of the four corners; the everyday weak-acid case.

What moves a compound across the grid

Adding water slides any solution horizontally (concentrated → dilute) with no strength change. Nothing short of changing the chemical identity moves it between columns: HCl stays strong in any beaker, CH₃COOH stays weak. The MS vocabulary for the columns: “completely / slightly (incompletely) ionised in water”.

Multi-blank Quiz — Strength & Concentration

1. A strong acid is one that is completely ionised in aqueous solution; a weak acid is one that is partially (slightly) ionised — the equation of a weak acid must carry the reversible arrow ⇌.
2. Concentration measures how much acid is dissolved per unit volume; diluting a strong acid cannot turn it into a weak one, because strength depends on extent of ionisation only.
3. The MS sentence for oxalic acid: “H₂C₂O₄ is a weak acid — it undergoes incomplete ionisation; as pH = −log₁₀[H⁺(aq)], its pH is greater than 1.”
4. To compare the strength of two acids fairly use the same concentration and the same temperature (and same Mg surface area), then compare pH, rate of H₂, or conductivity.
5. Comparing the amounts of HCl needed for neutralisation of equal volumes of NH₃(aq) and NaOH(aq) at equal concentration is invalid, because equal moles of alkali consume equal moles of acid whatever their strength (CP 2015 Q5).
6. Two acids of the same pH are diluted ten-fold: the strong acid’s pH rises by exactly 1, the weak acid’s by less than 1, because the weak acid’s unionised molecules continue to ionise and replace H⁺(aq).
7. Molarity: $c = n/V$ with V in dm³. 5.3 g of Na₂CO₃ (Mᵣ 106) in 250 cm³ gives 0.05 mol → 0.20 mol dm⁻³.
8. 4.9 g dm⁻³ of H₂SO₄ (Mᵣ 98) is 0.05 mol dm⁻³; multiply molarity by Mᵣ to go back to g dm⁻³ — and by basicity to get [H⁺(aq)].
9. A bottle reads 1.18 g cm⁻³, 36 % HCl (Mᵣ 36.5): one dm³ weighs 1180 g (multiply the density by 1000 — CP 2014 Q7 divided!), so c ≈ 1180×0.36÷36.5 ≈ 11.6 mol dm⁻³.
10. To prepare 100 cm³ of 0.1 M HCl from 1.0 M: pipette 10.0 cm³ (c₁V₁ = c₂V₂) of the stock into water and make up to the mark — adding water to concentrated acid is the CP 2015 Q4 procedure error.
11. Equal volumes, equal concentrations of HCl and CH₃COOH need the same moles of NaOH to neutralise, yet HCl reacts faster with Mg and has the lower pH — three different facts, one cause: the extent of ionisation.
12. A weak electrolyte solution conducts less well than a strong one of the same concentration because it has a lower concentration of mobile ions — the charge still travels by ions, never “mobile electrons” (CP 2016 Q2).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Strength & Concentration

Concentrated vs strong

Concentrated = much solute per volume (choose by pouring). Strong = ionises fully (choose by identity). Concentrated ethanoic acid is weak; dilute HCl is strong. CP’s most persistent wrong inference in this topic.

Strength vs pH

pH reports the current [H⁺(aq)] — a joint product of strength and concentration. Only at equal concentrations may you rank strengths by pH (CP 2014 Q5).

Strength vs basicity

Basicity counts how many H⁺ a molecule can release; strength asks what fraction does. H₂SO₄ is dibasic and strong; citric is tribasic and weak. Both words can point the other way independently.

Ionisation vs dissociation

For acids/bases in water the mark-scheme word is ionisation (molecules → ions). CP 2013 Q4: candidates “used the terms interchangeably” and explanations of the weak acid’s pH fell apart.

“Weak” vs “does not react”

Weak acids still neutralise alkalis fully and still dissolve Mg — slowly. “There is no acid in ethanoic acid solution, only molecules” overstates: the ⇌ means some H⁺(aq) and CH₃COO⁻(aq) do exist.

Same neutralising power ≠ same strength

Equal moles of HCl and CH₃COOH neutralise equal NaOH (titration says nothing about ionisation extent). CP 2015 Q5 lost half its marks on exactly this swap.

Dilution maths traps

Strong acid ×10 → pH +1, but pH never reaches 7 (the water’s own H⁺ takes over). And moles before = moles after: c₁V₁ = c₂V₂ counts solute, not “acid strength × volume”.

Pick a term

IV (d)

Neutralisation — bases as the chemical opposites of acids

$$\text{acid} + \text{base/alkali} \to \text{salt} + \text{water only} \qquad \text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Neutralisation destroys the properties of both partners: H⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq) combine into water; the ions left behind form the salt.
  • Write both the chemical equation (ZnO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)) and the ionic equation for any acid–base pair given.
  • With carbonates the products are salt + water + CO₂ — still “neutralisation” in school usage, but the strict definition here is salt and water only.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The definition mark needs “salt and water only” (or the H⁺+OH⁻ equation); “pH becomes 7” alone is an observation-style answer, weaker wording.
  • Insoluble bases (CuO) neutralise acids too — “only alkalis can be neutralised” is rejected: bases ≠ alkalis (Engine A).
IV (d)

Neutralisation is exothermic

$$\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \qquad \Delta H \approx -57\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\ (\text{strong acid + strong base; value belongs to Topic VIII — here just know neutralisation is exothermic})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mixing acid and alkali raises the temperature; the rise peaks when neither is in excess.
  • Investigation pattern (CP 2016 Q11): vary one reagent’s concentration/volume but keep the total volume constant so the comparison is fair.
  • Insulation (polystyrene cup), lid, stirring, thermometer reading to 0.5 °C — practical craft examinable in the paper.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2016 Q11: fewer than half stated the fair-test control “keep the total volume of the reaction mixture the same so that only [NaOH(aq)] varies” — write the control clause as its own sentence.
  • Weak acids give a smaller temperature rise (energy soaked up by further ionisation) — a legitimate Topic IV comparison, deeper numerics are not asked.
IV (d)

Naming salts & the solubility rules

$$\text{cation name} + \text{anion name}: \ \text{chloride (HCl)},\ \text{sulphate (H}_2\text{SO}_4),\ \text{nitrate (HNO}_3),\ \text{ethanoate (CH}_3\text{COOH)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Anion follows the acid: hydrochloric → chloride, sulphuric → sulphate, nitric → nitrate, ethanoic → ethanoate; variable metals keep Roman numerals (iron(II)/(III), copper(II)).
  • Soluble: all nitrates; all Na⁺/K⁺/NH₄⁺ salts; chlorides except AgCl, PbCl₂; sulphates except BaSO₄, PbSO₄ (CaSO₄ slightly).
  • Insoluble: carbonates and hydroxides except group I / NH₄⁺ — the short list that decides method choice (next three cards).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2012 Q6: PbSO₄ written off as soluble — it is the textbook insoluble sulphate, and that fact changes the whole preparation route.
  • “Lead(II) chloride is soluble in cold water” is the error to dodge — it dissolves in hot water and re-crystallises on cooling (PbCl₂ vs PbSO₄ is a classic separation item).
IV (d)

Method 1 — soluble salt from acid + excess insoluble solid

$$\text{CuO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \to \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \quad \xrightarrow[\text{filter}]{\text{warm, excess CuO}} \xrightarrow{\text{crystallise}} \text{CuSO}_4\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Use when the starting base is an insoluble solid (metal, oxide, hydroxide, carbonate): add it in excess to warm acid with stirring until solid remains unreacted.
  • Why excess? to make sure all the acid has reacted. Then filter — the residue is the leftover solid; the filtrate is pure salt solution.
  • Finish: evaporate filtrate to the crystallisation point, cool to grow crystals, filter, dry between filter papers (do not “evaporate to dryness” for hydrates).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Communication marks (CP 2014 Q4 pattern) reward an ordered, logical sequence — write add-excess → filter → crystallise as numbered steps, each with its reason.
  • For reactive metals (Mg, Zn) with acid skip “warm” gently at most; vigorous — and for carbonates add slowly, fizzing overflow is a real method mark.
IV (d,f)

Method 2 — soluble salt by titration (acid + alkali)

$$\text{HCl(aq)} + \text{NaOH(aq)} \to \text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \qquad \text{“both solutions — no visible sign”}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Use for soluble salt from two solutions (acid + alkali): an excess-solid route is impossible (nothing undissolved to filter off).
  • Run a titration with indicator to find the exact reacting volumes; repeat without indicator mixing those measured volumes; evaporate the salt solution to crystallisation.
  • Why “without indicator next time”? — the indicator would contaminate the crystals; the volumes are already known.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Excess NaOH then filter” is the standard losing answer here — NaOH(aq) has no solid residue to remove; the method mark distinguishes this case from Method 1.
  • When asked to suggest a method, name the apparatus (pipette, burette, conical flask, evaporating basin) — CP 2016 Q6 apparatus naming shows the marks live there.
IV (d)

Method 3 — insoluble salt by precipitation

$$\text{BaCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \to \text{BaSO}_4\text{(s)} + 2\text{NaCl(aq)} \qquad \text{ionic: Ba}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{SO}_4^{2-}\text{(aq)} \to \text{BaSO}_4\text{(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Salt insoluble? Mix two soluble salts supplying its two ions; a precipitate forms immediately.
  • Then: filter → wash the residue with distilled water (remove adsorbed NaCl etc.) → dry between filter papers / in a warm oven.
  • Both reactant solutions must be soluble — “make barium sulphate from BaSO₄ powder and water” has no chemistry in it.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Every step-word carries a mark: filter (not evaporate), wash (why: remove soluble impurity), dry (where: between filter papers). CP 2014 Q12 logged the filtration/crystallisation swap.
  • For PbSO₄, pick Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) — nitrates and sodium salts guarantee both partners are soluble; CP 2012 Q6 shows they are not always.
IV (d)

Separating the product — filtration vs crystallisation

$$\text{INSOLUBLE solid from mixture: } \text{filter, wash, dry} \qquad \text{SOLUBLE salt from solution: } \text{evaporate, crystallise, filter, dry}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ask what you are recovering: the residue (insoluble salt) or the filtrate (soluble salt crystals) — the answer dictates the whole technique paragraph.
  • Crystallisation route: evaporate to the crystallisation point (first crystals / saturated film), cool, filter the crystals, press dry between filter papers.
  • “Evaporate to dryness” only for salts stable without water of crystallisation considerations; hydrates (CuSO₄·5H₂O) pop and lose water — use the gentle route.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2014 Q12, verbatim: candidates “confused the proper procedure of obtaining an insoluble substance from a mixture with that of obtaining a soluble compound from a solution” — decide which you want in the first sentence.
  • Decanting, “rinse with a little distilled water”, and why (washings contain dissolved salt — combine filtrates) are the small clauses that turn a description into a full-marks account.
IV (d)

Applications of neutralisation

$$\text{Mg(OH)}_2\text{(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \to \text{MgCl}_2\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \quad (\text{antacid against stomach acid})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Antacid tablets (insoluble bases: Mg(OH)₂, CaCO₃, NaHCO₃) neutralise excess stomach acid (HCl(aq)).
  • Slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ treats acidic soil and acidic lake water (after acid-rain damage); chalk/limestone as alternatives.
  • Industry: neutralising acidic effluents before discharge; flue-gas SO₂ scrubbed with alkaline slurries (STSE link from the syllabus overview); relief of insect stings (an acidic sting — everyday example, the specific acid is not required → mild alkali).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • For “why is Mg(OH)₂ a better antacid than NaOH?”, the safety answer is NaOH’s corrosive nature (strand (a)) + insoluble base avoids overdose alkalinity.
  • Match the explanation level to the stem: name the acid neutralised and the products; “it balances the pH” earns nothing.
IV (f)

Standard solutions — accurately known concentration

$$\text{standard solution} = \text{a solution of accurately known concentration} \qquad \text{solid Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \to \text{weigh} \to \text{dissolve (beaker)} \to \text{make up (volumetric flask)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Preparation from a solid: weigh accurately → dissolve in distilled water in a beaker (stir) → transfer all solution + rinsings into the volumetric flask → add water to the mark → stopper and invert to mix.
  • A good primary-standard solid: stable, non-hygroscopic, high purity — anhydrous sodium carbonate fits the school role.
  • Why not NaOH(s)? It is deliquescent and absorbs CO₂ from air (MS wording); why not conc. HCl? HCl(g) escapes, so the bottle’s concentration drifts (CP 2014 Q7(c)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2014 Q7(b) is the crime scene: dissolving the powder inside the volumetric flask, skipping distilled water, or describing the task as a dilution — the heat of solution cracks the flask argument and the weight of the solid is what defines c.
  • “Rinse the beaker and glass rod with distilled water into the flask” — the rinsings clause is a named step mark in procedural questions.
IV (f)

Titration apparatus & technique — the marks live in the craft

$$\text{burette (variable volume)} \quad \text{pipette + filler (fixed volume)} \quad \text{conical flask (swirl)} \quad \text{white tile}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Rinse the burette with the solution that will fill it, the pipette with the solution it will measure; the conical flask with distilled water only.
  • Fill past the zero mark, open the tap to expel the air bubble below it, then adjust to/below 0.00 cm³ and record the initial reading to 0.05 cm³.
  • Add the burette solution swirl-and-drop, drop-wise near the end point, washing any wall-hanging drop in with distilled water; read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  • “pipette” spelled right earns the apparatus mark — the MS flags (†) correct spelling.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Conical flask over beaker: less splashing and easier swirl — if asked “why a conical flask?”, that is the answer; the residual distilled water in it changes no moles, so no titre.
  • Why rinsing burette/pipette matters: water left inside dilutes the solution it measures and the concentration delivered is no longer what the numbers claim — the rinse-error MS sentences live on the next card.
IV (f)

Concordant titres & error reasoning

$$\text{titre} = \text{final} - \text{initial reading} \qquad \text{concordant: two titres within } 0.10\ \text{cm}^3 \to \text{average those only}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Do a rough titration first to locate the end point region, then repeat until two titres agree to within 0.10 cm³; record readings to two decimal places (0.05 cm³ reading).
  • Exclude the rough (and any over-shot) titre from the average; the average of concordant titres feeds the calculation.
  • Error directions to own (mark-scheme wording): rinsing the conical flask with the acid → “some H⁺(aq) ions remain in the flask, and more alkali than actually required is used”; residual distilled water in the flask → moles unchanged → no effect on titre; funnel left on the burette → “NaOH(aq) clinging onto the stem of the funnel may fall into the burette — the volume of alkali used is smaller than what is expended”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • SBA 2014/2015/2016 all complain about one-decimal-place burette readings; write 22.50 not 22.5 — the trailing zero is a precision statement.
  • CP 2013 Q4(e) recorded candidates saying the residual acid “affects the concentration … instead of its volume” — it adds extra moles of H⁺(aq), so MORE alkali is needed; distilled water left in the flask changes no moles (only the volume/colour-shape), so the titre is unaffected.
IV (f)

Titre calculations & back titration (e.g. aspirin)

$$\text{MS example: } c(\text{NaOH}) = \frac{0.05 \times 25 \times 2}{17.20} = 0.145\ \text{mol dm}^{-3} \quad(\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 2\text{NaOH})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Standard chain: moles from the standard solution ($n = cV$) → mole ratio from the equation → moles of the analyte in the pipetted aliquot → × (flask ÷ aliquot) → concentration or mass.
  • Basicity sets the ratio: H₂C₂O₄:NaOH = 1:2, citric:NaOH = 1:3 — the “×2, ÷3” steps carry the step marks (*).
  • Back titration (solvable when sufficient information is given — NTF-2018 II-B IV (f); aspirin is the set example): add a known excess of standard reagent, react fully, then titrate what remains — moles used = added − excess.
  • Worked aspirin data (illustrative): 0.400 g crushed tablet + 50.0 cm³ of 0.110 mol dm⁻³ NaOH (excess, warmed; 1 mol aspirin reacts with 2 mol NaOH); back-titration of the excess needs 20.4 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. n(NaOH) = 5.50×10⁻³; excess = 2.04×10⁻³; with aspirin = 3.46×10⁻³; n(aspirin) = 1.73×10⁻³; m = 1.73×10⁻³ × 180.2 = 0.312 g → 77.9 % pure.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The MS step-mark skeleton for back titration is literally “moles of NaOH(aq) added … moles of A = (added) − (excess) … percentage by mass = m ÷ sample × 100 %” (ms bank §11) — line it up and the marks stack even if arithmetic wobbles.
  • Writing the aspirin analysis as a practical design is out of scope (NTF-2018 II-B XI (e)); solving the given-information calculation is fully examinable — read which one the stem wants.

Which preparation method? — one question, three routes

Ask the solubility question FIRST. The answer splits the work into three syllabus routes, each with its own exam sentence.

Soluble salt + insoluble starting solid

Acid + excess solid: warm acid, add solid until in excess, filter, crystallise the filtrate. Examples: CuSO₄ from CuO + H₂SO₄; ZnCl₂ from Zn + HCl; Ca(NO₃)₂ from CaCO₃ + HNO₃.

Soluble salt + two solutions (acid + alkali)

Titration method: indicator run to find volumes → repeat without indicator → evaporate to crystallise. Examples: NaCl, K₂SO₄, KNO₃ — every group-I salt from its alkali.

Insoluble salt

Precipitation: mix two soluble salts giving the ions, filter, wash with distilled water, dry between filter papers. Examples: BaSO₄, PbSO₄, AgCl, CaCO₃.

Not a neutralisation salt?

Direct combination (e.g. anhydrous chlorides from elements) sits outside the three routes — DSE method-choice questions stick to the three panels above. When the starting solid is a sulphate you cannot reduce like BaSO₄, no acid route exists: that is itself the exam answer.

The volumetric workflow — strand (f) in one strip

Make/obtain a standard solution → pipette a fixed aliquot into the conical flask + indicator → burette titrate with concordant-repeat discipline → average the concordant titres → $n = cV$, mole ratio, scale the aliquot → answer with units. Every bench in the Solver tab is one link of this chain; the back-titration variant inserts “add known excess → titrate the leftovers → subtract”.

Multi-blank Quiz — Salts & Volumetric Analysis

1. Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base / alkali forming salt and water only; the ionic equation is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) and the reaction is exothermic.
2. Solubility screening: BaSO₄ is insoluble, AgCl is insoluble, all nitrates and all sodium / potassium / ammonium salts are soluble — CP 2012 Q6 lost marks on PbSO₄ being called soluble.
3. To make copper(II) sulphate crystals: add excess copper(II) oxide to warm dilute sulphuric acid, filter the surplus solid away, then crystallise the filtrate; the excess is used to ensure all the acid has reacted.
4. Sodium chloride cannot be made by the excess-solid method because both NaOH(aq) and HCl(aq) are solutions — no excess solid to filter off; use the titration method, then mix the measured volumes without indicator and evaporate.
5. To prepare dry barium sulphate: mix aqueous BaCl₂ and Na₂SO₄, filter, wash the residue with distilled water (removes adsorbed NaCl) and dry between filter papers.
6. A standard solution has an accurately known concentration; from solid Na₂CO₃ you dissolve in a beaker with distilled water first, never inside the volumetric flask (CP 2014 Q7).
7. NaOH(s) cannot be a primary standard because it is deliquescent and absorbs CO₂ from the air; concentrated HCl cannot because HCl(g) escapes so its concentration drifts (CP 2014 Q7).
8. Rinsing discipline: burette and pipette are rinsed with the solution they will contain; the conical flask is rinsed with distilled water only — water left in it changes no moles, hence no titre error.
9. Burette readings are recorded to two decimal places (nearest 0.05 cm³), reading the bottom of the meniscus at eye level — the SBA reports 2014–2016 all flagged one-decimal recording.
10. Concordant titres agree within 0.10 cm³; the rough titre is excluded from the average because it was only a scout run.
11. MS rinse-error sentence: “Rinsing the conical flask with H₂C₂O₄(aq): some H⁺(aq) ions remain in the flask, and more alkali than actually required is used to reach the end point.”
12. In a back titration a known excess of standard reagent is added first, then the leftover reagent is titrated, so moles used = added − excess — examinable with sufficient information given, the aspirin pattern NTF-2018 II-B IV (f).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Salts & Volumetric Analysis

End point vs equivalence (stoichiometric) point

The end point is what you see — the indicator’s colour change; the equivalence point is where the reacting quantities match the equation. A good indicator makes them practically coincide (hence the MS trio of requirements: observable change, essentially complete, fast).

Rinse the burette… but never the flask

Rinsing the conical flask with analyte leaves extra moles → larger titre (MS wording); distilled water in it changes nothing. Same verb, opposite consequences — decide by “does it add moles to the reaction?”

Titre value vs readings

The burette delivers 22.50 cm³ as a difference of readings (e.g. 25.00 − 2.50); the reading starts anywhere ≤ 0.05 past zero, not always at zero — “start at 0.00 cm³” is a preference, the titre is final − initial.

Average the repeats, not everything

Include the rough titre (say 24.50 against 22.45/22.50) and the mean — and your answer — is wrong. Average only concordant titres within 0.10 cm³.

Filtration vs crystallisation targets

Insoluble salt = you want the residue: filter, wash, dry. Soluble salt = you want the filtrate: crystallise it out. CP 2014 Q12: the techniques were swapped wholesale.

Excess solid vs measured volumes

Method 1 needs excess (unreacted solid proves the acid is finished); Method 2 cannot use excess (both sides are solutions — excess of which? how seen?). Naming this asymmetry answers most “justify the method” marks.

Titration measures amount, not strength

A titre counts moles. Whether those moles came from a strong or weak acid is invisible to the burette — that is CP 2015 Q5’s neutralisation-amount fallacy in one line. Strength needs pH / rate / conductivity comparisons at equal concentration.

Pipette vs burette jobs

Pipette: deliver one fixed accurate volume (the aliquot). Burette: deliver a variable, measured volume (the titrant). CP 2016 Q6: candidates offered “beaker / conical flask” as apparatus for the fixed-volume job — the marks are on the roles, and (†) on the spelling “pipette”.

1 · Definitions to state word-perfect (Engines A–D)▾
TermExam wording
AcidA substance that ionises in water to produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq); its aqueous solution shows the typical acidic reactions and has pH < 7 at 25 °C.
BaseA substance (metal oxide / hydroxide) that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only — the chemical opposite of an acid.
AlkaliA base that dissolves in water to produce hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq); aqueous solution has pH > 7 at 25 °C.
Basicity of an acidThe number of ionisable hydrogen atoms per molecule of the acid, i.e. the number of H⁺ one molecule can release in water (HCl 1, H₂SO₄ 2, H₃PO₄ 3, CH₃COOH 1).
Strong acid / strong alkaliAn acid (alkali) that is completely ionised in aqueous solution.
Weak acid / weak alkaliAn acid (alkali) that is only partially (slightly) ionised in aqueous solution — a reversible equilibrium with the unionised molecules.
Concentrated solutionA solution containing a relatively large amount of the solute (acid/alkali) dissolved per unit volume.
Dilute solutionA solution containing a relatively small amount of solute per unit volume. Concentrated/dilute say nothing about the extent of ionisation.
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base/alkali to form a salt and water only; ionic basis H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). It is exothermic.
PrecipitationThe formation of an insoluble solid (a precipitate) by mixing two solutions whose ions combine into an insoluble salt; used to make insoluble salts.
Standard solutionA solution of accurately known concentration, e.g. made by weighing a solid, dissolving in distilled water and making up to a fixed volume in a volumetric flask.
End point vs equivalence pointThe end point is where the indicator changes colour during a titration; the equivalence (stoichiometric) point is where the reactants have been mixed in exactly the mole ratio of the equation. A suitable indicator changes colour at (or within a drop of) the equivalence point.
Back titrationA technique in which a measured excess of a standard reagent is added to the analysis sample, the reaction is completed, and the unreacted excess is then titrated with a second standard solution; moles used by the sample = moles added − moles in excess (solvable with sufficient information given — NTF-2018 II-B IV (f)).

4 · Titration technique checklist — the craft marks (Engine D)▾
☐ Rinse the burette with the solution that will fill it and the pipette with the solution it will measure — never the conical flask (distilled water only).
☐ Fill burette past zero, drain to expel the air bubble under the tap, then set the initial reading.
☐ Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, to the nearest 0.05 cm³; record with two decimal places (22.50 not 22.5) — the SBA complaint of CP 2014, CP 2015 and CP 2016.
☐ Swirl the conical flask; add titrant drop-wise near the end point; wash hanging drops in with distilled water.
☐ White tile under the flask so the colour change is seen clearly.
☐ Rough titre first, then repeat until two titres are concordant (within 0.10 cm³); average the concordant pair only.
☐ Pipette the fixed aliquot; note that residual distilled water in the flask changes no moles → no titre error (the CP 2013 Q4(e) error was about residual acid adding moles).
☐ Indicator: few drops, and its name spelled right — “phenolphthalein”, “pipette” († = correct spelling in the MS).
☐ Calculation uses n = c × V with the mole ratio of the equation (dibasic / tribasic acids!).
