Topic XIII (a) Why industry matters

Importance of industrial processes — development of synthetic products for modern ways of living

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Discuss advantages and disadvantages of industrial processes such as petrochemistry from social, economic and environmental perspectives (Part I wording).
  • Appreciate that synthetic manufacturing eases inadequate or shrinking supply of natural products — the syllabus’s own headline case is the recent progress in the production of vitamin C (awareness only: process details are not expected).
  • Name what industry delivers: fertilisers relieving food-supply problems, polymers, detergents, solvents, fuels, medicines (STSE connections, Part I).
  • Value safe transport and storage of hazardous substances: ammonia, acetic acid, hydrogen, chlorine, sodium hydroxide (values & attitudes).
  • Recall the two disaster names the Guide uses: Bhopal and Minamata — careless disposal or leakage of chemicals.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Use of the product” is not “use in the question”: when asked an industrial use of methanol, “making ethanoic acid” was accepted but “a fuel, a material for making wine or a food additive” was recorded wrong (CP 2012 Q1(b)(i)). When asked for advantages/disadvantages, the answer must be tagged to the named process, not general industry talk (CP 2012 Q1(b)(iii) pattern).

Topic XIII (b) Rate laws from data

Rate equation — rate equation determined from experimental results

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Understand the interrelationship between reaction rate, rate constant k, concentration of reactants and order of reaction: $$\text{rate} = k[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n$$ (Part I wording).
  • Determine the rate equation by the method of initial rates — compare experiments that change one reactant at a time; deduction must be visible.
  • Orders are experimental: 0, 1, 2 — and fractional (the bank prints rate $=k[\text{A}][\text{B}]^{3/2}$) — never read off the balancing coefficients.
  • State the overall order ($m+n$) and derive the correct units of k: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ (0th) · s⁻¹ (1st) · mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ (2nd).
  • Know that k changes only with temperature — concentration, surface area and catalyst leave k untouched.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Write “rate = k[NO]²[H₂]”, not “initial rate = …” — recorded format error (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)). Do not swap k and K (equilibrium constant) — recorded confusion (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)). Units are marks: “only about one third of the candidates were able to calculate the rate constant with correct unit” (CP 2016 Q1(c)). Half-life is not expected (NTF-2018 II-B XIII (b)).

Topic XIII (c) Energy & the Ea graph

Activation energy — energy profile, temperature effect, Arrhenius equation

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Activation energy: the minimum energy colliding particles need for reaction to occur — for the reaction to start (CP 2014 Q1).
  • Draw an energy profile of a reaction: reactants → transition-state hump → products; label Eₐ and ΔH; catalysed and uncatalysed routes go in the same sketch (CP 2015 Q1).
  • Explain temperature effects with the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curve: higher T → curve flattens and shifts right → larger portion of molecules will have kinetic energy greater than Eₐ (ms bank §7).
  • Arrhenius equation as the Guide prints it: $\log k = \text{constant} - \frac{E_a}{2.3RT}$; plotting log k vs $1/T$ gives slope $-\frac{E_a}{2.3R}$ (ms bank §7).
  • Determine Eₐ from first-hand data or from a given set of data (Part I wording).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

MB x-axis label: “molecular kinetic energy” — “energy” alone “is not precise enough” (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i)); the area under the curve = the total fraction of molecules and does not grow with temperature (CP 2016 Q1(a)). On the log-k graph read “log k” as log (not ln) and read the axis factor $10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ exactly (CP 2013 Q1(a)(ii)).

Topic XIII (d) Catalysis, paid wording

Catalysis and industrial processes — meaning and characteristics of catalyst; activation energy and catalysis

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • A catalyst “provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy” (ms bank §7) — the only causal clause the mark scheme pays.
  • Describe the effect of a catalyst on reversible reactions: forward and backward rates rise to the same extent; equilibrium position (and yield) unchanged; equilibrium is reached faster (ms bank §7).
  • Describe characteristics with examples: iron in the Haber process; enzymes in the production of alcoholic drinks (Part I wording).
  • Homogeneous vs heterogeneous advantage lines: “H₂SO₄ is readily available” vs “can be reused / easily be regenerated / easily be separated” (ms bank §7).
  • Industry facts: “finely divided iron” (Haber); “Impurities in the reaction mixture may poison the catalyst” (ms bank §7; CP 2015 Q1).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Never write “the catalyst lowers the activation energy” without the alternative-pathway clause (CP 2013 Q1(b)(i)). “Platinum” credited where the answer is iron (CP 2012 Q1(a)(i)). Enzymes are catalysts but “would be denatured at high temperature” — ignored by more than half of candidates (CP 2014 Q1). Same-pathway wording applies to both directions of a reversible reaction — “concentrated H₂SO₄ provides more H⁺(aq)” is not a catalysis explanation (CP 2013 Q1(b)(ii)).

Topic XIII (e) Named plants

Industrial processes — fertilisers from ammonia, chloroalkali industry, methane → methanol via syngas

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Ammonia: describe feedstock, principles, conditions, procedures, products; explain the physicochemical principles; the industry “often involves a compromise between rate, yield and economic considerations” (Part I wording).
  • Ammonia → fertilisers: conversion of ammonia to fertilisers; importance of fertilisers to our world; downstream NO → NO₂ → nitric acid chemistry (CP 2016 Q1(b)).
  • Chloroalkali: electrolysis of brine; flowing mercury cell and membrane cell principles; importance of the industry (Part I wording).
  • Methanol: conversion of methane, syngas, CO(g) + 2H₂(g) ⇌ CH₃OH(g); advancement of the process (Part I wording).
  • Discuss social, economic and environmental considerations of any of the three named processes (Part I wording).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Raw materials are named, not guessed: H₂ from “natural gas” (steam reforming) — “almost all candidates” failed that (CP 2014 Q1(c)); N₂ from “fractional distillation of liquefied air” (CP 2016 Q1(b)). Half of candidates could not write the Haber equation (CP 2015 Q1); only a quarter gave the chloroalkali overall equation plus the membrane-vs-diaphragm advantage (CP 2015 Q1).

Topic XIII (f) Green arithmetic

Green chemistry — principles, practices, atom economy, and evaluation of processes

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Describe the relation between sustainable development and green chemistry — green chemistry “reduces or eliminates the use or generation of hazardous substances in the design, manufacture and application of chemical products” (Part I STSE).
  • Calculate the atom economy of a reaction: Atom economy = Mᵣ(desired product) ÷ ΣMᵣ(all reactants) × 100% — the MS prints the chain, e.g. “Atom economy = 58/76 = 76.32%” (ms bank §10).
  • Relate green principles to practices “as exemplified by the manufacture of acetic acid (ethanoic acid)” — example-level only; process details are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B XIII (f)).
  • Evaluate an industrial process using green principles: higher atom economy, less waste, less hazardous chemicals used, renewable feedstock, biodegradability (ms bank §10).
  • Contrast feedstock choices: corn starch “is a renewable resource” / “may reduce the food supply… lead to deforestation” (ms bank §10).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Atom economy is not yield — differentiation recorded as a failure (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iv)); and atom economy “is based on a 100% completed reaction. Most reactions do not go to completion” (ms bank §10). Decide “which method is greener” from the given information — economics/politics/society arguments gain nothing (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iii)). A communication mark is allocated for green discussions (ms bank §10); round sensibly — “a long string of numerals” was flagged (CP 2013 Q1(c)(ii)).

Engine A · Strand (b)

Rate Equation — Inspector

The elective’s kinetics upgrade over Topic IX: rate laws come from experimental results, never from the equation. Every card below is keyed to a bank line or a Candidate Performance record — the two things Paper 2 marks.

Pick a rate-law item

Strand (b) in one line: “understand the interrelationship between reaction rate, rate constant, concentration of reactants and order of reaction” + “determine the rate equation … by method of initial rate” (Part I wording).
XIII (b)

The rate equation

$$\text{rate} = k[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n$$

The power m is the order of reaction with respect to A, n with respect to B. The equation is a result of experiments — the strand’s own bullet reads “rate equation determined from experimental results” (Part I wording).

Nothing in the balanced equation entitles you to write the powers. For 2NO(g) + 2H₂(g) → N₂(g) + 2H₂O(g) the data-led law is rate = k[NO]²[H₂] — matching coefficients here is a coincidence (CP 2012 Q1(c)).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • State the rate equation of a reaction from experimental data, labelling k, [ ], m, n.
  • Use the equation in both directions: predict rates from concentrations, and back out k.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Head the answer “rate = …”. “initial rate = k[NO]²[H₂]” was collected as a wrong format (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).
  • A wrong format still exposed wrong orders — “rate equation = k[NO][H₂]” was recorded (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).
XIII (b)

Order of reaction — including zero and fractional

zero order: rate is independent of [A]  ·  bank specimen: rate = k[A][B]^(3/2)

The bank’s own zero-order test: “zero order… rate of change of [A] is independent from [A]” (ms bank §7) — double the concentration, the initial rate does not move.

Orders are not confined to whole numbers. The bank prints a 3⁄2 order with its unit consequence: “order w.r.t. B = 3/2; unit of k = mol⁻¹·⁵ dm⁴·⁵ s⁻¹” (ms bank §7).

Never deduce an order from a coefficient: orders describe how the rate responds, a balanced equation does not.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Define the order with respect to each reactant from rate-vs-concentration behaviour.
  • Recognise zero order in data (rate unchanged when that concentration changes).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • In the 2016 sucrose hydrolysis item the two reactants had different treatment: “most candidates were able to deduce the order of reaction with respect to HCl(aq) but not many … with respect to C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁(aq)” (CP 2016 Q1(c)) — check each species separately.
  • “The reaction is first order overall” and “first order in X” are different claims; say which you mean.
XIII (b)

“Initial rate” — say what it means

$$\text{initial rate} = \text{slope of the tangent to the rate curve at } t = 0$$

The definition is worth a mark on its own: “About three quarters of the candidates were not able to explain the term ‘initial rate’” (CP 2014 Q1(b)). Spell it out: the rate at the start of the reaction, when concentrations have their known starting values and have not yet fallen.

Two measurement idioms appear in the bank: “Determine the slope of the tangent of curve at t = 10 s” (from a graph) and “Initial rate = 60/4 = 15 cm³/min” (first 60 cm³ collected in 4 min — early-time average taken as initial rate) (ms bank §7).

Why initial? Because rate changes as reactants are used up — the initial rate is the only point where the rate can be cleanly tied to the starting concentrations in the rate equation.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Define initial rate and locate it on a volume/time or concentration/time curve.
  • Compute an initial rate from early data (volume ÷ time) with units.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “A very small number of the candidates were able to give the initial rate of reaction with respect to BrO₃⁻(aq)” — with several species, name the species whose concentration you are tracking (CP 2014 Q1(b)).
  • When monitoring colourless-appearing product by colorimetry, “were not able to state that the solution is colourless at the start” — read the curve from its true beginning (CP 2014 Q1).
XIII (b)

Method of initial rates — the procedure

$$\frac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1} = \left(\frac{[\text{A}]_2}{[\text{A}]_1}\right)^{m}$$

(all other concentrations held constant)

Run the reaction several times, each time changing the starting concentration of one reactant only while keeping the others (and the temperature) constant; measure the initial rate of each run; compare pairs to extract m and n.

Fair-test discipline from the Paper 1B marklist: keep “the total volume of the reaction mixtures the same … so that the concentration of NaOH(aq) would be the only variable”, and keep experimental conditions constant “when varying the concentrations” (CP 2014 Paper 1B context).

Worked logic: doubling [A] quadruples the initial rate → 2m = 4 → m = 2. Doubling [B] does nothing → n = 0.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Design an initial-rate experiment for a two-reactant reaction (≥3 runs).
  • Turn a table of runs into a rate equation with a shown deduction.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Most candidates were able to deduce the reaction orders. However, some candidates did not show a correct deduction” — write the ratio step, do not just announce the order (CP 2012 Q1(c)(ii)).
  • The 2014 report’s dilution sin: changing a concentration by adding water without topping up to a fixed total volume voids the comparison (CP 2014 Paper 1B context).
XIII (b)

Overall order & rate forecasts

$$\text{overall order} = m + n$$

e.g. [A] ×2 with m = 2 ⇒ rate ×4

Overall order is the sum of the powers. For rate = k[NO]²[H₂] the overall order is 3 — and the bank’s fractional case, rate $=k[\text{A}][\text{B}]^{3/2}$, has overall order 2.5 (ms bank §7).

Forecasts are the cheap marks: [A] × 3 with m = 1 → rate × 3; both reactants doubled at once → multiply each factor. Temperature changes are not forecast by the rate equation — they act through k.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • State the overall order of any rate equation, fractional included.
  • Predict the factor change in initial rate for named concentration changes.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A zero-order component is a silent component — “rate of change of [A] is independent from [A]” (ms bank §7); do not keep writing 20=1 chatter where the data already show no effect.
  • Keep the forecast arithmetic visible; “many of them were not able to complete the calculation” (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).
XIII (b)

Rate constant k — and what k is not

$$k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n}$$

k is the proportionality constant tying rate to the concentration terms. It is constant at fixed temperature: concentration changes, catalysts and surface area do not change k; temperature does (strand (c) supplies the machinery: log k vs 1/T).

The k-vs-K confusion is named in the record: “Many candidates confused ‘rate constant k’ with ‘equilibrium constant K’” (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)). K_c is a ratio of equilibrium concentrations with no rate meaning; k is a rate constant.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Calculate k from one experiment’s concentrations + initial rate once the law is known.
  • State which factors change k (temperature) and which do not (concentration, catalyst, surface area).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Always carry the units of k — “Incorrect units were commonly presented after calculation” (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)); the general convention “the unit must be correct” runs through every bank (ms bank, general).
  • A catalyst changes the rate by opening another route (different k for that route) — it never “uses up” the rate law; do not write k = rate × [A]².

  • Produce the units of k for any overall order, including 2.5.
  • Switch time units (s⁻¹ vs min⁻¹) without breaking the mol/dm bookkeeping.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Second-order k is printed both ways in the wild — “mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹” and “dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹” — pick either and keep the mol and dm exponents consistent.
  • A wrong k unit on a correct numerical value still loses the unit mark; show the cancellation (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).

XIII (b)

Rate-determining step — one line, no arrows

overall rate is controlled by the slowest step (rate-determining step)

If a reaction happens in stages, the slowest step throttles the whole sequence — the rate equation reflects the species involved up to and including that step. That is the entire mechanism lesson here: no curly arrows, no intermediate hunting. (Reaction mechanisms to explain how carbon compounds react are “not expected” even in Topic XI — not_expected log, Topic XI.)

The Initial-Rate Route — four gates, every table question passes through

Every 2012 / 2015 / 2016 rate mark sits behind one of these gates. The failure recorded for that gate is printed underneath — revision means practising the gate you leak.

Gate 1 · Pair up (fix the variables)

Find two runs where only one concentration differs — total volumes and temperature identical (CP 2014 Paper 1B context).

Leak: run 1 vs run 3 comparisons that change both reactants — m and n come out meaningless.

Gate 2 · Ratio → exponent

$\frac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1} = 4$, $\frac{[\text{A}]_2}{[\text{A}]_1} = 2$ → $2^m = 4$ → $m = 2$ — write the line.

Leak: “deduction not shown” (CP 2012 Q1(c)(ii)); orders guessed from coefficients.

Gate 3 · Assemble the law

Head it “rate =”, insert orders, add the overall order if asked.

Leak: “initial rate = …” / “rate equation = …” prefixes (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).

Gate 4 · k + units

$k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n}$ → number + derived units ((mol dm⁻³)1−n time⁻¹).

Leak: units wrong “commonly” (CP 2012); only ⅓ finished with correct unit (CP 2016 Q1(c)).

The strip that connects: rate responds to concentration through the orders (this engine); rate responds to temperature through k (Engine B: Arrhenius); industry tunes rate with catalyst + conditions (Engines B & C). One reaction, three levers, three vocabularies — never mix them.

Multi-blank Quiz — Rate Equation

1. The rate equation of a reaction is determined from experimental results, never read from the balancing coefficients; its shape is rate = k[A]^m[B]^n (Part I wording).
2. The order with respect to A is the power m; the overall order is the sum m + n. For rate = k[NO]²[H₂] the orders are 2 in NO, 1 in H₂, 3 overall (format keyed CP 2012 Q1(c)).
3. Zero order means “rate of change of [A] is independent from [A]” (ms bank §7); a fractional specimen in the bank is rate = k[A][B]^(3/2), whose k-unit line reads mol⁻¹·⁵ dm⁴·⁵ s⁻¹ (ms bank §7).
4. “Initial rate” is the rate at the start of the reaction (t = 0), i.e. the slope of the tangent at t = 0 on a progress curve (bank idiom: “slope of the tangent … at t = 10 s”, ms bank §7). About three quarters of candidates could not explain the term (CP 2014 Q1(b)).
5. The method of initial rates changes one reactant’s starting concentration at a time, keeps the others — and the temperature — constant, and measures the initial rate of each run; fair tests need the total volume of the reaction mixtures kept the same (CP 2014 Paper 1B context).
6. Doubling [A] at fixed [B] quadruples the initial rate ⇒ m = 2; doubling [B] does nothing ⇒ n = 0; if a candidate writes “initial rate = k[A]²”, the marker has seen the recorded wrong format (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).
7. The rate constant k changes only when the temperature changes; adding a catalyst, more surface area or more concentrated solutions change the rate but not this k. Confusing k with the equilibrium constant K is a recorded error (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)).
8. Units of k: order 0 → mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹; order 1 → s⁻¹; order 2 → mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ (also printed dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹); order 3 → mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹.
9. For the synthetic 2012-format table (0.010 → 0.020 mol dm⁻³ NO, rate 1.2 × 10⁻³ → 4.8 × 10⁻³): the deduction line is “2^m = 4, so m = 2”, the orders read 2 in NO, 1 in H₂, 3 overall, and k = 1.2 × 10⁻³ ÷ (0.010)²(0.010) = 1.2 × 10³ mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹.
10. In a multi-step mechanism the overall rate is controlled by the slowest step (rate-determining step) — a one-line idea with no curly-arrow mechanism expected; the orders still come from experimental data first (definition statement; RDS not bank-quoted, see CP-silence log).
11. The half-life formula t½ = 0.693/k is not expected in this topic (NTF-2018 II-B XIII (b)); concentration-vs-time “calculation + instrumentation details” likewise sit under Topic IX’s cap (NTF-2018 IX notes).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Rate Equation

Order vs balancing coefficient

2NO + 2H₂ → … happens to have orders 2 and 1 (CP 2012 Q1(c)) — but the order of “rate = k[A][B]^(3/2)” was measured (ms bank §7). Writing powers from the equation is the single most common illegitimate shortcut on this strand.

“rate = …” vs “initial rate = …”

The rate equation is a law, headed rate. “initial rate = k[NO]²[H₂]” and “rate equation = k[NO][H₂]” were both collected in 2012 (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii)) — the format sentence and the orders each cost marks.

Rate constant k vs equilibrium constant K

k: rate per unit concentration-power, temperature-dependent, kinetics. K (or K_c): ratio of equilibrium concentrations, thermodynamics, temperature-dependent only too — but the two have never met in one correct sentence (CP 2012 Q1(c)(iii) confusion). If your k has units of “nothing” at order 2, you borrowed K’s habit.

Changing k vs changing rate

More concentrated ⇒ bigger rate, same k. Catalyst ⇒ bigger rate by a different route, this k statement stays. Heat ⇒ k itself changes (log k vs 1/T, Engine B). Only temperature edits k in this syllabus.

Zero order vs “no reaction”

Zero order in B does not mean B is inert — it means the measured rate ignores [B] (“independent from [A]” test, ms bank §7). B can be consumed stoichiometrically while contributing nothing to the rate law.

Deduction vs announcement

“m = 2” with no ratio line was flagged: candidates “did not show a correct deduction” (CP 2012 Q1(c)(ii)). One visible line (×2 conc → ×4 rate ⇒ 2²) converts an assertion into a mark.

Value of k vs units of k

The number can be perfect and the mark still lost: “incorrect units were commonly presented after calculation” (CP 2012) and “only about one third … with correct unit” (CP 2016 Q1(c)). Derive the units in one line every time.

Units → order (the two-way street)

Units are evidence, not decoration: a k whose unit is s⁻¹ forces rate = k[N₂O₅]1 — “Numerical value of a can be obtained with reference to the unit of the given rate constant”, reached by only about half (CP 2024 Q1(c)(ii)(1)). The reverse direction is rarer still: the 3/2-order k₂ unit (mol⁻¹·⁵ dm⁴·⁵ s⁻¹ style) was correct for “about 1/3” (CP 2021 Q1(a)(iii)). Derive both ways; the order and the unit must agree before you hand in.

“In excess” vs “zero order”

When H⁺(aq) floods the thiosulphate run, its real order does not become zero — its concentration barely moves, so k·[H⁺]b hides inside k′: the paid line is “[H⁺(aq)] changes negligibly… k[H+(aq)] can be regarded as a constant” (deck Expected). “Many wrongly thought that the reason was the order of reaction respect to H+ being zero” (CP 2022 Q1(c)(ii)) — about three quarters of the cohort fell for it; folding a constant is algebra, not kinetics.

XIII (c)

Activation energy — defined

Eₐ = the minimum energy colliding particles need for reaction to occur

The 2014 report is blunt about the definition mark: candidates “were not able to state that activation energy is the minimum energy for a reaction to start” (CP 2014 Q1). “Minimum energy” must appear — it is the whole mark.

Eₐ is a barrier height on the energy profile (card 1) and a threshold on the Maxwell–Boltzmann plot (card 2). Both pictures read the same quantity; the words never change: minimum energy required for reaction.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • State the definition verbatim, with “minimum energy”.
  • Locate Eₐ on a profile and on an MB distribution with Eₐ marked.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Energy needed for anything to happen” loses to “for reaction to occur/start” — the particles must react, not merely move.
  • Eₐ is positive for both forward and reverse reactions; do not attach a minus sign to a barrier.
XIII (c)

Energy profiles & the same-sketch rule

$$\Delta H = H(\text{products}) - H(\text{reactants})$$

and Eₐ(reverse) = Eₐ(forward) − ΔH

Draw axes “energy” (y) against “progress of reaction” (x); curve from reactant level over the hump to products; ΔH between the levels, Eₐ from reactant level to the top. Bank arithmetic: for HCOOH(l) ⇌ CO(g) + H₂O(l) with ΔH = +28.5 kJ mol⁻¹ and forward Eₐ = 77.7, the mark scheme computes “Ea(reverse) = 77.7 − 28.5 = 49.2 kJ mol⁻¹” (ms bank §8). A catalysed curve peaks lower but starts and ends at the same levels — ΔH untouched. The 2015 drawing command failed widely: “Many candidates were not able to draw the energy profiles in the same sketch” — one set of axes, both humps, both labelled (CP 2015 Q1(b)).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Sketch exo- and endothermic profiles with Eₐ and ΔH correctly placed.
  • Add the catalysed route to the same sketch and read Eₐ(reverse) from it.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The hump’s top is the transition state — measure Eₐ to the top, not to the products.
  • Two separate sketches where one is demanded = the 2015 zero. Keep ΔH arrow between the flat levels, never to the hump.
XIII (c)

Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution — axes & area

x-axis: molecular kinetic energy  ·  y-axis: number of molecules per unit energy

Axis discipline is marked wording: “The x-axis should be molecular kinetic energy. Some candidates simply put down energy, which is not precise enough” (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i)) — kinetic, not potential (that belongs to the profile, card 1). The curve starts at the origin, peaks, then tails towards the axis without touching it (no upper limit to molecular energy). The area under the curve = the total fraction of molecules (all of them) — the mark-scheme fact three quarters missed in 2016 (CP 2016 Q1(a)). Shade right of Eₐ to answer “how many molecules can react”: that area is the fraction with KE ≥ Eₐ.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Sketch the distribution with correct, precise axis labels.
  • State what a shaded area to the right of Eₐ represents — and what the whole area represents.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Whole area never changes with temperature — the sample’s molecule count is fixed; only its distribution spreads (CP 2016 Q1(a) area rule).
  • y-axis may read “number (or fraction) of molecules” — but energy alone is a rejected x-label (CP 2013).
XIII (c)

Temperature effect via the curve

T↑ ⇒ curve flattens, peak shifts right ⇒ area beyond Eₐ grows

The bank’s three-part temperature engine: “An increase in temperature… will increase the average kinetic energy of the molecules”, “larger portion of molecules will have kinetic energy greater than Eₐ”, so “more effective collisions per unit time” (ms bank §7). The energy-distribution argument dominates the collision-frequency one — the rate rises far faster than collision counts alone. Cooling reads backwards: “Lowering the temperature makes the reaction proceed at a slower rate” (ms bank §10).

Graph fidelity is a mark: “Some candidates just stated that the collision frequency of the reactant molecules would increase with temperature, but without making reference to the graph provided” (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i)). Point at the shaded area; say it grows.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Draw the higher-T curve on the same axes and shade the enlarged Eₐ-exceeding area.
  • Write the full causal chain: T → average KE → fraction ≥ Eₐ → effective collisions → rate.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not raise the peak at higher T — the curve flattens; area conservation forces a lower peak.
  • Never claim “more molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ because collisions increase” — same subject, wrong verb order; fraction first, collisions consequence.
XIII (c)

Arrhenius — log k against 1/T

$$\log k = \text{constant} - \frac{E_a}{2.3RT}$$

straight line on log k vs 1/T; slope = −Eₐ/(2.3R)

The Guide prints the Arrhenius equation exactly as above (Part I wording; ms bank §7). Plot log k (y) against 1/T (x): straight line, downward slope; multiply slope by −2.3R to recover Eₐ. Two-point form seen in the bank: $\log\frac{k_2}{k_1} = -\frac{E_a}{2.3R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right)$.

Bank specimen computation: “Ea = 1.73 × 10³ × 8.31 × 2.3 = 33.1 kJ mol⁻¹” (ms bank §7) — slope magnitude × 2.3R, then J→kJ.

Recorded graph crimes in one sentence: “confusing ‘log k’ with ‘ln k’ …, writing ‘ln k’ as ‘log k’ …, and mis-interpreting the unit of the x-axis as ‘/10⁻³ K⁻¹’ instead of ‘10⁻³ K⁻¹’” (CP 2013 Q1(a)(ii)). If the axis says $10^{-3}\,\text{K}^{-1}$, tick “2” means 1/T = 2 × 10⁻³.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Determine Eₐ “with a given set of data” from a log k vs 1/T graph (Part I wording).
  • Predict k at a new temperature using the two-point form.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Very few candidates were able to correctly calculate the activation energy of the reaction” (CP 2013 Q1(a)(ii)) — practise slope extraction with the 10³ factor until it is reflex.
  • Report Eₐ in kJ mol⁻¹ after the ×2.3R multiplication; the unit mark is live (“the unit must be correct”, ms bank general).
XIII (d)

The catalyst sentence (the only one)

“A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.”

That exact string is the mark scheme’s own (ms bank §7). XIII (d)

Catalysts in reversible reactions

“A catalyst will increase the rate of forward reaction and that of backward reaction to the same extent.”

The two bank locks: forward and backward rates rise to the same extent, and “A catalyst has no effect on the equilibrium position” (ms bank §7). Mechanically: one alternative route serves both directions — the same lower barrier is shared, so ΔH (the gap between levels) is untouched.

Paper 1B caught the shallow version: candidates “only stated that adding a suitable catalyst would not increase the number of moles of SO₃(g) obtained without giving a proper explanation” (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q10) — the explanation is “same extent both ways, position unmoved, equilibrium sooner”.

Why industry still pays for it: “It takes a long time for the equilibrium to be attained” and the catalyst “can increase the total amount of NH₃(g) produced per unit time” (ms bank §10). Faster arrival, identical arrival point.

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Justify industrial catalyst use in “per unit time” language, never “more yield”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The 2013 wrong sentence again: conc. H₂SO₄ “provides more H⁺(aq)” as catalysis reasoning — catalysts are not reagent dilution (CP 2013 Q1(b)(ii)).
  • K_c has no catalyst column — no shift, no K change; “reaches equilibrium faster” is the only bonus.

Characteristics & named industrial catalysts

Fe (finely divided) · Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ · V₂O₅ · enzymes

“Lowers Eₐ” vs “alternative pathway with lower Eₐ”

The first is the recorded wrong phrase (CP 2013 Q1(b)(i)); the second is the bank string (ms bank §7). One word of difference, one mark of difference.

Kinetic energy (MB) vs potential energy (profile)

Two graphs, two y-axes, one word between right and wrong: the MB x-axis is molecular kinetic energy (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i); digest §5.1 “NOT potential energy”); the profile y-axis is (potential) energy of the chemicals. Labelling the distribution “potential energy” contradicts its own definition.

Bigger area vs same area, different shape

Heating does not create molecules; the whole area stands (CP 2016 Q1(a)) while the shaded portion beyond Eₐ grows. Writing “the area increases with temperature” fails the rule the first clause celebrates.

Collision frequency vs fraction above Eₐ

Both rise with temperature, but the rate story the MS pays for is the fraction above Eₐ (bank: “larger portion of molecules will have kinetic energy greater than Eₐ”). Stopping at “collisions increase, without making reference to the graph” was the 2013 partial-credit ceiling (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i)).

log k vs ln k (and 10⁻³ vs ÷10⁻³)

The printed equation uses log and the divisor 2.3R — swapping to ln drops the 2.3 and shrinks your Eₐ by that factor. Misreading the axis scale (as ‘/10⁻³ K⁻¹’) was named explicitly (CP 2013 Q1(a)(ii)). Both mistakes survive into Eₐ — carry units, carry 2.3.

Catalyst faster vs catalyst more

“Faster equilibrium” is true; “more SO₃ at equilibrium” is not — the 2016 Paper 1B candidate who said the moles stay the same but could not say why only half-answered (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q10). Rate verbs only; never touch yield verbs.

Hot is fast vs hot suits enzymes

For Fe or Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃, raising T feeds k. For enzymes — the strand’s own example (alcoholic drinks) — excess heat denatures the catalyst itself; “more than half of the candidates neglected” it (CP 2014 Q1). Name the catalyst, then choose the temperature argument.

Poisoned catalyst vs poisoned equilibrium

Poisoning deactivates the catalyst (“impurities … may poison the catalyst”, ms bank §7): the route it offered slows or vanishes, arrival at equilibrium is delayed — the equilibrium position and K are chemically unharmed. Rate damage, not yield damage.

Poisoned by impurities vs poisoned by feed

The 2022 paper sharpened the poisoning word: “about 1/3” could state that the catalyst “can be poisoned by impurities after prolonged use” after feedstock purification was asked, and “Some wrongly mentioned that the catalyst would be poisoned by the reactants” (CP 2022 Q1(a)(ii)(2)). Purify the feed to protect the bed — poisons, not reactants, do the killing.

“Lower Eₐ” — whose Eₐ?

2013’s phrase error has a 2021 grandchild: “Some candidates wrongly suggested that the catalyst would lower the activation energy of the uncatalysed pathway” (CP 2021 Q1(b)(iv)(2)). The uncatalysed hump is sacred — the catalyst brings a new road with its own smaller hump (deck Expected: “add catalyst : lower activation energy in an alternative pathway”). On the MB curve the same rule bites: one curve, a lower-Eₐ marker — “Wrongly use 2 curves to explain” was faulted in the same sentence.

Profile vs MB — wrong graph, wrong axis

Asked for the Haber energy profile: “Some candidates wrongly labelled the horizontal axis as ‘time’”, and some “wrongly drew the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve” (CP 2023 Q1(b)(ii)). Asked for the labels in 2024: x “reaction coordination”/“time” and y “kinetic energy”/“energy level” were the collected faults (CP 2024 Q1(b)(i)). Chloroalkali II — membrane cell vs diaphragm cell

$$\text{cathode: } 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{OH}^-\text{(aq)}$$

Methanol via syngas — methane’s upgrade

$$\text{CO(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{OH(g)}$$

Feedstock route: natural gas → “steam reforming of natural gas (methane)” gives syngas, the mixture of the “two major constituent gases” CO and H₂ (ms bank §10; CP 2016 Q1(a)); the shift step CO(g) + H₂O(g) → H₂(g) + CO₂(g) tunes the H₂ share (ms bank §10). 1. Haber: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g); the nitrogen comes from fractional distillation of liquefied air (CP 2016 Q1(b)) and the hydrogen from steam reforming of natural gas (methane) (ms bank §10).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Industrial Processes

High yield vs fast (the compromise)

The exothermic forward reaction is most generous cold — and intolerably slow: “It takes a long time for the equilibrium to be attained” (ms bank §10). The plant temperature is the truce, not the optimum of either side. Saying “450 °C gives a good yield” alone forgets which lever it is.

Catalyst vs yield booster

Iron does not make more ammonia per pass — same-extent both ways, no shift (ms bank §7). Its payroll is total amount per unit time (ms bank §10). The 2012 “platinum” answer and the 2016 “more SO₃” claim are the same error wearing different plants.

Recycling vs equilibrium shifting

Returned gases raise utilisation and total output, not the per-pass equilibrium yield — “NOT accept ‘recycling increases yield’” (digest §21.1). The 2012 “removal of product mixture vs removal of product (ammonia)” confusion shows what happens when separation and shift get welded (CP 2012 Q1(a)(iv)).

“High pressure is expensive” vs the paid sentence

Cost of what: “reaction chamber, pipelines” construction, and safety — “The lower pressure is dictated by limits of mechanical design / safety concerns” (ms bank §10). Bare “expensive” was named insufficient (CP 2012 Q1(a)(iii)).

Brine vs seawater vs “sodium chloride solution”

All three are chlorides; only “brine / concentrated sodium chloride solution” is the industry’s word (ms bank §6; CP 2013 Q1(c)(i)). Dilute feed wastes energy; seawater is a soup of competing ions — the report says the wording is the mark.

Products vs feedstock (NaCl)

Chloroalkali manufactures Cl₂, H₂, NaOH; sodium chloride walks in — “wrongly suggested sodium chloride as one of the chemicals manufactured” (CP 2014 Q1).

Mercury cell vs membrane cell cathode output

Mercury cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (amalgam), alkali made downstream in a separate reactor (ms bank §6). Membrane/diaphragm cathode: water discharges → H₂ + OH⁻, alkali forms in the compartment (digest §7.6/§21.4). Swap the half-equations and the whole cell story contradicts itself — contradictory answers score zero (ms bank general).

Methanol use vs methanol abuse

Industry uses methanol as a chemical feedstock (ethanoic acid, larger carbon numbers) (ms bank §10; CP 2012 Q1(b)(i)). It is toxic — “making wine / food additive” was collected as wrong (CP 2012 Q1(b)(i)); “as a fuel” was not the answer the question wanted.

Feedstock use vs feedstock equation

The methanol→ethanoic acid link the bank pays for was rewritten as an equation ask in 2018: “a quarter of the candidates were able to write a chemical equation for the industrial production of ethanoic acid from methanol” (deck Expected: CH₃OH + CO → CH₃CO₂H) (CP 2018 Q1(b)(iii)). Learn the carbonylation as a 100%-atom-economy equation, not a use-case sentence.

“Expensive” vs cost-effectiveness arithmetic

Bare cost talk dies twice in Section A. Haber 2012 wanted chambers and pipelines named (CP 2012 Q1(a)(iii)); the Contact companion now has its own records: 2017 — “A very small number of the candidates were able to suggest that it is not cost effective for increasing the pressure of the reaction system as the conversion percentage will not increase to a high extent” (CP 2017 Q1(b)(ii)(2)); 2025 — fewer than half gave both reasons against ~200 atm: “Some candidates did not recognise that the yield is already very high at atmospheric pressure and hence it is not cost effective to use a high pressure of 200 atm” (CP 2025 Q1(b)(iv)(2)), and an exemplar fault reads “Not mentioning why it is expensive”. The argument is two-part: gain is tiny, cost is real.

XIII (f)

Atom economy — the calculation

$$\text{Atom economy} = \frac{M_r(\text{desired})}{\sum M_r(\text{reactants})} \times 100\%$$

Bank specimen chains: “Atom economy = 58/76 = 76.32%” — the propylene-oxide-era numbers (ms bank §10; propylene oxide formula mass named in CP 2013 Q1(c)(ii)) — and for CO + 2H₂ → CH₃OH, “atom economy of the reaction is 100%”: every atom ends up in methanol (ms bank §10). Addition-type routes (ethene hydration, NH₃ + HNO₃) score 100%; routes expelling a second product never do.

Method: balance, take the desired species Mᵣ over the total Mᵣ of reactants, ×100%, round sensibly — “paid no attention to significant figures and gave answers with a long string of numerals” (CP 2013 Q1(c)(ii)). Its honesty limit is bank-stated: “The calculation of atom economy is based on a 100% completed reaction. Most reactions do not go to completion” (ms bank §10); solvents, catalysts and energy are invisible to it (digest §19.4 “stoichiometry only”).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Compute atom economy from any balanced equation, chain shown.
  • Recognise which route shapes (addition vs substitution-with-coproduct) give 100%.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Only the desired product goes on top — co-products, even useful ones, count as denominator unless the question says otherwise.
  • A 100%-atom-economy answer is not “this process is green” — hazards and energy can still veto it (digest §19.4 “NOT green if…”).
XIII (f)

Atom economy vs percentage yield

atom economy = desired Mᵣ ÷ Σ reactant Mᵣ
yield % = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%

One is a blueprint property of the equation (always computable before the lab exists); the other is an experiment report (needs real mass). The record: “Many candidates were unable to differentiate between the terms ‘atom economy’ and ‘reaction yield’” (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iv)).

A route can be 100% atom-economic and run at 15% yield (equilibrium-limited) — waste arises twice: bad blueprint and bad execution. Green sentences should therefore name which twin you are discussing: “higher atom economy” / “produces less waste” are bank phrases (ms bank §10).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • State each definition with its formula and its data needs.
  • Interpret a two-method comparison: AE favours method X, yield/conditions favour method Y — say both.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never define atom economy as “how much product you get” — that is yield, and the confusion is a recorded zero (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iv)).

  • “Green” is not an adjective for anything plant-shaped — the deforestation counterweight is bank-supplied; using both sides is what a full-mark paragraph looks like.
XIII (f)

Safer chemicals & solvents

NOT green if: highly toxic HCN · toxic CH₃OH · highly corrosive conc. H₂SO₄

The digest’s red list, from mark-scheme practice: a synthesis is “not green” if it depends on “highly toxic HCN / toxic CH₃OH / highly corrosive H₂SO₄” (digest §11.5/§19.4) — atom economy cannot launder a hazardous reagent. Bank confirmation: “Less hazardous chemicals are used” (ms bank §10); “Methanol is toxic” (ms bank §10).

Solvent and process thinking follows the same clause — the STSE definition targets the use or generation of hazardous substances across design, manufacture, application. A route needing corrosive acid in excess and generating sulphate waste loses on reagent hazard and waste mass (compare the propene + H₂O₂ specimen whose chain prints as 58/76 = 76.32% (ms bank §10)); deciding “which named method is greener” was exactly the 2013 task (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iii)).

Storage and transport close the loop: the Guide’s values list demands valuing “safe transport and storage of hazardous substances such as ammonia, acetic acid, hydrogen, chlorine and sodium hydroxide” (Part I values).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Screen a synthesis for toxic/corrosive dependencies and state them as disqualifiers.
  • Compare two methods on hazard + waste, referencing the given information.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Name the substance and the property (“corrosive”, “toxic”) — “dangerous chemicals” alone is too loose for the bank’s phrasing.
XIII (f)

Effluent & waste-gas treatment

scrubbers · precipitators · filtration · catalytic converters · fuel switching

The bank’s control shelf, nearly verbatim: “Install scrubbers in power plant” (SO₂), “Use electrostatic precipitator” / “Remove dust by mechanical filtration” (particulates), “Initial catalytic converters in car” + “Use unleaded petrol” (NOₓ/CO — unleaded because lead poisons the converter catalyst), “Replace diesel with LPG”, “Ultra Low Sulphur Diesel (ULSD)” (ms bank §10). VOCs and effluent from processes like optical-brightener manufacture are the Guide’s STSE example of “not environmentally friendly” output needing management (Part I STSE).

Incineration, carefully: the analytical bank keys dioxin to “Incineration … Combustion of materials containing chlorine / PVC … Emission from incinerators”, and “Dioxin is carcinogenic / can cause cancer” (ms bank §11); candidates wrongly linked airborne dioxin “to the burning of fossil fuels, and/or to chemicals released from furniture” (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context). Incinerating chlorine-containing waste can create a greener-looking problem — a favourite exam reversal. Mercury from chloroalkali cells sits in the same argument (Engine C; CP 2015 Q1(c)).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Match each pollutant class (SO₂, NOₓ, CO, particulates, dioxin) to its bank control measure.
  • Explain why “treat the effluent” is end-of-pipe, while green chemistry prefers preventing it.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Scrubber” spelling and purpose (acid gas neutralised by alkaline slurry/water) both matter; the 2012 report’s general comments punished invented instrument names — name the device or name the chemistry, never both vaguely (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context).
XIII (f)

Design for degradation (PHB)

“PHB is biodegradable.” · “PHB undergoes hydrolysis in the presence of acids / alkalis.”

Both lines are bank-quoted (ms bank §10): PHB — a microbial polyester — hydrolyses its ester links under acidic or alkaline conditions, which is exactly why it biodegrades. The same reasoning pattern appears for PLA: “There are many ester groups … broken down in acidic / alkaline / bacterial conditions” (ms bank §5), and for condensation polymers generally: “Small molecules (e.g. H₂O) would be eliminated in the reaction” (ms bank §10).

Green audit placement: “Raw material is biomass” (ms bank §10) + biodegradable product = two of the evaluation ticks that make a fermentation-linked plastic greener than a polyalkene that persists as litter.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Explain biodegradability via hydrolysable linkages (esters) in acids/alkalis.
  • Pair “biomass feedstock” with “degradable product” in an evaluation.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Polymer structural detail lives in Topics V/XI — here the green property sentence is enough; do not drift into mechanism (not expected, XI note).
XIII (f)

Evaluating a process the HKEAA way

given data → green principle → verdict on THIS process

Three failures on record define the genre. 2012: “just raised a few general principles … without making reference to the methanol production technology as required” (CP 2012 Q1(b)(iii)). 2013: “unable to decide, with reference to the given information, whether Method 1 or Method 2 is greener”; wrong-material arguments (economic/political/financial/societal) credited nothing (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iii)). 2015: no reasons offered “for considering the fermentation route as a green process” (CP 2015 Q1(b)).

The winning template: pick a green principle (atom economy, waste, hazard, feedstock renewability, degradation, energy), attach it to a fact in the stem (an equation, a reagent, a condition), and land an explicit verdict. The bank’s specimen verdicts: “It has a higher atom economy”, “produces less waste”, “Less hazardous chemicals are used” (ms bank §10).

Extended green discussions carry a communication mark (ms bank §10) — organised paragraphs with each claim tied to data, not bullet salad.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Write data→principle→verdict paragraphs to a fixed rhythm.
  • Protect the communication mark: order, connectives, no contradiction (contradictory answers score zero, ms bank general).
💡 Examiner Tips

The Green Audit — five gates every process must walk

Any evaluation question — methanol, epoxides, ethanol, acetic acid — is the same walk. Each gate carries its bank sentence; a full-mark answer passes ≥3 gates with the stem’s data attached.

Gate 1 · Atom economy

$${\text{desired } M_r \over \sum \text{reactant } M_r}$$

“atom economy of the reaction is 100%” / “= 58/76 = 76.32%” (ms bank §10). Addition routes pass; co-product routes pay.

Gate 2 · Real waste (yield)

% yield + unreacted feed + separation losses: “Most reactions do not go to completion” (ms bank §10). Recycle lines (Haber) claw back at this gate only.

Gate 3 · Feedstock

“Raw material is biomass” / “Corn starch is a renewable resource” vs food-supply & deforestation counterweights (ms bank §10). Depleting petrocarbon loses points, cheap energy wins some back.

Gate 4 · Hazard & energy

“Less hazardous chemicals are used”; veto list: toxic HCN, toxic CH₃OH, corrosive H₂SO₄ (ms bank §10; digest §19.4). Mild-temperature routes (fermentation, enzymes) bill less energy; high T/P plants bill more.

Gate 5 · End of life

“PHB is biodegradable” via ester hydrolysis (ms bank §10); persistent polyalkenes litter; incineration of chlorinated waste risks dioxin (ms bank §11).

Verdict strip

Say which way: “It has a higher atom economy … produces less waste … less hazardous chemicals are used → greener” — bank grammar (ms bank §10). Half-answers that list principles without verdicts were the 2012/2013 failure pattern.

Life-cycle strip: raw material → synthesis → product use → disposal/reuse. Green chemistry is the only lens in this topic that reads the whole strip — which is exactly why a single 100% atom-economy number never settles an argument by itself.

Multi-blank Quiz — Green Chemistry

1. Green chemistry “reduces or eliminates the use or generation of hazardous substances in the design, manufacture and application of chemical products” (Part I STSE), keeping industry’s benefits at an “acceptable cost” without damaging the environment.
2. Atom economy = $M_r$ of desired product ÷ Σ$M_r$ of all reactants × 100%; the bank’s printed chains are “58/76 = 76.32%” and “atom economy of the reaction is 100%” (ms bank §10).
3. CO + 2H₂ → CH₃OH scores 100% because it is an addition-type route with no co-product, while fermentation C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ gives atom economy 92/180 = 51.11% (study arithmetic by the bank’s chain method) — the CO₂ is the denominator’s tax.
4. The terms that must not blur: “atom economy” (from the equation) vs “reaction yield” (actual ÷ theoretical) — differentiating them was a recorded failure (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iv)), and atom economy “is based on a 100% completed reaction, most reactions do not go to completion” (ms bank §10).
5. Feedstock tensions: corn starch “is a renewable resource”, yet its use “may reduce the food supply” and “may lead to deforestation” (ms bank §10) — in 2016 only about a quarter of candidates gave the starch-over-sucrose green reason (CP 2016 Q1(c)).
6. The hazard vetoes, digest-worded: a synthesis is not green if it uses highly toxic HCN, toxic CH₃OH, or highly corrosive H₂SO₄ (digest §19.4); “Less hazardous chemicals are used” is itself a bank credit line (ms bank §10).
7. Control measures, matched to the bank: SO₂ → scrubbers in power plant / ULSD fuel; particulates → electrostatic precipitator / mechanical filtration; car NOₓ & CO → catalytic converters (with unleaded petrol); diesel → replace with LPG (ms bank §10).
8. Dioxin discipline: its source string is “incineration … combustion of materials containing chlorine / PVC”, and it “is carcinogenic” (ms bank §11); blaming it on fossil-fuel burning or furniture emissions was the 2012 recorded wrong link (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context).
9. Degradation design: “PHB is biodegradable” because it “undergoes hydrolysis in the presence of acids / alkalis” (ms bank §10) — the same ester-link logic that breaks down PLA (ms bank §5).
10. Evaluation grammar: bind every claim to the given information; economics, politics and “society” are not green chemistry (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iii)); general principles without the named technology scored the 2012 zero (CP 2012 Q1(b)(iii)); long discussions may earn a communication mark (ms bank §10).
11. What atom economy cannot see: yield, solvent and energy (digest §19.4 “stoichiometry only”) — so “100% atom economy ⇒ green” is the reasoning error the digest pre-empts; and the acetic-acid exemplar must stay example-level — process details not expected (NTF-2018 II-B XIII (f)).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Green Chemistry

Atom economy vs percentage yield

Blueprint vs experiment — “differentiate between the terms” was failed outright in 2013 (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iv)). A 100%-AE route at 20% yield still wastes 4/5 of its feed and dilutes its greenness; two different sentences, two different numbers.

Green chemistry vs green economics

“Cheaper / creates jobs / politically popular” was argued instead of green chemistry and credited as irrelevant (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iii)). Cost belongs in the “social–economic” columns of a process discussion — different marks, different paragraph.

Renewable vs automatically green

Corn starch is renewable and food-competing, land-driving — both bank lines belong in one answer (ms bank §10). A feedstock tick at Gate 3 does not veto scrutiny at Gates 1–2 and 4.

High atom economy vs clean process

Stoichiometry-only means solvents, toxic catalysts and energy bills are invisible to the number (digest §19.4). The HCN/CH₃OH/H₂SO₄ veto list exists precisely to catch 100%-AE-with-hazard routes.

Incineration as disposal vs dioxin factory

Burning waste that contains chlorine (PVC) links to “Emission from incinerators” and carcinogenic dioxin (ms bank §11); “burn it” is not the green exit — the green move is removing chlorine from the input or capturing the effluent (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context).

Principles recited vs process evaluated

The genre-failure quote again: “general principles … without making reference to the methanol production technology” (CP 2012 Q1(b)(iii)). Principle + stem-fact + verdict, every paragraph, or you are writing a different exam.

Recycling waste vs 100% atom economy

Recycling (Haber loop) improves utilisation — the blueprint’s atom economy is unchanged by a pipe that returns unreacted gas. Recycling is a Gate-2 repair for a route that failed Gate 1 honestly; do not claim the equation “became” 100%.

Significant figures vs calculator vomit

“Paid no attention to significant figures and gave answers with a long string of numerals” (CP 2013 Q1(c)(ii)). Report atom economy like the MS does — “76.32%” — and stop; a 6-decimal string reads as uncertainty about your own method.

Hazard class vs vague alarm

“Harmful” is not a hazard class. For methanol: “Some candidates just stated: methanol is harmful without giving an explicit description of the hazard” (CP 2021 Q1(c)(i)); for carbon monoxide: “Some mentioned that CO is harmful instead of toxic” (CP 2022 Q1(a)(i)(2)). The decks key toxic / corrosive / flammable — 2023’s vitamin-C mark scheme writes “Use of toxic reactant: Cl2” and corrosive NaOH / conc. H₂SO₄ (CP 2023 Q1(a)(i)(2)); and 2020 faulted a “too general” cell advantage until it named the poison: “Better mention Hg is toxic” (CP 2020 Q1(a)(i)).

Greener claim vs comparative sense

A “greener” verdict without the comparator earns the deck’s shorthand interrogation: 2025’s exemplar notes read “A comparative sense?” and “Why less??” (CP 2025 Q1(a)(iii)); 2024’s fault line was “Not mention that less chemical wastes are produced in less steps” (CP 2024 Q1(b)(v)(1)). Sentence template: “Method X is greener than Method Y because its atom economy / waste / hazard is higher / less / fewer than Y’s.”

📈 The Maxwell–Boltzmann rules list (8) — the curve is Topic XIII property
  1. x-axis: molecular kinetic energy. “Energy” alone is “not precise enough”; potential energy is the profile graph’s variable (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i); digest §5.1).
  2. y-axis: number (or fraction) of molecules per unit energy; the curve starts at the origin — no molecule has exactly zero kinetic energy.
  3. The tail never touches the x-axis: there is no ceiling on molecular energy.
  4. The whole area = the total fraction of molecules — identical at every temperature; three quarters missed the statement in 2016 (CP 2016 Q1(a)).
  5. Heating: peak shifts right and down, curve flattens and widens — area conservation forces the drop (ms bank §7 chain).
  6. The shaded area beyond Eₐ is the fraction with KE ≥ Eₐ; it grows steeply with temperature — “larger portion of molecules will have kinetic energy greater than Eₐ” (ms bank §7).
  7. Catalyst on this graph: mark a lower Eₐ on the same curve — the distribution is not redrawn (digest §5.1).
  8. Every temperature explanation must point at the graph — “without making reference to the graph provided” capped 2013 marks (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i)).

🧠 Misconceptions corrected by evidence (14) — each one CP- or bank-sourced
  • “Balancing numbers are orders.” The NO/H₂ law matched coefficients by luck; the 3/2-order specimen could not come from any small integer. (CP 2012 Q1(c); ms bank §7)
  • “More concentrated ⇒ bigger k.” Concentration moves the [ ] terms; k only answers to temperature. (Engine A card 5; CP 2012 k/K record)
  • “A catalyst lowers the original barrier.” It opens a new road with its own, lower barrier — “instead of” was the recorded wrong phrase. (CP 2013 Q1(b)(i))
  • “A catalyst shifts the equilibrium.” Same extent both directions; position and K_c unmoved. (ms bank §7; CP 2016 Paper 1B Q10)
  • “Heating enlarges the MB area.” The sample is the same molecules; the distribution spreads. (CP 2016 Q1(a))
  • “The collision-frequency sentence is the temperature explanation.” It is a half-sentence without the fraction-above-Eₐ clause and the graph reference. (CP 2013 Q1(a)(i); ms bank §7)
  • “Condensing ammonia pulls the equilibrium.” In the workup it is a separation step — the 2012 report punished the shift borrowing. (CP 2012 Q1(a)(iv); digest §21.1)
  • “Seawater feeds the chloroalkali plant.” Brine — concentrated sodium chloride solution — does; wording was graded. (CP 2013 Q1(c)(i))
  • “Salt is one of their products.” NaCl enters; Cl₂, H₂, NaOH leave. (CP 2014 Q1)
  • “Atom economy and yield are cousins.” They are strangers with different data requirements — the differentiation was failed en masse. (CP 2013 Q1(c)(iv))
  • “Renewable feedstock makes a process green.” The bank itself pairs “renewable resource” with food-supply and deforestation costs. (ms bank §10)
  • “Incineration finishes the waste story.” Chlorine-containing waste burnt can emit dioxin — “Emission from incinerators”, carcinogenic. (ms bank §11; CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context)
  • “An excess reactant is zero-order.” A flood of H⁺(aq) hides a real order inside k′ — the paid reasoning is “[H⁺(aq)] changes negligibly… k[H+(aq)] can be regarded as a constant”; assuming b = 0 was the recorded failure. (CP 2022 Q1(c)(ii))
  • “Units of k are an afterthought.” They are evidence: s⁻¹ forces a = 1 (about half of 2024 saw it — “Numerical value of a can be obtained with reference to the unit of the given rate constant”), and the 3/2-order unit was correct for only about a third in 2021.
