Topic III (a) Occurrence & why we extract

Metals in nature: free state, combined forms and the challenge of extraction

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Only a few very unreactive metals (gold, platinum) occur in the free state; most metals occur in combined forms — oxides, carbonates and sulphides mined as ores.
  • Extraction is needed because a metal locked in a compound is not usable — and extraction of a metal = reduction of its ore (gain of electrons by the metal ion: $\text{M}^{n+} + n\text{e}^- \to \text{M}$).
  • The ease of extraction tracks position in the reactivity series → explains the chronology: copper (ancient) → iron (later) → aluminium (only early 1800s).
  • Write word equations for extraction, e.g. copper(II) oxide + carbon → copper + carbon dioxide.
  • Metal ores are a finite resource → recycling metals conserves reserves; evaluate recycling from social, economic and environmental perspectives (energy saved vs mining pollution vs cost of collection).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Silver can be obtained by a physical method” is a recorded wrong answer — most silver ores need chemical extraction; only truly native metals are physically separated (CP 2014, Paper 1B Q4 context). The word reduction must appear when explaining why extraction is needed. Recycling answers must evaluate (two-sided: energy/reserve saving against collection cost and processing pollution), not just say “recycling is good”.

Topic III (a) Three extraction routes

Getting the metal: heating alone · heating the oxide with carbon · electrolysis of molten ore

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Just heating the oxide works for very unreactive metals whose oxides decompose on heating: $2\text{Ag}_2\text{O(s)} \to 4\text{Ag(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$ (mercury(II) oxide the classic demo).
  • Reduction with carbon for moderate metals (zinc down to copper): $2\text{CuO(s)} + \text{C(s)} \to 2\text{Cu(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$; carbon sits between aluminium and zinc in the series.
  • Electrolysis of the molten compound for very reactive metals (potassium → aluminium) — studied only as an illustration; electrolysis itself is Topic VII.
  • Method-choice rule: position in the reactivity series decides — the more reactive the metal, the more readily its atom loses electrons — and the more difficult it is to reduce its ion → more energy-intensive extraction.
  • Cost enters the decision: carbon is cheaper than aluminium, so iron is extracted with coke, not by aluminium reduction, at industrial scale.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Judge “can metal X reduce metal Y’s oxide?” by comparing X with Y — the metal in the oxide, not with a third metal the student memorised (CP 2013, Paper 1B context). Explain the carbon-versus-aluminium choice with “cost” (aluminium is more expensive than carbon) — recorded omission (CP 2013, Paper 1B context). Blast-furnace operations and equations belong to Topic XIII; here: “heating with carbon” only.

Topic III (b) Reactivity series & displacement

Reactions with oxygen/air, water and dilute acids → the reactivity series → displacement

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Compare sodium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron, lead, copper with oxygen/air, water (cold · steam) and dilute hydrochloric / sulphuric acid; write the word and symbol equations.
  • Build the series K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe Pb (H) Cu Ag Au from experimental evidence; reactivity = tendency of atoms to lose electrons and form positive ions.
  • Displacement: a more reactive metal reduces a less reactive metal’s aqueous ion: $\text{Zn(s)} + \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} \to \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}$ — with observations (blue fades, reddish-brown solid deposited, temperature rises).
  • Predict feasibility of metal + acid, metal + water, metal + metal-ion reactions; deduce an unknown order from given observation results.
  • Relate extraction method to series position (strand (a) back-link); use state symbols (s)(l)(g)(aq) correctly.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Iron displaces copper” scores nothing — name the ion: “iron displaces copper(II) ions” (CP 2012 Q5, Paper 1B context). Explanations want the mark-scheme chain “loses electrons more readily … and becomes …²⁺”. Magnesium’s burn is “with a bright light”; copper + dilute acid gives no hydrogen (copper is below hydrogen). Iron with acids/ions gives Fe²⁺ (pale green), never Fe³⁺ unless the question forces it (CP 2016, Paper 1B Q2 context).

Topic III (c) The mole toolkit

The mole, Avogadro’s constant, molar mass, formulae and reacting masses

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Mole: the amount of substance containing as many particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12; Avogadro’s constant $L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.
  • Convert freely: $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ and $N = n \times L$; molar mass in g mol⁻¹ is the formula mass with units attached.
  • A balanced equation gives the mole ratio of reactants and products — the gateway of every reacting-mass calculation.
  • Percentage by mass of an element in a compound: $\dfrac{\text{mass of element}}{\text{mass of compound}} \times 100\%$; work empirical formulae from composition by mass, then molecular formulae from molar mass ($\text{molecular} = (\text{empirical})_n$).
  • Limiting reagent: divide each reactant’s moles by its equation coefficient — the smallest quotient runs out first and caps the product.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Step marks are awarded per line of working — write the division chain the way the scheme does: “number of moles of P : number of moles of Cl = 0.226/31.0 : 0.774/35.5 = 1 : 3” (MS bank). Formula mass is unitless; only molar mass carries g mol⁻¹ — “the unit must be correct”. Many candidates still fail the empirical-formula arithmetic outright (CP 2015, Paper 1B Q3 context), and a balanced equation is always the first mark.

Topic III (d) Rusting & protection

Rusting of iron — conditions, indicators, and the seven ways to stop it

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide — reddish-brown, flaky and porous, so it does not protect the metal beneath (unlike aluminium’s oxide).
  • Essential conditions: water AND oxygen (air) together; speed rises with salt / other electrolytes (mobile ions), acids and temperature.
  • Rust indicator (K₃Fe(CN)₆ + phenolphthalein): blue with Fe²⁺(aq), pink with OH⁻(aq); describe both colours before and after.
  • Prevention: coating (paint/oil/plastic), galvanising (zinc — barrier + sacrificial), tin-plating (barrier only — a scratch makes iron rust faster), electroplating, cathodic and sacrificial protection (attach Mg/Zn: it “loses electrons more readily and corrodes more readily”), alloying (stainless steel: chromium → corrosion-resistant).
  • Aluminium seems unreactive because its coherent, impervious Al₂O₃ film seals the surface; anodisation thickens it electrochemically (and the porous film takes dye). Rusting carries socio-economic costs — bridges, ships, pipelines.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Do not call it “magnesium is preferentially discharged” — corrosion wording is corrodes/oxidises preferentially (CP 2012 Q9, Paper 1B context). Galvanising (barrier + sacrificial, Zn more reactive than Fe) and anodisation (thickening Al₂O₃) are different principles — confusing them is on record (CP 2015, Paper 1B Q3 context). Tin vs galvanised scratch behaviour is the most-tested contrast in this strand.

🚫 Out of scope for Topic III (depth caps — do not test here)

Electrolysis details — electrodes, half-equations, discharge rules, faraday-type calculations: Topic VII; here electrolytic extraction appears only as an illustration for potassium → aluminium. Alloys as materials (structures, properties, extraction of metals on industrial scale): Topic XIV / XIII — in Topic III alloying appears only as one named rust-prevention method. Blast-furnace chemistry (coke → CO, slag, furnace regions): Topic XIII. Concentration-based acid calculations and titration stoichiometry: Topics IV / XV. Group trends behind metal reactivity: Topic II. (scope wording: Part I, Topic III + NTF-2018 rows VII, XIII, XIV)

Engine A · Strand (a)

Occurrence & Extraction Inspector

From ore to metal: where metals come from, why extraction is needed, and how the reactivity series chooses the method.

Pick a topic

III (a)

Occurrence of metals in nature

$$\text{Au, Pt: free state}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Only the least reactive metals (gold, platinum) occur as the free element; most are locked in compounds — oxides, carbonates, sulphides — mined as ores.
  • Occurrence tracks reactivity: the more reactive the metal, the more exclusively combined it is.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Silver is extracted by a physical method” was a recorded wrong answer — only native metals allow physical separation; most silver ores need chemical extraction (CP 2014, Paper 1B Q4 context).
  • Give the reason with the fact: gold is found free because it is unreactive.
III (a)

Extraction of a metal = reduction of its ore

$$\text{M}^{n+} + n\,\text{e}^- \to \text{M}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • In the ore the metal exists as a positive ion; to obtain the element the ion must gain electrons — its oxidation number decreases.
  • “Extraction involves reduction of the ore” is the phrase the mark scheme looks for.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not say the ore “is oxidized” — the metal ion is reduced; oxygen (if present) leaves as CO₂ or H₂O.
  • Every method below differs only in where the electrons come from: heat, carbon, or the power supply.
III (a)

Method 1 · just heating the oxide

$$2\text{Ag}_2\text{O(s)} \to 4\text{Ag(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Very unreactive metals (mercury, silver) have oxides that thermally decompose on strong heating — no reducing agent needed.
  • The classic lab demonstration: heating mercury(II) oxide or silver oxide gives the shiny metal plus a gas that relights a glowing splint (O₂).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Heating alone works only at the very bottom of the series — do not extend it to copper(II) oxide.
  • State symbols and balancing both carry marks: $2\text{Ag}_2\text{O} \to 4\text{Ag} + \text{O}_2$ must balance.
III (a)

Method 2 · heating the oxide with carbon

$$2\text{CuO} + \text{C} \to 2\text{Cu} + \text{CO}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Zinc, iron, tin, lead, copper (from below aluminium down to the bottom) are extracted by reducing their oxides with coke / carbon.
  • Carbon is cheaper than a reactive-metal reductant, so industrial iron extraction uses carbon — cost is part of the official reasoning (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
  • Observation: black copper(II) oxide turns reddish-brown; the gas turns limewater milky (CO₂).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Carbon sits between aluminium and zinc: it can reduce oxides below it, never MgO/Al₂O₃.
  • Blast-furnace chemistry is Topic XIII — at Topic III level the answer is “heated with carbon”, nothing more.
III (a)

Method 3 · electrolysis of molten ore (illustration)

$$\text{NaCl(l)} \to \text{Na(l)} + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Metals from potassium to aluminium have ions that carbon cannot reduce — the electrons must come from an external circuit.
  • Required only as an illustration: e.g. sodium from molten sodium chloride, aluminium from its dissolved molten oxide. Electrolysis details (electrodes, half-equations, discharge) are Topic VII.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The exam-relevant phrase is “electrolysis of the molten compound” — “electrolysis of the solution” is automatically wrong for reactive metals.
  • Do not spend revision time on cell mechanics here; that mark language is tested in Topic VII.
III (a, b)

Choosing the extraction method

$$\begin{aligned}&\text{metal reactivity } \downarrow\\[-2pt]&\text{ease of reduction } \uparrow\end{aligned}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Position in the series decides: K → Al electrolysis of molten compound; Zn → Cu reduction by carbon; Hg, Ag heating alone; Au, Pt free in nature.
  • A question may give a new metal (“metal Q”) plus facts — place Q relative to carbon/aluminium, then pick the method.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Compare with the metal actually in the oxide: “copper cannot reduce Fe₂O₃” because copper is less reactive than iron — writing “…than aluminium” answered a different question (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
  • Add the economic clause: carbon is cheaper than aluminium, hence coke for iron.
III (a)

Ease of extraction & the order of discovery

$$\text{Cu} \to \text{Fe} \to \text{Al (1825)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Metals were used in the order their ions accept electrons: copper (native / easily reduced by charcoal) → iron (needs hotter furnace) → aluminium (isolated from 1825, industrial electrolysis only from ~1886).
  • Deduce an unknown metal’s reactivity from “known since antiquity” vs “isolated only recently”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The explanation mark is “the more reactive the metal, the later it was discovered” — link, don’t just list dates.
  • Availability of raw material (rich surface ores) is the syllabus’s second allowed factor.
III (a)

Ores are a finite resource → recycle metals

$$\begin{aligned}&\text{ore dug } \downarrow\\[-2pt]&\text{extraction energy } \downarrow\end{aligned}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Describe metal ores as a finite (non-renewable) resource — conservation demands less mining and re-use.
  • Recycling saves the large energy cost of extraction (especially electrolytic metals) and cuts land disruption, spoil heaps and smelting pollution.
  • Link to Hong Kong’s metal-recycling trade as the syllabus activity suggests.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Name two distinct benefits (reserve + energy) when asked for two — two phrasings of one idea score once.
  • “Less landfill of scrap” is a separate accepted point from “less mining”.
III (a)

Evaluating recycling — social · economic · environmental

$$\text{benefit} \; \text{vs} \; \text{cost} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \textbf{judgement}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Evaluate means weigh both sides: reserves and energy saved vs collection cost, the need to separate sorted scrap, and residual reprocessing pollution.
  • Social angle: jobs and cleaner cities; economic: cheaper than primary extraction for Al; environmental: habitat loss avoided vs transport emissions.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A one-sided “recycling is good because…” cannot earn the evaluation marks — always include a limitation.
  • Match the wording to the stem: if it says “from an economic perspective”, money words must appear.
III (a, c)

Word & symbol equations for extraction

$$\text{CuO} + \text{CO} \to \text{Cu} + \text{CO}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • copper(II) oxide + carbon → copper + carbon dioxide; lead(II) oxide + carbon → lead + carbon dioxide.
  • For CO reduction (the carbon monoxide role in a blast furnace, known from context): $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{CO} \to 2\text{Fe} + 3\text{CO}_2$ — the studio’s teaching example for haematite reduction (CP 2015 Q3: about half could not do the empirical formula of the iron oxide; that item’s equation was Fe₃O₄ + 4CO → 3Fe + 4CO₂).
  • Transcribe word → symbol equations with state symbols (s)(l)(g)(aq).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Balanced except reaction schemes” is the convention — a half-balanced $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + \text{CO} \to \text{Fe} + \text{CO}_2$ loses the mark.
  • Carbon dioxide vs carbon monoxide: with C the product is CO₂; with CO the product is CO₂ and the reductant is CO — do not swap.

The Extraction Route Map — one question, three gates

Every “how is this metal obtained?” item passes through the same gates. Read the metal’s position, then the gate answers itself.

Gate 1 · Native metal?

Gold, platinum — found as the free element; physical separation only. Everything else needs chemistry.

Gate 2 · Below carbon?

Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, Cu oxides are reduced by heating with carbon — cheap coke supplies the electrons. Iron’s oxide can also be reduced by CO.

Gate 3 · Very unreactive oxide?

Hg, Ag oxides simply decompose on heating — the ion grabs electrons from its own oxide partner.

Gate 4 · Above carbon (K → Al)?

Carbon is not reactive enough — only the electrolysis of the molten compound forces electrons onto these ions. (Cell details = Topic VII.)

Multi-blank Quiz — Occurrence & Extraction

1. Most metals occur in nature in combined form — as ores (oxides, carbonates, sulphides); only very unreactive metals such as gold (or platinum) are found free.
2. Obtaining a metal from its ore is a reduction: the metal ion gains electrons, so its oxidation number decreases.
3. Silver is obtained from silver oxide by heating alone: $2\text{Ag}_2\text{O} \to$ 4Ag $\text{+ O}_2$; such oxides decompose on heating — the same method suits the oxide of mercury.
4. Zinc and copper oxides are reduced by heating with carbon — carbon is more reactive than these metals but less reactive than aluminium.
5. Potassium to aluminium are extracted by electrolysis of their molten compounds because carbon cannot reduce their ions; the electrolysis process itself is studied in Topic VII.
6. The more reactive a metal, the more readily its atom loses electrons — its ion is correspondingly more difficult to reduce, and the more energy-intensive the extraction method must be.
7. Metals were discovered in order of ease of extraction: copper in the Bronze Age, iron in the Iron Age, aluminium only after electrolysis was possible.
8. Metal ores are a finite resource; recycling saves energy compared with extraction, reduces pollution from mining, and preserves reserves for future generations.
9. Recycling is not free: collection is expensive, scrap must be separated (sorted), and reprocessing still causes some pollution — an evaluation must weigh these against the benefits.
10. copper(II) oxide + carbon → copper + carbon dioxide: the oxide is reduced and carbon is the reducing agent; blast-furnace chemistry in industry belongs to Topic XIII.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Occurrence & Extraction

Reduction of the ore vs oxidation of the metal

The metal ion gains electrons — that is reduction. Saying “the metal is oxidized during extraction” contradicts the ore chemistry and is capped zero by the “contradictory answers” convention.

Free state vs combined state

“All metals occur in ores” is wrong: gold and platinum are native. Name the metal’s reactivity when justifying which state it occurs in.

Heating alone vs heating with carbon

Only Hg and Ag oxides fall apart on heating. CuO needs carbon; ZnO and Fe₂O₃ need carbon (or CO); MgO/Al₂O₃ cannot be reduced by carbon at all.

Molten vs aqueous electrolysis (illustration)

Reactive metals come from the molten compound. An aqueous solution cannot give sodium (the metal is never deposited from solution). (The discharge rule itself is Topic VII; here keep the word “molten”.)

Right comparator vs memorised comparator

“Can X reduce Y’s oxide?” compares X with Y. CP recorded “copper … less reactive than aluminium” as the wrong reason for copper vs iron(III) oxide (CP 2013).

Benefit list vs evaluation

An evaluation = benefits + limitations + judgement. “Recycling protects the environment” alone is a bullet, not an evaluation.

Engine B · Strand (b)

Reactivity & Displacement Inspector

Evidence first: the reactions that build the series, the series that predicts every metal reaction.

Pick a topic

III (b)

Reactions with oxygen / air

$$2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{MgO(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Magnesium burns in air/oxygen with a bright (dazzling white) light, leaving a white powder (MgO) — the MS-recorded wording.
  • Sodium tarnishes rapidly (white Na₂O coating — stored under oil); iron wool sparks and burns in pure oxygen to a black oxide; copper only darkens (black CuO) on strong heating.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Observation marks want light + solid colour: “burns with a bright light, white solid forms” — not “it burns”.
  • Sodium’s product in air is sodium oxide, Na₂O — write the formula from the charges Na⁺, O²⁻.
III (b)

Sodium + cold water

$$2\text{Na(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Full observation set: floats (low density), fizzes rapidly / moves on the surface, dissolves to a colourless alkaline solution; the gas gives a pop with a burning splint (H₂); phenolphthalein turns pink.
  • The reaction is exothermic — enough heat to melt the sodium in many descriptions.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Safety value: only a small (pea-sized) piece of sodium — “large piece” answers lose the safety mark.
  • Keep products straight: metal + water → alkali + hydrogen; metal oxide + water → alkali only (no H₂) (CP 2015, Paper 1B Q10 context).
III (b)

Calcium + cold water

$$\text{Ca(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to \text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Calcium reacts steadily with cold water — slower than sodium: bubbles form, the metal sinks (denser), a white suspension of slightly-ca-soluble Ca(OH)₂ makes the water milky; solution is alkaline.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Calcium hydroxide” formula = Ca(OH)₂ — brackets matter; the charge notation is 2+/2− after the number.
  • Comparing vigor: say “more/slower than …” with both metals named — bare “faster” with no comparator is capped.
III (b)

Magnesium, zinc, iron + steam

$$\text{Mg} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \to \text{MgO} + \text{H}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but burns in steam with a bright light, white MgO + H₂.
  • Zinc and iron react with steam (red-hot) to their oxides + hydrogen; copper does not react with water or steam at all.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Iron + water → iron hydroxide + hydrogen” — at steam level the product is the oxide; keep to the equation taught.
  • A metal failing with cold water but reacting with steam sits mid-series — use exactly this evidence logic when ranking.
III (b)

Metals + dilute hydrochloric / sulphuric acid

$$\text{Zn(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq)} \to \text{ZnSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mg (vigorous), Zn (moderate), Fe (slow) + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen; effervescence, metal dissolves, reaction exothermic.
  • Iron gives the iron(II) salts: Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂; FeSO₄(aq) is pale green.
  • Copper and silver show no reaction with dilute acids — they sit below hydrogen.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Dark green / dirty green” for Fe²⁺(aq) are recorded wrong colours — write pale green (CP 2016, Paper 1B Q2 context).
  • “Fe²⁺”, not “Fe³⁺”, from dilute acids; charge written magnitude-then-sign, HKEAA style.
III (b)

The metal reactivity series

$$\begin{aligned}&\text{K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe}\\[-2pt]&\text{Pb (H) Cu Ag Au}\end{aligned}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Construct the series from evidence: reactions (or lack of them) with oxygen/air, water, steam, dilute acids and displacement results.
  • (H) is the inserted reference point deciding which metals liberate hydrogen from dilute acids.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Questions say “arrange in decreasing / ascending order of reactivity” — read the arrow direction before writing.
  • Every ranking claim must be backed by the evidence line the question supplied — opinions score nothing.
III (b)

Reactivity = tendency to form positive ions

$$\text{M} \to \text{M}^{n+} + n\,\text{e}^-$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • A more reactive metal’s atoms lose electrons more readily — the mark-scheme phrase that powers every “explain” mark.
  • The metal is the reducing agent (it gives electrons to the ion / H⁺ being reduced).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Candidates “wrongly suggested Zn as the oxidising agent” while writing Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ — an electron giver is a reducing agent (CP 2016, Paper 1B Q8 context).
  • Trend language from the MS bank: reactivity increases down Group I because the outermost electron “is weakly bounded by the nucleus” — reuse this causal shape.
III (b)

Displacement in aqueous solution

$$\text{Zn(s)} + \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} \to \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Full observation set for Zn + CuSO₄(aq): reddish-brown solid deposits on the zinc, the blue colour fades towards colourless, the mixture gets hotter (exothermic).
  • Fe + Cu²⁺(aq): grey iron turns brown-coated, blue → pale green.
  • Ionic equation form is what “write balanced ionic equations” demands — spectator SO₄²⁻ omitted.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Say “zinc displaces copper(II) ions” — “displaces copper” was a recorded wrong answer (CP 2012 Q5, Paper 1B context).
  • Solid mass of the strip: zinc dissolves; don’t claim the zinc gains colour — the deposit is on its surface.
III (b)

Solid-state displacement (Zn + CuO)

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Same ranking logic: more reactive element takes oxygen (or electrons) from the less reactive one’s compound.

III (b)

Deducing the order from given results

💡 Examiner Tips
  • Chain each conclusion to its experiment — “B reacts with C²⁺ but not A²⁺ ⇒ B below A, above C”. Loose lists without reasoning are capped.
III (b)

Predicting feasibility of metal reactions

💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Silver + dilute HCl → H₂” is impossible — silver is below hydrogen; the reaction mark pairs with the correct series clause “because … is lower than … in the reactivity series”.
III (a, b)

Extraction method ↔ position in the series

The two displacement rules — everything else is bookkeeping

Multi-blank Quiz — Reactivity & Displacement

1. Magnesium burns in oxygen with a bright (dazzling white) light, leaving a white solid; sodium tarnishes in air to white sodium oxide, formula Na₂O; heated copper turns black copper(II) oxide.
2. Sodium + water: it floats, fizzes rapidly and dissolves to an alkaline solution (phenolphthalein turns pink); the gas is hydrogen.
3. Calcium reacts with cold water more slowly than sodium; the milky solution contains slightly-soluble Ca(OH)₂; magnesium needs steam to react, forming MgO and hydrogen.
4. Metal + dilute acid gives a salt solution and a gas that burns with a squeaky pop; iron in dilute sulphuric acid gives a pale green Fe²⁺ solution; copper gives no reaction.
5. Complete the series: K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe Pb (H) Cu Ag Au.
6. A more reactive metal loses electrons more readily and forms its positive ion more readily; the metal acts as a reducing agent; for Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ to call Zn an “oxidising agent” is marked wrong.
7. Zinc in copper(II) sulphate solution: a reddish-brown solid deposits, the blue solution fades, the temperature rises; the ionic equation is Zn + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu.
8. Iron in copper(II) sulphate solution: the ionic equation is Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s); saying “iron displaces copper” names the element, not the ion — a recorded mark-killer (CP 2012 Q5).
9. Sodium oxide with water gives only an alkaline solution (NaOH) — claiming it gives sodium hydroxide and hydrogen pastes the metal + water result onto the oxide (CP 2015); the oxide dissolving gives pH > 7.
10. If metal X displaces Y from solution and Y displaces Z, the decreasing order is X > Y > Z; no visible change means the added metal is less reactive; a displacement reaction happens only when the free metal sits higher in the series.
11. Heating zinc powder with black copper(II) oxide: the powder turns reddish-brown (copper) with white ZnO formed — displacement in the solid state, explained by zinc being higher than copper in the series.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Reactivity

Displaces copper vs displaces copper(II) ions

In solution the partner is the aqueous ion. CP 2012 Q5 records “Fe displaces Cu” as the wrong wording — and “CuSO₄·5H₂O” wrongly forced into the equation.

Fe²⁺ pale green vs Fe³⁺ yellow

Displacement and dilute-acid reactions of iron give iron(II) — pale green. Yellow/brown Fe³⁺ needs a different story (not this topic). “Dark green / dirty green” are rejected colour words (CP 2016).

Metal + water vs metal oxide + water

Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂. Na₂O + H₂O → NaOH only. CP 2015 records the hydrogen wrongly added to the oxide reaction.

Mg + cold water vs Mg + steam

Cold water: very slow (magnesium “does not react with water” in many tables means liquid water). Steam: burns, MgO + H₂. Name the form of water in the answer.

Dilute acid vs concentrated acid

The “hydrogen from acids” rule holds for dilute HCl / H₂SO₄ only. Concentrated sulphuric acid reacts with copper by a different (oxidising) chemistry — out of scope here; never write H₂ for copper + acid, dilute or otherwise.

Loses electrons vs gains electrons

Reactivity of metals = loss tendency. The metal is the reducing agent; its ion (product) is what a weaker metal cannot reduce. CP 2016 Q8: Zn wrongly labelled “oxidising agent”.

Observation vs conclusion

“Blue fades, brown solid” is observation; “zinc is more reactive” is conclusion. The convention “observation and explanation should match” pairs them — one without the other caps the mark.

Pick a topic

III (c)

The mole

$$1\ \text{mol} \equiv 12\ \text{g of carbon-12}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Word-perfect: the mole is the amount of substance containing as many particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.
  • A mole is a counting unit — “a dozen” for particles; the particles must be specified (atoms, ions, molecules, formula units, electrons).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Definition marks hinge on “atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12” — dropping “carbon-12” or “exactly” caps the mark.
  • Never write “a mole of H₂SO₄ atoms” — say molecules.
III (c)

Avogadro’s constant L

$$L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Avogadro’s constant = the number of particles per mole of a substance; unit mol⁻¹.
  • Use it to convert moles ↔ numbers of atoms/molecules/ions/electrons.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Avogadro’s number has no unit” is wrong — mol⁻¹ is required; “the unit must be correct”.
  • One ion-electron count trap: 1 mol of SO₄²⁻ contains 50 mol of electrons? (S:16 + 4×8 + 2 = 50) — count carefully when asked.
III (c)

Molar mass vs formula mass

$$M(\text{NaCl}) = 58.5\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Formula / relative molecular mass: sum of Aᵣ — unitless. Molar mass: the same number expressed in g mol⁻¹.
  • Count every atom in the formula: (NH₄)₂SO₄ → 2N + 8H + S + 4O; hydrates add whole waters (CuSO₄·5H₂O: + 5 × 18).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Relative atomic mass answered as “35.5 g mol⁻¹” was marked wrong — a relative mass is a ratio (CP 2012 Q1, Paper 1B context).
  • Aᵣ values come from the paper’s data sheet (Cu 63.5, Cl 35.5, Fe 55.8): use those, not memorised roundings.
III (c)

The workhorse: n = m ⁄ M

$$n = \dfrac{m}{M}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • 5.58 g Fe ÷ 55.8 g mol⁻¹ = 0.100 mol; 6.54 g Zn ÷ 65.4 = 0.100 mol.
  • Reverse it: mass of 0.500 mol CuO = 0.500 × 79.5 = 39.75 g.
  • Every reacting-mass and formula question opens with this conversion — moles first, always.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Show the division as its own line — step marks “*” are allocated per line of working.
  • Carry the unit: n in mol, m in g, M in g mol⁻¹.
III (c)

Particles ↔ moles: N = n × L

$$N = n \times L$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • 0.2 mol Fe → 0.2 × 6.02×10²³ = 1.204×10²³ atoms.
  • 3.01×10²³ molecules → 3.01×10²³ ÷ 6.02×10²³ = 0.5 mol.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Multiply vs divide: particles are bigger than moles — if your answer is smaller than n, check the direction.
  • For “number of electrons/ions” questions, multiply by the count in the formula as well (1 mol MgCl₂ → 3 mol ions).
III (c)

What a balanced equation reveals

$$2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{MgO(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Coefficients = mole ratio: 2 mol Mg : 1 mol O₂ : 2 mol MgO — i.e. 48 g Mg : 32 g O₂ : 80 g MgO.
  • The equation “shows a quantitative relationship between reactants and products” — the syllabus’s own phrase for strand (c).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Balance first — “chemical equations should be balanced” is a paper-wide convention; keep the studio’s teaching example Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ fully balanced (CP 2015 Q3: about half could not do the empirical formula of the iron oxide; that item’s equation was Fe₃O₄ + 4CO → 3Fe + 4CO₂).
  • Masses do NOT combine in the ratio of the written numbers (48:32, not 2:1) — convert to moles.
III (c)

The reacting-mass route

$$m \to n \to n' \to m'$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Four beats: mass → moles (÷M) → mole-ratio hop across the equation → moles → mass (×M).
  • Worked (paper-style rounding Mg = 24, MgO = 40): 4.8 g Mg (÷ 24 = 0.2 mol) → n(MgO) = 0.2 mol → m(MgO) = 0.2 × 40 = 8.0 g.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • State which substances the ratio refers to (“mole ratio of Mg to MgO is 1:1”) — the hop mark is for the statement, not just the answer.
  • Interrelationship wording (“masses of reactants and products … state the interrelationship”) wants conservation: total mass of reactants = total mass of products.
III (c)

Percentage by mass of an element

$$w(\text{Fe}) = \frac{2 \times 55.8}{159.6} \times 100 = 69.9\%$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • From formula: (number × Aᵣ) ÷ formula mass × 100% — Fe in Fe₂O₃ = 69.9%; Al in Al₂O₃: 54.0 ÷ 102.0 × 100% = 52.9%.
  • From experiment: (mass of element ÷ mass of compound) × 100% — e.g. magnesium oxide data.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The coefficient (×2 for Fe₂O₃) is the most-missed factor.
  • For hydrates, the water counts in the denominator — CuSO₄·5H₂O uses 249.5 (Cu 63.5, S 32 convention), not 159.5.
III (c)

Empirical formula from composition

$$\frac{70.0}{55.8} : \frac{30.0}{16.0} = 1.25 : 1.88 \approx 2 : 3$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Route: assume 100 g → mass → moles (÷Aᵣ) → divide by smallest → multiply to whole numbers.
  • MS working style: “number of moles of P : number of moles of Cl = 0.226/31.0 : 0.774/35.5 = 1 : 3” — write the chain, get step marks.
  • 1 : 1.5 becomes 2 : 3 — not “about 2”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Definition mark: “the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element” (MS: “simplest positive integer ratio of the numbers of atoms”).
  • Half of candidates failed exactly this calculation (CP 2015, Paper 1B Q3 context) — drill the division chain until it is boring.
III (c)

Molecular formula · limiting reagent

$$\begin{aligned}&\text{molecular} = (\text{empirical})_n\\[-2pt]&n = M_r \div M_{\text{emp}}\end{aligned}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Empirical CH₂O (Mr 30) with molar mass 180 → n = 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆.
  • Limiting reagent: for $a\text{A} + b\text{B} \to \dots$ compare $n_A/a$ with $n_B/b$; the smaller quotient runs out first and fixes the product moles.
  • Chained problems (two reactions) re-check the limiter at each stage (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never compare raw moles: 0.1 mol Mg “beats” 0.1 mol O₂ only after dividing by 2 and 1.
  • “Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ” is the MS sentence — quote it, then plug in Mr.

The Mole Highway — four gates into n

Every reacting-mass question enters the highway through one of these gates, crosses the equation bridge, and exits by the reverse gate.

Gate 1 · From mass

$$n = \dfrac{m}{M}$$ grams ÷ (g mol⁻¹) → mol.

Gate 2 · From particles

$$n = \dfrac{N}{L}$$ atoms (or molecules) ÷ 6.02×10²³ mol⁻¹ → mol.

Gate 3 · Across the bridge

$$n' = n \times \frac{\text{coefficient of target}}{\text{coefficient of known}}$$ the balanced equation is the only bridge between two substances.

Gate 4 · From composition

Assume 100 g, divide each % by Aᵣ → the mole ratio is the empirical formula after ÷ smallest.

Every calculation is a route

Limiting-reagent item = two Gate-1 entries, a comparison, then one Gate-3 hop from the limiting substance. Empirical formula = Gate 4 only. Reacting masses = Gate 1 → 3 → reverse Gate 1. Exit ramp: moles → mass via ×M, or moles → particles via ×L.

Multi-blank Quiz — Reacting Masses

1. A mole is the amount of substance containing as many particles as atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12; Avogadro’s constant is 6.02×10²³ mol⁻¹.
2. The formula mass of NaCl is 58.5 (unitless); 5.85 g of NaCl = 0.1 mol; the mass of 0.5 mol CuO (M = 79.5 g mol⁻¹) is 39.75 g.
3. The number of atoms in 0.2 mol iron = 0.2 × 6.02×10²³ = 1.204×10²³; 3.01×10²³ molecules are 0.5 mol.
4. From 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: 2 mol Mg react with 1 mol O₂; 12 g Mg need 8 g O₂ and form 20 g MgO.
5. Iron(III) oxide’s formula mass is 159.6; iron’s percentage by mass = 2×55.8 ÷ 159.6 × 100 = 69.9% — the factor 2 for the two Fe atoms is the easy miss.
6. A compound is 70.0% Fe, 30.0% O: divide by Aᵣ → 1.25 : 1.875; ÷ smallest → 1 : 1.5; ×2 → the empirical formula is Fe₂O₃.
7. The molecular formula equals (empirical formula)ₙ; empirical CH₂O has unit mass 30, and with molar mass 180 the molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆.
8. To find the limiting reagent divide each number of moles by its equation coefficient; for 0.1 mol Mg + 0.1 mol O₂ (2:1) the quotients are 0.05 and 0.1, so magnesium is the limiting reagent.
9. Product moles come from the limiting reagent only: n(MgO) = 0.1 mol; the excess O₂ left = 0.1 − 0.05 = 0.05 mol, a mass of 1.6 g.
10. The working form “0.226/31.0 : 0.774/35.5 = 1 : 3” gives PClₙ with n = 3, formula PCl₃, because (31.0 + 3×35.5) = 137.5 g mol⁻¹, and the next multiple would exceed the given molar mass (250 g mol⁻¹).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Reacting Masses

Mole ratio vs mass ratio

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO is 2 : 1 in moles but 48 : 32 = 3 : 2 in grams. “2 g of magnesium react with 1 g of oxygen” is false.

Formula mass vs molar mass

Same number, different clothing: 58.5 (no unit) vs 58.5 g mol⁻¹. Relative masses with g or g mol⁻¹ attached are marked wrong (CP 2012).

Empirical vs molecular formula

Empirical = simplest ratio; molecular = actual atom count = (empirical)ₙ. Fe₂O₃ is both; CH₂O vs C₆H₁₂O₆ distinguishes them. Give the definition each time the term appears.

Raw moles vs moles ÷ coefficient

0.1 mol Mg vs 0.1 mol O₂ “look equal” but O₂ is in huge excess (quotients 0.05 vs 0.1). CP 2013: candidates missed Na as the limiter in a second reaction — re-test at every stage.

× L vs ÷ L

moles → particles: multiply; particles → moles: divide. Sanity check: 0.2 mol must give ~10²³ atoms, never 10²².

Ratio 1 : 1.5 → “≈ 2” vs ×2 → 2 : 3

Empirical formulae are exact whole-number ratios — multiply all terms by the smallest factor that clears fractions. Rounding a 1.5 up is a different (wrong) compound.

“% of the compound” vs “% of the element”

“Percentage by mass of iron in iron(III) oxide” = 69.9%, not 30.1 (that is oxygen). Read which numerator the question asks for, and include waters of crystallisation in the denominator.

Engine D · Strand (d)

Corrosion & Protection Inspector

Rusting is a redox race — learn the conditions, read the indicator, and match each prevention method to its principle.

Pick a topic

III (d)

The nature of iron rust

$$\text{rust} = \text{hydrated iron(III) oxide}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Corrosion = gradual attack of a metal by substances in its environment; rusting is corrosion of iron specifically.
  • Rust is reddish-brown, flaky and porous — it traps moisture and lets air through, so it never seals the surface (contrast Al₂O₃).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Iron oxide” alone is loose — the description mark wants hydrated iron(III) oxide.
  • Do not describe the indicator as turning reddish-brown — that colour is the rust itself (CP 2012 Q9, Paper 1B context).
III (d)

Essential conditions — the three-tube test

$$\text{water AND oxygen} \to \text{rust}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Tube A: nail in boiled distilled water under an oil layer → only water (air excluded). Tube B: nail in dry air with calcium chloride → only air. Tube C: ordinary water + air → rusts; A and B do not.
  • Conclusion sentence pattern: “rusting requires both water and oxygen (air)” — each tube removes exactly one condition.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The oil layer’s purpose: prevent air (oxygen) dissolving into the water; calcium chloride’s purpose: absorb water. Swap the purposes and both marks die.
  • Fair test in hexane-style MC: neither nail in dry organic solvent rusts — “iron corrodes faster than copper” ≠ rusting in the absence of water/oxygen (CP 2012 Q23).
III (d)

Factors that speed rusting

$$\text{salt, acid, heat} \uparrow \text{ rate}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Electrolytes (salt solutions, acids) speed rusting — corrosion is a redox process and mobile ions facilitate it.
  • Iron in AgNO₃(aq) corrodes fastest (displaced silver + mobile ions), then Zn(NO₃)₂(aq), then plain water.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • ~46% ranked that MC wrong — the report’s own line: “corrosion of iron, being a redox reaction, can be facilitated by mobile ions” (CP 2014 Q3).
  • Coastal cities rust faster: salt spray, not “more oxygen”.
III (d)

Rust indicator — two colours, two ions

$$\text{Fe}^{2+} \xrightarrow{\ \text{K}_3\text{Fe(CN)}_6\ } \text{blue}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Rust indicator = potassium hexacyanoferrate(III) + phenolphthalein in jelly.
  • Blue (precipitate) marks Fe²⁺(aq) from corroding iron; pink marks OH⁻(aq) — both appear as rusting starts; the un-rusted gel shows neither colour (colourless/pale — never rust-coloured).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Half the cohort missed that K₃Fe(CN)₆ + Fe²⁺ gives blue — “it is the main ingredient in rust indicator” (CP 2014 Q21).
  • Give the colour before and after: a “correct colour of the gel before rusting” was itself an examined mark (CP 2012 Q9).
III (d)

Barrier coatings — paint, oil, grease, plastic

$$\text{coat} \Rightarrow \text{no water, no O}_2 \text{ at surface}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Barrier logic word-perfect from the MS bank: “sealed with grease, the iron cannot contact with water and less air (oxygen), so rusting cannot occur”.
  • Choose by use: machinery → grease; gates/rails → paint; tools → oil; kettles/handles → plastic.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Say BOTH exclusions (water and air/oxygen); “keeps it dry” alone is half a mark.
  • Intact barrier only — mention what happens if it cracks when the question asks about scratched tin.
III (d)

Galvanising vs tin-plating — the scratched-coating question

$$\begin{aligned}&\text{Zn: barrier + sacrificial}\\[-2pt]&\text{Sn: barrier only}\end{aligned}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Galvanising: iron coated with zinc. Zinc is more reactive than iron — even when the coating is scratched, the zinc continues to corrode in place of the iron (sacrificial).
  • Tin-plating: steel coated with tin (less reactive). Intact it is a good barrier (“tin cans”), but once scratched the iron rusts faster — the iron becomes the metal that corrodes preferentially.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The comparison sentence that wins both marks: “zinc is higher than iron in the reactivity series, tin is lower”.
  • Galvanising ≠ electroplating: galvanised coatings are dipped/metallurgically bonded, and it is the zinc’s position in the series that does the extra work.
III (d)

Electroplating & cathodic protection

$$\text{cathodic: } e^- \text{ forced in}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Electroplating: thin protective/attractive metal layer (tin, chromium, silver) — barrier protection; the electrolysis method itself is Topic VII.
  • Cathodic protection: the structure is connected to the negative terminal of an external power supply, which forces electrons onto it so the metal cannot lose them to corrosion.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Keep the two electron sources distinct: cathodic = power supply; sacrificial = a more reactive metal block — examiners pair-test them.
III (d)

Sacrificial protection — the MS sentence

$$\text{Mg} \to \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2e^- \;(\text{iron spared})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Buried ships’ hulls and pipelines carry magnesium (or zinc) blocks — replaceable, since they corrode away.
  • The mark-scheme chain verbatim: “Mg is higher than iron in the metal reactivity series … loses electrons more readily and will become Mg²⁺ … Mg corrodes more readily”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Magnesium is preferentially discharged” is a recorded wrong answer — discharge is electrolysis vocabulary; here magnesium corrodes / is oxidised preferentially (CP 2012 Q9).
  • Copper attachments would make things worse — copper is below iron, so the iron would sacrifice itself.
III (d)

Alloying & the socio-economic cost of rusting

$$\text{Fe} + \text{Cr (/ Ni)} \to \text{stainless steel}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Alloying as a protection method: “Introducing chromium into iron makes iron become corrosion resistant” (MS wording); stainless steel = iron + carbon + chromium (often nickel).
  • Rusting costs: replacing bridges, ships, cars, pipelines; safety inspection and painting programmes — a socio-economic problem the syllabus asks you to recognise.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Hardness-vs-corrosion pairing: carbon makes iron harder, chromium makes it corrosion resistant — the alloy-structure chemistry itself is Topic XIV.
III (d)

Aluminium — reactive yet corrosion resistant

$$4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Aluminium is more reactive than expected from its surface behaviour: a thin, tough, coherent and impervious Al₂O₃ film forms instantly and seals the metal.
  • Anodisation: electrolytically thickening that oxide film — corrosion resistance enhanced; the porous thickened layer also takes dye (decorative anodised frames).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • MS wording: “Al₂O₃(s) is impermeable / impervious to water and oxygen / air, thus the corrosion resistance of aluminium can be enhanced”.
  • Never write “aluminium is unreactive”; write “its oxide film protects it”. The oxide formula Al₂O₃ and the word “anodisation” each carry their own marks.

Protection Decision Map — match the method to the job

One question orders everything: is the shield a barrier, a sacrifice, or an electron pump?

Branch 1 · Just keep air & water off

Paint (gates, ships’ topsides), oil/grease (machinery), plastic (handles, kettles). Cheap, but a crack exposes the iron. Same branch: electroplating with tin/chromium for cans and cutlery — barrier (plus looks).

Branch 2 · Coating that forgives scratches

Galvanising — the zinc is above iron in the series, so a scratched coating still sacrifices for the iron. Tin plating is NOT branch-2: Sn is below Fe, so a scratch accelerates rusting.

Branch 3 · Sacrifice a block

Magnesium (or zinc) blocks bolted to ship hulls, buried pipes, jetties — the block “loses electrons more readily”, becomes Mg²⁺, corrodes more readily; iron is spared. Blocks are replaced.

Branch 4 · Pump electrons in

Cathodic protection — structure wired to the negative terminal of an external supply; permanent for major steelwork (pipelines, offshore). Plus Branch 0: alloy the whole thing (stainless: Cr/Ni).

Every method attacks one essential condition

Rusting needs water and oxygen and the iron’s electron-loss tendency. Barriers delete the first two; sacrificial and cathodic methods hijack the third — a more willing electron donor (Mg, or the power supply) supplies electrons so iron atoms never have to leave as Fe²⁺.

Multi-blank Quiz — Corrosion & Protection

1. Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, reddish-brown and porous (flaky) — unlike aluminium’s oxide it does not protect the metal beneath.
2. The essential conditions for rusting are water and oxygen (air); in the three-tube test the oil layer keeps air out of boiled distilled water, while calcium chloride keeps the air in the other tube dry.
3. Rusting is faster in salt solution because corrosion is a redox reaction facilitated by mobile ions; a nail in AgNO₃(aq) corrodes fastest of the three examined liquids (CP 2014 Q3).
4. Rust indicator is a mixture of potassium hexacyanoferrate(III) and phenolphthalein; a blue colour shows Fe²⁺(aq) and a pink colour shows OH⁻(aq); the reddish-brown seen later is the rust itself, not the indicator.

6. Barrier coatings (paint, oil, grease, plastic) prevent rusting by stopping iron contacting water and air (oxygen); machinery joints use grease.
7. In sacrificial protection a block of magnesium (or zinc) is attached to the hull; it “loses electrons more readily and will become Mg²⁺”, so it corrodes more readily than iron — writing that it is “preferentially discharged” is wrong wording.
8. Cathodic protection differs because the electrons come from an external power supply, the structure being connected to its negative terminal; no more-reactive metal block is consumed.
9. Stainless steel resists corrosion because iron is alloyed with chromium (often with nickel) — “introducing chromium into iron makes iron become corrosion resistant”; alloy structures are studied in detail only in Topic XIV.
10. Aluminium is corrosion resistant because its oxide film, Al₂O₃, is impervious to water and oxygen; anodisation uses electrolysis to make that film thicker.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Corrosion & Protection

Corrosion vs rusting

Corrosion = any metal’s environmental attack; rusting = corrosion of iron only. Copper’s green coating is corrosion, never “rust”.

Galvanised vs tin-plated (when scratched)

Both start as barriers. After a scratch the story splits: zinc (above iron) keeps sacrificing; tin (below iron) makes the iron corrode faster. The series position, not “quality”, decides.

Sacrificial vs cathodic protection

Same physics (electrons forced onto iron), different donor: a more reactive metal block (sacrificial, consumed) vs a power supply (cathodic, adjustable). Examiners test the distinction directly.

“Corrodes preferentially” vs “is preferentially discharged”

Discharge belongs to electrolysis ions at electrodes. In corrosion language: corrodes / reacts / is oxidised preferentially (CP 2012 Q9).

Indicator blue/pink vs rust reddish-brown

Three colours, three owners: blue = Fe²⁺ with hexacyanoferrate(III); pink = OH⁻ with phenolphthalein; reddish-brown = rust. Mixing them was recorded (CP 2012 Q9; CP 2014 Q21).

Aluminium unreactive vs aluminium protected

Al is well above iron in reactivity — “unreactive” is factually wrong. The coherent, impervious Al₂O₃ film does the protecting.

Anodisation vs plating

Anodisation thickens the object’s own oxide; electroplating adds a different metal. Both use electrolytic cells (Topic VII machinery), but what grows is different — the CP-logged confusion between galvanising and anodisation principles lives here (CP 2015, Paper 1B Q3 context).
