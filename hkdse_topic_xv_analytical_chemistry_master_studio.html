Syllabus Master Overview

Topic XV Learning Points & Mark-Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(e))

Every What students should learn / be able to strand of Analytical Chemistry, with HKEAA watch-outs from mark-scheme language and Candidates’ Performance reports. Depth cap for this elective: the topic overview itself — “in-depth understanding of the principles and detailed operation procedure of the instruments are not expected” — plus NTF-2018 II-B XV (c) on volumetric details. Paper 2 (§C) sets you choose two of three electives; citations like 2013 2 Q3 name the elective in context.

Topic XV (a) Chemical tests bank

Detecting the presence of chemical species — flame tests, gases, cations, anions, functional groups

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Gather the tests already taught in Topics I–XI into one reference bank: flame test for Ca, Cu, K, Na (Part I names exactly these four), gas tests for H₂, O₂, chlorine, carbon dioxide, water, ammonia, sulphur dioxide, hydrogen chloride.
  • Cation set: Al³⁺, NH₄⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Zn²⁺ — NaOH(aq) and NH₃(aq) in excess discriminate; anion set: Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, CO₃²⁻, ClO⁻, SO₃²⁻ (Part I wording).
  • Functional groups in carbon compounds: C=C, –OH, –CHO, >C=O, –COOH; “state the reaction conditions and observations of the tests for the presence of carbonyl compounds using 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine and Tollens’ reagent” is an explicit able-to (Part I).
  • Skills marks ride on reporting: “record observations accurately and systematically”, and “justify the conclusion of the presence of a chemical species” (Part I right column).
  • Mark allocation pattern: “Correct chemical reagent” = 1 mark; “Correct observations with comparison” = 1 mark (ms bank §11) — comparison between the samples is a mark, not decoration.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Potassium is lilac — the bank records “[Not Accept deep purple and violet]” (ms bank §11). The 2012 general comments: candidates “were not able to state the expected colour changes/observations in chemical tests, or the difference in results of positive and negative tests” (CP 2012). A gas test must name the gas: giving an HCl(aq) test for HCl(g) scored nothing (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).

Topic XV (b) Purify & separate

Separation and purification methods — crystallisation, distillation / fractional distillation, liquid-liquid extraction, paper / column / thin-layer chromatography

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • “Devise a scheme to separate a mixture of known substances” and “justify the choice of an appropriate method” (Part I right column) — the property you exploit must be named (boiling point, solubility, particle size, adsorption).
  • Chromatography trio in scope: paper, column, TLC; “determine the Rf values of substances in a chromatogram”; Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front, no units (master summary §8.5; CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Crude organic work-up register from the bank: “Use separating funnel to remove water (the lower liquid layer)” then “Carry out fractional distillation on the upper layer” (ms bank §11).
  • “Determine the melting point or boiling point of a substance” and “examine the purity of a substance by measuring its melting or boiling point” (Part I right column) — pure = sharp fixed point; impure = lower and blurred.
  • Liquid-liquid extraction beyond earlier topics (Part I): solute partitions between two immiscible solvents — “Cinnamaldehyde is a non-polar compound which can dissolve in a relatively non-polar organic solvent like ethyl ethanoate” (ms bank §5).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Fractional distillation sorts by boiling point — “some candidates wrongly stated that the separation was based on the melting point of the alkanes” (CP 2014 Paper 1B Q6). Water is immiscible with hex-1-ene/octane: say two layers, then separate them — “just mentioned that hex-1-ene can be isolated … by fractional distillation” lost the marks (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3). Extraction with ether needs the ether removed by distillation after the funnel (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).

Topic XV (c) Volumetric analysis

Quantitative method of analysis — volumetric analysis (acid-base and redox), with error awareness and evidence-based calculations

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • “Investigations using different types of volumetric method, which may involve acid-base reactions and redox reactions” (Part I overview); burette readings and titres recorded to two decimal places (CP 2014/2015/2016 SBA comments).
  • Three requirements for a titrimetric reaction, banked verbatim: “a significant observable change can easily be detected”, “the reaction must be significantly complete”, “the rate of reaction must be fast enough to be practical” (ms bank §11).
  • Redox volumetrics carried in from earlier topics: MnO₄⁻ (pale yellow → pale pink), I₂/thiosulfate–starch (colourless → dark blue), Cr₂O₇²⁻/Fe²⁺ (ms bank §11; CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).
  • “Be aware of and take necessary steps to minimise possible sources of error” (Part I) — rinse rules, meniscus, air bubble, over-shoot, each with its direction of error (master summary §18.2).
  • Back-titration arithmetic lives here: moles reacted = moles added − moles excess; aspirin-type analyses are “solvable when sufficient information is given” (NTF-2018, Topic IV (f) row; worked bank line 0.003 − 0.00102 = 0.00198, ms bank §11).
  • Depth cap: NTF-2018 II-B XV (c) — “details of specific chemical processes involved in volumetric analysis, other than those included in the previous topics, are not expected”.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Colour changes need both halves: “from colourless to pink”, never “clear to red” or a bare “pink is observed” (CP 2013 Paper 1B Q4); methyl orange ends at red → orange — “red to yellow” means you over-shot (CP 2012 Paper 1B Q7; ms bank §4 [NOT ACCEPT]). Units: “did not express the total concentration of SO₂ in mg dm⁻³ as required” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3), and “the unit must be correct” is a standing bank rule (ms bank, general conventions).

Topic XV (d) IR · MS · colorimetry

Instrumental analytical methods — basic principles and applications of colorimetry, IR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (incl. simple fragmentation patterns)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Colorimetry: “construct a calibration curve by measuring absorbance of standard solutions” and “determine the concentration of a solution using a calibration curve” (Part I right column); absorbance tracks colour intensity ∝ concentration (ms bank §11).
  • IR: identify from a spectrum and a given correlation table: C–H, O–H, N–H, C=C, C≡C, C=O, C≡N (Part I right column) — the ranges you need are printed; reading them is the skill.
  • MS: “m/z of the molecular ion” = relative molecular mass; identify R⁺, RCO⁺ and C₆H₅CH₂⁺ (Part I right column); banked exemplars m/z 91 → C₇H₇⁺, 105 → C₆H₅CO⁺, 77 → C₆H₅⁺ (ms bank §1).
  • Isotope signatures: ³⁵Cl : ³⁷Cl = 3 : 1 gives M and M+2 peaks; bromine gives 1 : 1 (master summary §1.5).
  • Purpose line from the overview: students “should be aware of the limitations inherent in the use of conventional chemical tests … and hence appreciate the application of modern instruments” (Part I overview).
  • Depth cap: “in-depth understanding of the principles and detailed operation procedure of the instruments are not expected at this level of study” (Part I overview; not_expected.txt XV).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The plus sign on fragment ions is worth marks and “[Must show plus sign]” is banked (ms bank §1); m/z 91/108 without charges failed twice (CP 2012 & CP 2014, Paper 2 Q3). Match the instrument to the question asked — 60% could not explain why MS not IR distinguishes two similar liquids (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3). And write cm⁻¹, never “/cm” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).

Topic XV (e) Sources · impact

Contribution of analytical chemistry to our society — analysis of food and drugs, environmental protection, forensic science, clinical diagnoses

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Four named application fields (Part I left column): food and drugs (dye in soft drinks, vitamin C, analgesics by TLC), environmental protection, forensic chemistry (“its role in providing legal evidence” (Part I right column)), clinical diagnoses (“diagnosis, treatment and prevention of diseases”).
  • Air-quality line to quote: “gauging levels of atmospheric pollutants like CO and dioxin, and indoor air pollutants like formaldehyde” (Part I right column); dioxin is “carcinogenic / can cause cancer”, formed by “combustion of materials containing chlorine / PVC”, “incineration / emission from incinerators” (ms bank §11).
  • Instrument + application pairs pay: GC-MS “can measure more accurately the low level of dioxin than using gravimetric analysis or volumetric analysis” (ms bank §11 · 2012 2 Q3); (gas) chromatography for octane content of petrol (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Clean water STSE: “filtration, precipitation and distillation” for metropolitan supply; for travellers, “iodine tincture followed by ascorbic acid” makes natural water ‘safe’ (Part I STSE).
  • Values line worth echoing in extended answers: “integrity in recording and reporting data”, “following standard methods … and validating measurements” (Part I values & attitudes).
Sources / impact line: every method in this studio is sourced below — tests from Topics I/III/IV/VII/XI gathered into one bank; separation from I/II/V; titration from IV/VII/VIII; instruments read against the HKEAA Data Book correlation table; society items from the Part I STSE list and 2012–2025 §C papers.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Name instruments precisely: “computer” and “machine” are recorded loose terms (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3) — write gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) in full (ms bank §11). Dioxin-source answers that blamed “burning of fossil fuels” or “chemicals released from furniture” scored poorly (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3) — the banked source is chlorine-containing combustion.

🚫 Out of scope — deliberately NOT taught in this studio (with reasons)

  • Instrument internals: ionisation chambers, magnetic-sector mechanics, IR source/interferometer design, colorimeter lamp/filter/detector circuitry — “in-depth understanding of the principles and detailed operation procedure of the instruments are not expected” (Part I overview · not_expected.txt XV). Basic principles + applications ARE expected.
  • IR wavenumber recall beyond the Data Book table: the able-to says identify groups “from an IR spectrum and a given correlation table” (Part I right column) — this studio teaches the eight banked ranges (they recur in papers) but exams supply the table.
  • GC / HPLC mechanics: only name + application level (octane content, dioxin levels, blood-alcohol GC is a suggested research activity, not an examinable principle) (Part I activities; ms bank §11).
  • Volumetric chemistry outside prior topics: NTF-2018 II-B XV (c) — new specific chemical processes in volumetric analysis are not expected; Mohr’s method, hypochlorite-in-bleach, iron tablets, permanganate index and iodometric vitamin C work appear as suggested activities (Part I) — named for context, no exotic chemistry taught beyond Topics IV/VII/VIII.
  • Optical isomer detection (polarimetry) and NMR spectroscopy: not on the XV list at all (polarimetry is explicitly NOT expected even in XI) (not_expected.txt XI/XV).
  • SBA administration: practical logs and moderation belong to school assessment, not this studio (EXAM-ANATOMY).

XV (a)

The detective’s template — reagent · observation · conclusion

$$\text{test} \to \text{observation} \to \text{conclusion} \\ \text{reagent 1 mark; comparison 1 mark}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Part I skills chain: “decide on and carry out an appropriate chemical test”, “record observations accurately and systematically”, “justify the conclusion of the presence of a chemical species” (Part I right column).
  • Banked marking: “Correct chemical reagent” = 1 mark; “Correct observations with comparison between the tests on X and Y” = 1 mark (ms bank §11) — in compare questions, the comparison word pair is itself the mark.
  • Observation sentences name what you SEE (colour, precipitate, gas) — never the name of the product; the conclusion line carries the identity.
  • “Assess possible risks associated with chemical tests” is on the list (Part I) — hazard lines are part of full marks.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2012 general comments record candidates who could not state “the expected colour changes/observations … or the difference in results of positive and negative tests” — learn each test as a PAIR of outcomes.
  • Write “a white precipitate forms”, not “it turns white” — a precipitate is a substance; “white” alone describes a colour change of the whole mixture and reads as ambiguity.
🧤 Risk register
  • Small volumes; AgNO₃(aq) stains skin; chlorine and SO₂ test in fume hood / with paper at the tube mouth, not by inhaling.
XV (a)

Flame test — Ca, Cu, K, Na (the four named)

$$\text{Na: golden yellow} \quad \text{K: lilac} \\ \text{Ca: brick red} \quad \text{Cu: blue-green}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Part I names exactly four flame-test cations: calcium, copper, potassium, sodium.
  • Banked procedure, sentence-ready: “Moisten a clean nichrome / platinum wire with concentrated hydrochloric acid and dip the wire into a small amount of … crystals. Heat the tip … in a non-luminous flame” (ms bank §11 · 2017 scheme).
  • Colour wording is banked: “Sodium ions give an intense golden yellow flame while potassium ions give a lilac flame” (ms bank §11).
  • Potassium’s lilac is masked by sodium traces — view through blue cobalt glass (standard practical convention; the bank fixes the colours, not the glass).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Flame test – gives a lilac [purple/light purple] flame” — and the bank says [Not Accept deep purple and violet] (ms bank §11). Lilac is the safe word.
  • A flame test was a recorded WRONG answer where the 2016 solids were best separated by dissolving in water first — “Many candidates incorrectly considered that flame test can be used”, and “bright white light for MgSO₄” was given (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q9). Flame tests need a named-ion contrast, not any pair of white solids.
XV (a)

Gas tests I — hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide

$$\text{H}_2\text{: squeaky pop} \quad \text{O}_2\text{: relights} \\ \text{CO}_2\text{: limewater turns milky}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • H₂ — a lighted splint at the tube mouth: “squeaky pop”.
  • O₂ — a glowing wooden splint: it relights. Lighted vs glowing is the mark-killer swap.
  • CO₂ — bubble through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution): turns milky / cloudy white (CaCO₃ suspension); excess CO₂ re-clears — extension, not the required answer.
  • Gas tests come from Topics I/II/IV teaching, gathered here as Part I lists them among XV’s species (master summary §8.1).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “The gas relights a glowing splint” — subject and instrument both named; “the splint burned” is not a test result.
  • For the pop test write the sound (“squeaky pop”), not “a small explosion occurs”.
XV (a)

Gas tests II — chlorine, ammonia, hydrogen chloride

$$\text{Cl}_2\text{: bleaches damp litmus} \\ \text{NH}_3\text{: damp RED litmus} \to \text{blue}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Chlorine — damp litmus paper is bleached (white): acidity turns it red first, bleaching is the signature — “Litmus bleaches (turns white)” (master summary §8.1).
  • Ammonia — the only common basic gas: turns damp red litmus blue; holding a glass rod dipped in conc. HCl gives dense white smoke (NH₄Cl) — supporting observation.
  • Hydrogen chloride GAS — turns damp blue litmus red, and fumes white in moist air; the 2014 §C report: candidates “wrongly gave a chemical test for HCl(aq) instead of one for HCl(g)” (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3) — acid tests (litmus solution, carbonates) answer a different question about the solution, not the gas.
  • Both litmus tests need the paper damp — dry litmus in dry gas changes nothing.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The suck-back design belongs with these soluble gases: in an inverted-funnel trap for very soluble NH₃ the funnel’s job is “to prevent sucking back” — most 2012 candidates wrongly said it stopped gas escaping (CP 2012 Paper 1B Q7).
  • Ammonia test sentences need “damp” + “red” + “turns blue” — three carriers, up to full marks.
🧤 Risk register
  • Cl₂ and HCl: toxic/corrosive gases — test with paper at the mouth of the vessel, never exhale through the tube.
XV (a)

Gas tests III — sulphur dioxide & water vapour (with banked sentences)

$$\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\text{(aq)/H}^+ + \text{SO}_2\text{: orange} \to \text{green} \\ \text{CuSO}_4\text{(s) white} \to \text{blue with H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • SO₂, verbatim bank sentence: “Add a piece of filter paper which is soaked with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇(aq) to the gas. The filter paper turns from orange to green” (ms bank §11) — SO₂ is the reducing agent doing it.
  • SO₂ also decolourises acidified KMnO₄(aq) (purple → colourless) — Topic VII/VIII reducing-agent register (master summary F.3).
  • Water vapour — anhydrous copper(II) sulphate white → blue; on heating hydrated salts, “The crystals change from blue to white / pale blue” (CuSO₄·5H₂O) and condensation forms at the cool end (ms bank §11; master summary F.2).
  • Second water paper: “dry CoCl₂ paper … turns from blue to pink” with water (ms bank §11 · 2017 scheme) — both reversible tests are banked.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • For heating-hydrated-salt questions the 2017 scheme pairs the procedure words: “Heat respectively… place a piece of dry CoCl₂ paper in the mouth of the tube” (ms bank §11) — paper at the mouth, not dipped in the solid.
  • Do not propose CuSO₄(s) or CoCl₂(s) as a drying agent in a desiccator — 2013 §B report records those as inappropriate choices (CP 2013 Paper 1B Q5 context); they are detection papers here.
XV (a)

NH₄⁺ and Ca²⁺ spotlights

$$\text{NH}_4^+\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^-\text{(aq)} \to \text{NH}_3\text{(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\text{(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ammonium: add NaOH(aq), warm, test the gas with damp red litmus → turns blue. The half-equation above is banked (2018 scheme, “Neglect physical state” note in the extract) (ms bank §4).
  • Calcium: the syllabus route is the flame test (brick red) (Part I) — Ca(OH)₂ with NaOH gives no reliable visible precipitate at typical concentrations; do not force it into the cation table.
  • NH₄⁺ also carries Topics III–IV chemistry: it is the cation of soluble ammonium salts, liberating NH₃ only from alkali + heat.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Warm gently” — not “burn” nor “electrolyse”; and the litmus must be damp and held at the tube mouth.
  • Absent the alkali, no ammonia: mixing an ammonium salt with acid is a classic zero-scoring suggestion.
XV (a)

Cations + aqueous sodium hydroxide (then in excess)

$$\text{M}^{n+}\text{(aq)} + n\,\text{OH}^-\text{(aq)} \to \text{M(OH)}_n\text{(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The table (master summary §8.3 + Part I set): Cu²⁺ light-blue ppt, insoluble in excess · Fe²⁺ green ppt, insoluble · Fe³⁺ red-brown ppt, insoluble · Al³⁺ / Zn²⁺ white ppt, dissolves in excess NaOH · Mg²⁺ / Ca²⁺ white, insoluble (Ca²⁺ unreliable at low concentration — flame is Part I’s test).
  • Procedure discipline: add dropwise with shaking, then in excess — one reagent gives two observations and the pair is the evidence.
  • The white-dissolves-in-excess pair is Al³⁺ or Zn²⁺; card 7 finishes the discrimination.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • 2017 MS banked the comparison sentence: “White precipitate formed initially for all of them. But only the precipitate of ZnSO₄ dissolves in excess aqueous ammonia / NaOH(aq)” (ms bank §11) — note the report style: initial ppt AND excess behaviour.
  • Fe²⁺ is the green one and darkens on standing (air oxidation to Fe(III)) — do not “correct” it to red-brown in a test answer.
XV (a)

Cations + aqueous ammonia — the Al³⁺/Zn²⁺ split

$$\text{Al(OH)}_3\text{: insoluble in excess NH}_3 \\ \text{Zn(OH)}_2\text{: dissolves in excess NH}_3$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • With NH₃(aq): Cu²⁺ light-blue ppt that dissolves in excess to a deep blue solution (the copper–ammonia complex, Topics III/XII) · Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ red-brown ppts insoluble in excess · Zn²⁺ white ppt dissolves in excess · Al³⁺ white ppt insoluble in excess (master summary §8.3).
  • The key distinguisher: “Al³⁺ vs Zn²⁺ — both white ppt with both reagents; only Zn²⁺ dissolves in excess NH₃” (master summary §8.3).
  • Plan answer shape: “Add aqueous ammonia / NaOH(aq) until excess” after “Dissolve the solids separately in water” — the 2017 banked scheme order (ms bank §11).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • 2016 report: most candidates “failed to use water to dissolve the solids first” before choosing reagents (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q9) — schemes earn the “systematic” marks by sequencing.
  • Never mix reagents mid-sentence (“add NaOH and NH₃”): one reagent, one excess, two observations.
XV (a)

Halide ions — AgNO₃(aq) acidified with HNO₃, then NH₃

$$\text{Ag}^+\text{(aq)} + \text{X}^-\text{(aq)} \to \text{AgX(s)} \\ \text{Cl}^-\text{: white} \quad \text{Br}^-\text{: cream} \quad \text{I}^-\text{: yellow}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Procedure: acidify a little of the solution with dilute nitric acid, add AgNO₃(aq) — white ppt AgCl (Cl⁻), cream AgBr (Br⁻), yellow AgI (I⁻) (master summary §8.4).
  • Why HNO₃ and not HCl: “HCl would introduce Cl⁻ → false positive” (master summary §8.4); nitric also destroys carbonate/sulphite interferents.
  • Confirmation ladder in ammonia: “AgCl dissolves in dilute NH₃; AgBr needs conc. NH₃; AgI dissolves in neither” (master summary §8.4).
  • Only 40% of 2015 candidates could give the Br⁻(aq) test (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3) — the three-colour trio is the most-missed one-mark in §C.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • 2012 §C scheme banked the bulk-scheme version: “Add excess AgNO₃(aq) to the solution to form AgCl(s) and AgI(s)” then “Wash the solid residue with excess ammonia solution to dissolve AgCl(s)” (ms bank §11 · 2012 2 Q3) — reagent plus solvent, one step each.
  • Colour words only: “milky white” is limewater’s; a halide line is “a white precipitate forms”. Some 2012 candidates offered “starch and iodine” here — recorded wrong reagent (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3).
XV (a)

Carbonate, sulphite, hypochlorite — the gas-evidence anions

$$\text{CO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \to \text{CO}_2\uparrow + \text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{SO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \to \text{SO}_2\uparrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • CO₃²⁻: add dilute acid → effervescence; the gas turns limewater milky (master summary §8.4).
  • SO₃²⁻: add dilute acid → gas with the pungent SO₂ signature; test with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ paper: orange → green (ms bank §11). Effervescence alone does NOT separate CO₃²⁻ from SO₃²⁻ — the gas test decides.
  • ClO⁻ (chlorate(I)) — Part I lists it but the cache prints no MS test sentence: 2016 “most of them were not able to give a chemical test for hypochlorite ions” (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3). Teach the defensible oxidising-anion register: acidify with dilute sulphuric acid and add KI(aq) + starch — ClO⁻ liberates I₂ (colourless → blue-black); or show bleach’s litmus-bleaching action. Marked as register, not as banked MS text.
  • Link home: these are the anions of Topics IV (carbonates) and VII/XI (bleach) re-taught as detection.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Acid choice matters: use dilute HCl for the carbonate gas test but for halide work acidify with HNO₃ — carrying HCl into an Ag⁺ tube is a self-inflicted white ppt.
  • Hypochlorite + acid liberates chlorine-type smell/toxicity — “in the fume hood” earns the risk awareness the Part I list demands.
XV (a)

Organic I — the C=C double bond and the –OH group

$$\text{Br}_2\text{(aq)/CCl}_4\text{: orange-brown} \to \text{colourless} \\ \text{(in the dark — no substitution)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • C=C: banked sentence — “Orange / brown Br₂ solution turns to colourless” (ms bank §5); shake the organic with bromine solution, in daylight-free conditions so substitution of alkanes cannot be credited.
  • Alternative: acidified KMnO₄(aq) purple → colourless (and in alkali: “MnO₄⁻/OH⁻: brown precipitate formed”) (ms bank §5) — also positive for –CHO, so position in the flow matters.
  • –OH (alcohol): acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ orange → green on warming with primary/secondary alcohols (ms bank §5) — tertiary alcohols stay negative; sodium metal giving H₂ (‘pop’ gas) is the Topic XI all-alcohol reaction.
  • Distinction discipline (2012 §C): “some candidates wrongly used the following terms interchangeably: ‘hydroxy group’, ‘alcohol’ and ‘alkanol’” (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3) — group ≠ compound class.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Write the full pair “decolourises / turns from orange-brown to colourless” — the bare word “orange” as an observation was recorded (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3).
  • 2,4-DNPH is for carbonyls only; bromine for C=C; keep the bottles mentally labelled.
XV (a)

Organic II — carbonyls: 2,4-DNPH & Tollens, conditions stated

$$\text{C=O} + \text{DNPH} \to \text{hydrazone precipitate} \\ \text{RCHO} \xrightarrow{\text{Tollens}} \text{RCOO}^- \,;\\ \text{Ag}^+\text{ reduced} \to \text{Ag (mirror)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Part I demands conditions + observations for exactly these two reagents (Part I right column): 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine — shake the sample with a few drops of the solution (in dilute sulphuric acid / methanolic reagent as supplied); a yellow / orange / red precipitate proves the carbonyl >C=O (ms bank §5).
  • Tollens’ reagent — add the sample to the reagent in a clean test tube and warm in a water bath; aldehydes give a silver mirror (grey deposit); ketones: no change (master summary §20.1).
  • Both carbonyl reagents together: DNPH says “carbonyl”; Tollens (or Fehling’s, blue → brick-red) says “aldehyde, not ketone”.
  • Banked negative: “2,4-DNPH reacts with aldehyde or ketone to give yellow / orange / red precipitate” — [Do not accept brown] (ms bank §5).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Testing for carbonyl group is the same as testing for ketone” — a recorded misconception; DNPH is positive for BOTH families (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).
  • A boiling water bath, not a naked flame — state “warm” if the question wants the condition. And the acid that closes the organic flow: –COOH effervesces with NaHCO₃(aq) (CO₂, limewater milky), the only one of the five groups that bubbles; “does not react with NaHCO₃(aq)” rules it out (master summary F.5; ms bank §5).
🧤 Risk register
  • Tollens’ reagent is alkalistic and must not be stored (explosive residues on drying); DNPH reagent is acidified — both: drops only, goggles on.

The test map — from an unknown to a justified conclusion

Four gates, in order. Every branch names its reagent and its banked observation; the instrument exit at the bottom is Engine D’s territory.

Gate 1 · What do I have?

Aqueous solution / solid? → inorganic ion route. Organic liquid? → functional-group route. Gas coming off a reaction → gas route. Do the physical looking first: colour of the solution already narrows cations (blue Cu²⁺, pale-green Fe²⁺, yellow/brown Fe³⁺, colourless the rest).

Gate 2 · Inorganic route

Cations: NaOH(aq) then excess → colours; the white-ppt-dissolves pair goes to NH₃(aq) then excess (Zn²⁺ dissolves, Al³⁺ does not) (master summary F.4). NH₄⁺: alkali + warm + damp red litmus. Anions: HNO₃ then AgNO₃ for halides; dilute acid + limewater for CO₃²⁻; acid + dichromate paper for SO₃²⁻. Solids that won’t discriminate: dissolve first (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q9).

Gate 3 · Organic route (the banked F.5 flow)

Br₂ solution colourless? → C=C. 2,4-DNPH ppt? → carbonyl; then Tollens mirror? → –CHO, else ketone. NaHCO₃ effervescence? → –COOH. K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺ orange→green with warm sample? → 1°/2° –OH. No reaction anywhere? suspect ester/haloalkane chains — beyond strand (a) iv’s five groups.

Gate 4 · When tests fail — go instrumental

The syllabus’s own reason list (Part I overview): colour already masks the endpoint (“the intense red colour of red wine may mask the colour of iodine/starch complex” — ms bank §11), species at too-low level, five similar white solids, or a “complete structure” wanted from a milligram. Then: IR for groups, MS for Mr + fragments, colorimetry for concentration → Engine D.

Multi-blank Quiz — The Chemical Tests Bank

1. Part I’s flame-test list names exactly four metals: calcium, copper, potassium, sodium; the wire is nichrome (or platinum), moistened with concentrated hydrochloric acid, and heated in a non-luminous flame (ms bank §11).
2. Banked flame colours: sodium gives an intense golden yellow, potassium a lilac — the mark scheme will not accept “deep purple” or violet (ms bank §11); calcium is brick-red and copper blue-green (master summary §8.2).
3. Gas tests: hydrogen — lighted splint → squeaky pop; oxygen — glowing splint relights; carbon dioxide — limewater turns milky (master summary §8.1).
4. Chlorine turns damp litmus paper bleached / white; ammonia turns damp red litmus blue — for the 2014 item the gas was hydrogen chloride, HCl(g), and testing the aqueous acid instead scored nothing (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).
5. The SO₂ sentence to recite: “Add a piece of filter paper which is soaked with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇(aq) to the gas. The filter paper turns from orange to green” (ms bank §11).
6. Water detection: anhydrous copper(II) sulphate goes white → blue; dry CoCl₂ paper goes blue → pink; heating hydrated copper(II) sulphate crystals, “the crystals change from blue to white / pale blue” with condensation at the tube mouth (ms bank §11).
7. With NaOH(aq) added in excess: Cu²⁺ gives a light-blue precipitate, Fe²⁺ a green one, Fe³⁺ a red-brown one — all three insoluble in excess (master summary §8.3).
8. The two white-precipitate cations whose precipitates dissolve in excess NaOH are Al³⁺ and Zn²⁺; aqueous ammonia separates them because only Zn²⁺ (Zn(OH)₂) dissolves in excess NH₃(aq) (master summary §8.3; ms bank §11).
9. Halides: acidify with dilute nitric acid (not HCl — it adds Cl⁻), add AgNO₃(aq): Cl⁻ white, Br⁻ cream, I⁻ yellow precipitates; in ammonia only AgCl dissolves in dilute NH₃ (AgBr needs concentrated; AgI neither) (master summary §8.4).
10. For the test tube scheme in the 2017 bank: “Dissolve the solids separately in water”, then “Add aqueous ammonia / NaOH(aq) until excess” — “White precipitate formed initially for all of them. But only the precipitate of ZnSO₄ dissolves in excess” (ms bank §11).
11. Carbonyl tests with conditions: 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine gives a yellow / orange / red precipitate (never accept “brown”) for aldehydes AND ketones; Tollens’ reagent, warmed in a water bath, gives the silver mirror only with the aldehyde (ms bank §5; master summary §20.1; CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3).
12. The carboxyl test: add NaHCO₃(aq) → effervescence of CO₂ (limewater milky); a compound that “does not react with NaHCO₃(aq)” is not a carboxylic acid (master summary F.5; ms bank §5); C=C is proven by bromine solution turning colourless (ms bank §5).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Chemical Tests

lilac vs “deep purple / violet”

K⁺’s flame is lilac; the bank explicitly rejects “deep purple and violet” (ms bank §11). Purple-ish words belong to MnO₄⁻ solutions, not potassium flames — keep vocabularies apart.

lighted splint vs glowing splint

H₂ needs a lighted splint (pop); O₂ needs a glowing one (relights). Swapping adjectives inverts which gas you appear to have found.

damp litmus vs dry / solution litmus

Gas tests run on damp paper — dry gas cannot ionise the dye. Conversely the 2014 §C trap: an aqueous-acid test given as the test for HCl(g) (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3). Gas in, gas out.

carbonyl test vs ketone test

2,4-DNPH detects the C=O family — aldehydes AND ketones. Calling a DNPH positive “a ketone” assumes half the evidence; Tollens’ silver mirror is the aldehyde discriminator (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).

“orange” vs “yellow/orange/red precipitate” (DNPH)

“Some candidates just stated the colour ‘orange’ as the observation” (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3) — the awardable unit is a precipitate in the yellow-to-red range; “brown” is explicitly not accepted (ms bank §5).

AgNO₃ acidified with HNO₃ vs with HCl

Self-poisoning: HCl carries Cl⁻, so every tube clouds. Dilute HNO₃ removes carbonate/sulphite interference without adding the precipitating ion (master summary §8.4).

hydroxy vs alcohol vs alkanol

Recorded interchange abuse (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3): the –OH group is (in this strand) named the hydroxy group; alcohol is the compound class in everyday speech; alkanol is the IUPAC-family word. The question tells you which register it wants.

observation vs conclusion

“A white solid forms that redissolves in excess ammonia” is an observation; “so Zn²⁺ is present” is the conclusion. Dumping product names inside the observation line (“ammonium gas is released”) loses the observation mark — gases are named in the conclusion, tested on their way out.

2017–2025 addendum · the small words in a test answer

Three registers the newer papers police: state the condition — “did not mention that acidic conditions should be required” (CP 2022 2 Q3 (a)(i)); wet the paper — for a gas test, candidates “did not mention that the testing paper must be moistened with water” (CP 2025 2 Q3 (a)(ii)); and write the reagent’s name, not a nickname — “Candidates should write ‘2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine’ instead of ‘2,4-DNP’” (CP 2024 2 Q3 (a)(i)). The protocol itself is still the scarce skill: a 2025 compulsory-part answer “Failed to mention the use of platinum wire or nichrome wire, conc. HCl as well as the necessity of using a non-luminous flame to heat the sample”, and some “Wrongly suggested using sulphuric acid or nitric acid to conduct the test” (CP 2025 Paper 1B Q2).

Pick a method

XV (b)

Read the mixture first — methods exploit properties

$$\text{mixture} \to \text{property gap} \to \text{method} \\ \to \text{justified choice}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Part I able-to pair: “devise a scheme to separate a mixture of known substances” + “justify the choice of an appropriate method” (Part I right column) — the justification names the property: solubility, boiling point, particle size, adsorption/partition.
  • The overview’s framing: “Instead of learning a number of tests and analytical methods, students can select the most appropriate means to solve problems in different situations” (Part I overview).
  • Four named methods in XV(b): crystallisation · distillation/fractional distillation · liquid-liquid extraction · paper, column or TLC; filtration and centrifuging return from Topics I/II as the workhorse first steps.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Wrong-property answers die: for purifying solid product Y in 2016, candidates “wrongly suggested the use of distillation as the final step in the separation” of a solid — distillation moves liquids (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Two-step answers need BOTH steps: 2013’s hex-1-ene/octane/water mix — “Many candidates were unaware that water is immiscible with hex-1-ene and octane, and the mixture would appear as two layers of liquids” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
XV (b)

Filtration & centrifuging — insoluble from liquid

$$\text{residue (on paper)} \;/\; \text{filtrate (through)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Exploits particle size: the insoluble solid cannot pass the paper’s pores. Vocabulary line: residue vs filtrate — both words score.
  • When the solid is a fine suspension, centrifuging packs it down first — the column-activity counterpart in Part I’s practical world; decant or pipette the supernatant.
  • The washing sentence: “Filter the mixture, wash with deionised water and dry the residue” — banked verbatim inside the 2012 gravimetric scheme (ms bank §11); wash removes adhering solution, otherwise the residue is contaminated.
  • Complete-precipitation check: a drop of precipitant into the clear filtrate — no further cloudiness means done.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Wash with deionised / distilled water” — washing with any reacting solution is a different operation and a different mark.
  • Fluted paper speeds drying — match the diagram to the stated purpose.
XV (b)

Evaporation & crystallisation — dissolved solid from solution

$$\text{solution} \to \text{saturated (crystals form on a glass rod)} \to \text{cool} \to \text{crystals}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The salt-making pair from Topic IV returns: “heat to concentrate → crystallise” (master summary §8.5 area: “evaporate some water — NOT to dryness — then cool”).
  • Crystallisation exploits the solubility–temperature gap: hot concentrated solution, slow cooling, well-formed crystals exclude most mother-liquor impurities.
  • Finish the job: filter, wash with a little cold water / suitable solvent, dry between filter papers.
  • Hydrated salts (e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O): evaporating to dryness drives off water of crystallisation — exactly why “not to dryness” is the rule.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Test sentence worth marks: “evaporate the filtrate until a saturated solution is obtained (crystals form when a glass rod dipped in the solution)” — then cool to crystallise.
  • “Evaporate to dryness” answers the wrong question whenever crystals, not powder, are wanted.
XV (b)

Recrystallisation — purifying an impure solid

$$\text{dissolve (hot, min. solvent)} \to \text{filter} \to \text{crystallise} \to \text{recrystallise again}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The solid dissolves readily in the hot solvent but little dissolves when cold; impurities either stay dissolved in the mother liquor or are filtered off.
  • Minimum volume of hot solvent, hot filtration if insoluble grit, slow cooling, repeat until constant melting point (purity criterion — Engine C).
  • 2016 §C: “Most of the candidates were not able to outline the experimental procedure in separating Y from the given mixture and wrongly suggested the use of distillation as the final step” (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3) — for a solid product the final step is crystallisation/recrystallisation, then dry.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • How do you show the crystals are pure? — measure the melting point and compare with the data-book value; sharp and equal = pure (Part I right column). 2016 records “a high proportion … not able to suggest a method to identify the solid obtained as pure Y” (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).
  • “Rinse the crystals with plenty of hot solvent” is a self-own — cold, small portions, or you redissolve the product.
XV (b)

Simple distillation — the solvent, not the salt

$$\text{solution} \xrightarrow{\text{boil}} \text{vapour} \xrightarrow{\text{condense}} \text{distillate (pure liquid)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Separates a volatile liquid from dissolved non-volatile solids — “Solvent from solution → simple distillation” (master summary §8.5); Topic IV’s sea-water picture: the salts are non-volatile and stay behind (CP 2013 Paper 1B context).
  • Labelled-diagram discipline (Topic V/XI drawing standards still mark here): thermometer bulb level with the side-arm, condenser water in at the bottom, out at the top, anti-bumping granules (“S is anti-bumping granules” was missed in 2013), open system never sealed (CP 2013 Paper 1B context; CP 2012 Paper 1B Q12 context).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • 2012 §B: “common mistakes were found in the drawings including wrong position of the thermometer and set-up of a closed system” (CP 2012 Paper 1B Q12 context) — practise the drawing until the three labels are reflex.
  • The distillate is the pure liquid; the residue in the flask is the concentrated solution — swap the words and lose the mark.
XV (b)

Fractional distillation — miscible liquids, different boiling points

$$\text{miscible liquids} \to \Delta\text{b.p.} \to \text{fractionating column} \to \text{fractions}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Two miscible liquids → fractional distillation (different boiling points)” (master summary §8.5); the column gives repeated vaporisation–condensation cycles so closer boiling points still separate.
  • The lowest-boiling component is collected first and richest in it — 2013 §C: “Many candidates knew that β-carotene would be the first collected fraction, but were unable to provide a correct explanation” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Crude-oil home (Topic V): fractions by boiling-point range; the property is boiling point — the recorded error “separation was based on the melting point of the alkanes” (CP 2014 Paper 1B Q6).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • If two liquids are immiscible, no column needed — separating funnel first (card 6); “fractional distillation” answers the solvent-extraction question only for the organic layer afterwards (banked chain, card 7).
  • Propene is not sitting in naphtha to be distilled out — 2013 §B recorded that exact error; cracking first, then fractional distillation (CP 2013 Paper 1B Q6 context).
XV (b)

Separating funnel & liquid-liquid extraction

$$\text{solute: prefers organic layer} \quad \text{layers: denser on the bottom}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Two jobs, one piece of glass. (i) Immiscible liquids: drain the lower layer, tap the top out the mouth. (ii) Extraction: shake the mixture with an immiscible solvent in which the wanted solute is more soluble — “Cinnamaldehyde is a non-polar compound which can dissolve in a relatively non-polar organic solvent like ethyl ethanoate” (ms bank §5).
  • Layer order by density, stated not guessed: halogenoalkanes/aqueous layer sit low; most hydrocarbons and ethers float. The banked work-up line says water was “the lower liquid layer” in that particular mixture (ms bank §11) — read the question’s chemistry, not a habit.
  • Shake with the funnel inverted and vent frequently — volatile solvents build pressure (risk line, Part I “assess possible risks”).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Extraction is a transfer step, not a finish: “Some suggested using solvent extraction with ether, but without mentioning the removal of the ether by distillation” — the missing sentence cost the mark (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
  • 2014 §C: “Many candidates did not mention the use of a separating funnel and the subsequent use of fractional distillation” for purifying T (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3) — apparatus names are marks.
XV (b)

The banked work-up chain — crude organic product

$$\text{separating funnel} \to \text{drying agent} \to \text{filter} \to \text{simple/fractional distillation}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Banked pair from a preparative scheme: “Use separating funnel to remove water (the lower liquid layer)” / “Carry out fractional distillation on the upper layer” (ms bank §11).
  • Before the final distillation the organic layer must be dried: add anhydrous MgSO₄ (or Na₂SO₄) to a clear organic layer — “about a third of the candidates failed to recognise anhydrous magnesium sulphate as the most suitable drying agent for ethyl ethanoate” (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Decant/filter the drying agent off, then distil over the thermometer setup; the fraction boiling at the product’s b.p. is collected.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Concentrated H₂SO₄ is NOT the “drying agent” here — it is the catalyst/dehydrating reagent inside the reaction; the post-separation water-remover is the anhydrous salt (register seen in §C answers).
  • Order marks: funnel → dry → distil. Writing “distil then separate” is a contradiction — the bank convention “award zero marks for answers which are contradictory” applies to scrambled sequences (ms bank, general conventions).
XV (b)

Paper chromatography — partition on cellulose

$$\text{spot mixture} \to \text{solvent rises} \to \text{components separate} \to \text{chromatogram}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Setup rules (all examinable): pencil baseline (ink would itself run); spots small and concentrated; solvent level below the baseline (or the spots dissolve into the reservoir); lid on beaker (vapour equilibrium); dry before measuring the solvent front.
  • Separation principle: each component partitions between the mobile solvent and the water held in the cellulose — better-soluble / less-adsorbed species ride higher.
  • Purpose bank: “Coloured substances → paper chromatography” (master summary §8.5); food dyes in soft drinks and ink analysis are the Part I activity exemplars (Part I activities).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Reading answers use comparison: “component A shares its Rf AND its colour with dye 2 in the standard, so the drink contains dye 2” — identification pairs chromatogram with reference run in the same solvent.
  • For colourless species the syllabus stops at detection methods beyond it — say “a locating agent is used to make spots visible” only when the stem supplies one.
XV (b)

TLC & column chromatography

$$\text{TLC: identify (fast)} \qquad \text{column: separate (collect fractions)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Same partition/adsorption logic as paper: TLC plate (adsorbent layer) runs in seconds, gives clean Rf values; Part I’s exemplar: “analyse the major components of selected over-the-counter analgesics by TLC” (Part I activities).
  • Column chromatography does the preparative job — adsorbent packed in a column, solvent runs through, components elute in order and are collected as fractions; “column chromatography” is a banked keyword (ms bank §11).
  • Banked contrast pair: “lycopene has a smaller Rf value / moves slower” (ms bank §11 · 2013 §C) — smaller Rf = more strongly held on the stationary phase.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The 2015 octane-in-petrol item wanted chromatography as the instrumental technique (gas chromatography) — only 20% said it (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3); match technique wording to the sample’s state (volatile fuel → GC; dye in solution → paper/TLC; bulk purification → column).
XV (b)

Rf — substance ÷ solvent, unitless

$$R_f=\frac{\text{distance travelled by the substance}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent front}}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Measure from the baseline to the spot centre, and baseline to solvent front; 0 < Rf ≤ 1 always (a spot cannot outrun the front) (master summary §8.5).
  • “Determine the Rf values of substances in a chromatogram” (Part I right column); Rf is a ratio → no units — “Many failed to realise that Rf has no units”, and report to sensible 2 s.f. (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Compare with values obtained in the same solvent; Rf changes when the solvent does — quote solvent with the number.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Sanity-check any Rf > 1 answer — the numerator and denominator were swapped (see traps). Live it in Solver Bench 1: spot 3.6 cm, front 6.0 cm → Rf = 0.60.
  • Two spots with identical Rf in one solvent are consistent with the same substance — full claim needs a second solvent or a co-spot; exam answers may state “run in different solvents to confirm”.

Scheme design, worked — three exam patterns

$$\text{scheme} = \text{steps in order} + \text{apparatus named} + \text{property per step}$$
🎯 Learning Goals

  • Hex-1-ene + octane + water (2013 §C): water is immiscible with the two hydrocarbons → separating funnel drains the aqueous layer → the hydrocarbon mix is separated by fractional distillation (boiling points) (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).

11. The 2015 §C fuel item’s instrumental separation technique is chromatography (gas chromatography) for the octane content of petrol — only about twenty percent of candidates named it (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Separation

Rf upside-down — solvent ÷ substance

The classic inversion gives Rf > 1 — physically impossible, since nothing outruns the solvent front. Numerator = substance, denominator = solvent front, both measured from the baseline (master summary §8.5).

Rf with units vs unitless ratio

“Many failed to realise that Rf has no units” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3) — cm/cm cancels. Write 0.60, not 0.60 cm, and mind significant figures.

Boiling point vs melting point (distillation)

Distillation reads the boiling point; “the separation was based on the melting point of the alkanes” is recorded wrong (CP 2014 Paper 1B Q6). Melting points belong to purity checks of solids (Engine C).

Crystallisation vs evaporating to dryness

Concentrate, cool, filter → crystals. Boil to a crusty floor and you get powder plus every dissolved impurity baked in — and, for hydrates, you strip water of crystallisation (master summary §8.5).

Miscible vs immiscible liquids

Fractional distillation for miscible (ethanol+water); separating funnel for immiscible (oil+water, the 2013 hex-1-ene case). Choosing the column when layers are staring at you lost the 2013 marks (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).

Distillation vs reflux

Reflux heats a reaction mixture with the condenser vertical — vapour returns, nothing collected (Topic XI/V protocols; in 2016 candidates “wrongly gave the drawing of a distillation set-up” when reflux was asked (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q13)). Distillation takes product away: condenser angled to a receiver.

“Separate” vs “purify”

A separating funnel separates phases; a product is purified only after drying + final distillation/recrystallisation. The 2014 §C answer line needed the whole train — funnel AND fractional distillation (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).

2017–2025 addendum · adsorb, and mind the starting line

adsorb = held on the surface of the stationary phase; absorb = taken into the bulk — “Some candidates confused the two words ‘adsorb’ and ‘absorb’” (CP 2025 2 Q3 (a)(iii)(2)). A separation sentence needs two phases and two properties: “different solubility and adsorptivity of the components in the mobile phase and stationary phase” (CP 2019 2 Q3 (c)(i)). The drawings fail on small parts: “Some candidates did not draw the starting position” (CP 2022 2 Q3 (b)(v)(1)), “the starting position of black ink spot should be higher than the solvent level” (CP 2024 2 Q3 (a)(ii)), and “Better if there is a cover” for the chamber (CP 2020 2 Q3 (b)(i)). Work-up omissions are still reported — “Some candidates did not mention the use of a separating funnel in liquid-liquid extraction” (CP 2021 2 Q3 (b)(i)), under half naming the funnel in 2023 (CP 2023 2 Q3 (a)(iii)) — and crystallisation fails on patience: “Did not mention that crystallisation requires a definite period of time”, with the finishing steps “washing the crystals with a small amount of cold distilled water” and “drying the crystals using a filter paper or a desiccator” omitted (CP 2023 Paper 1B Q9).

Acid choice in schemes (2015 §C)

Dilute nitric acid and hot concentrated sulphuric acid both dissolve copper itself (oxidising acids — Topic VII register), and heating with carbon drags metal oxide reduction into your scheme. The wanted-metal-aware choice is excess dilute H₂SO₄/HCl: the basic oxide dissolves; Cu, below hydrogen, does not (wrong list: CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).

Performing the titration — the procedure examiners read for

The titration technique flow — setup to result

Seven stations; the examiners grade at every one of them (method marks + recording marks + calculation step marks).

1 · Setup & rinse

Burette rinsed with titrant; pipette with its solution; flask with distilled water only. Leak-free tap; no grease plug in the jet.

2 · Zero

Fill above 0, open tap to expel the air bubble, adjust meniscus base to 0.00 cm³; read at eye level, 2 d.p.

3 · Aliquot

Pipette 25.00 cm³ analyte into the flask (filler, never mouth); add indicator; white tile. Moles-in-flask now fixed.

4 · Rough

Fast run, swirling, to first colour change; record to 0.01 anyway; note it as “rough”.

5 · Accurate repeats

Run to ~0.5 cm³ below rough, then dropwise, rinse splashes down, stop at the banked colour pair persisting.

6 · Concordance

Keep titring until two titres agree within ≤ 0.10 cm³. Exclude the rough; mean the pair to 2 d.p.

7 · Calculate

n = cV (standard side) → balanced equation ratio → unknown n → c or % → units (mg dm⁻³ if asked) → 3 s.f.

Error branch

Any “what effect” line is §18.2: analyte-rinsed flask ↑, water-filled burette ↑, bubble ↑, CO₂-loaded NaOH ↑, top-meniscus habit → no effect (systematic cancels).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Quantitative Analysis

“Concordant” vs “all the titres”

The mean is taken over titres agreeing within the concordance window (≤ 0.10 cm³ apart — school-lab convention), never over the rough run. Averaging everything biases the answer toward the deliberately over-run rough value.

Two decimal places vs one

“25.0” under-reads the burette; the graduation demands “25.00” (and a whole number gets “25.00”, never “25”). The SBA comments of 2014, 2015 and 2016 each report the one-decimal habit — it is a recording mark, free and forfeited alike (CP 2014/2015/2016 SBA comments).

Rinse the flask “ready” vs water-only

Analyte-rinsed flask = extra moles = larger titre (bank sentence); water-wet flask = harmless, moles unchanged. The trap is applying burette logic (never water) to the flask, where water is the ONLY correct rinse (ms bank §4; master summary §18.2).

added − excess vs added + excess

Back-titration counts what disappeared: reacted = added − excess. “Total = added + excess” describes a world where nothing reacted; excess − added goes negative. The bank’s line prints the order: 0.003 − 0.00102 (ms bank §11).

End point vs equivalence point

Equivalence = stoichiometric perfection; end point = what the indicator actually shows. Choose indicator so they coincide (steep pH jump region); answers that declare “at the end point the solution is exactly neutral” conflate the two (pH 7 is NOT the equivalence pH for weak-acid titrations).

mg dm⁻³ vs mol dm⁻³ (the unit the paper demanded)

“Some candidates did not express the total concentration of SO₂ in ‘mg dm⁻³’ as required … wrongly expressed the answer in ‘mol dm⁻³’” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3). The question’s unit is part of the question; “the unit must be correct” (ms bank, conventions).

“Red to yellow” vs red → orange (methyl orange)

The methyl-orange stop point is orange; riding on to yellow is an over-titration the bank refuses outright (ms bank §4 [NOT ACCEPT]; CP 2012 Paper 1B Q7). And methyl orange is already yellow in neutral/alkaline — the acid-side start colour is red.

2017–2025 addendum · the mean is a recorded value too

Two decimals are demanded twice over: on every burette reading and on the “reasonable average”. The 2017 §C item awarded the average only where it was written to two decimal places — “about a quarter of the candidates were able to calculate a reasonable … used in titration with two decimal places” (CP 2017 Paper 2 context) — and in 2025 the same mark was lost wholesale: “Some candidates did not recognise that the reasonable average volume should be written to 2 decimal places” (CP 2025 2 Q3 (b)(ii)). The 2021 compulsory part prints the target number itself — “Reasonable average volume of hydrochloric acid used = 27.27 cm³” — reached by only “About 1/4 candidates” (CP 2021 Paper 1B Q7). Set-up drawings keep failing on the same two details nine years apart: the missing stopcock and the beaker standing in for the conical flask (CP 2019 2 Q3 (b)(ii); CP 2025 2 Q3 (b)(i)). And an ion you forgot to precipitate is an error with a direction: in a chloride assay by silver nitrate, “Br–(aq) ions would also form a precipitate with AgNO₃(aq)”, so the calculated chloride comes out too high — only about a quarter argued it (CP 2025 2 Q3 (b)(iv)).

Pipette-safe vs heat-safe thinking (dilutions)

Accurate glassware measures; it does not absorb exotherms. Diluting conc. H₂SO₄ in a pipette/volumetric flask was a recorded proposal — acid to water, in a beaker, stirring, cool before transfer (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q4).

Instrumental Analysis — IR · MS · Colorimetry

Basic principles and applications — and nothing more, per the syllabus’s own depth cap. What this engine drills: read the spectrum, quote the range, write the plus sign, choose the right machine for the right question.

Pick a technique

XV (d)

Why instruments exist — the limits of test-tube chemistry

$$\text{tests: subjective} \to \\ \text{instruments: trace + objective}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Part I’s aim: students “should be aware of the limitations inherent in the use of conventional chemical tests … and hence appreciate the application of modern instruments” (Part I overview).
  • The limitation list writes itself from §C history: a coloured sample masks the end point (red wine vs iodine–starch (ms bank §11)), the species is too dilute for a titre (very dilute dichromate (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3)), the analyte is at trace level in a mixture (dioxin — GC-MS “can measure more accurately the low level of dioxin than using gravimetric analysis or volumetric analysis” (ms bank §11)), or five white solids look identical (Engine A’s comparison fatigue).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Suggest how the instrument overcomes the limitation” = one sentence pairing problem → capability. Naming instruments “arbitrarily … loose terms like computer and machine” scored nothing (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3).
XV (d)

IR — principle and how to read the axes

$$\text{bonds vibrate;} \ \text{IR absorbed at characteristic wavenumbers} \ (\text{cm}^{-1})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Basic principle only (depth cap, Part I overview): covalent bonds absorb infrared radiation at frequencies matching their vibrations — more absorption shows as downward peaks/troughs on the % transmission plot.
  • Axes: x = wavenumber / cm⁻¹ (usually 4000 → 400, decreasing left to right), y = transmittance. The unit line from 2013 §C: “The unit for wavenumber in the infra-red spectrum is ‘cm⁻¹’ … wrongly wrote ‘/cm’” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
  • You are never asked for instrument innards — source, interferometer, cells are out (“detailed operation procedure … not expected”).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • IR is the functional-group tool, per Part I’s own phrasing: “identification of functional groups of carbon compounds using IR spectroscopy” (Part I left column) — do not claim it gives Mr.
XV (d)

IR — the reading logic: presence AND absence arguments

$$\text{peak in range} \Rightarrow \text{bond present}; \quad \text{no peak in range} \Rightarrow \text{bond absent}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The verification pattern, banked from the 2021 scheme: distillate “shows C=C peak at 1610–1680 cm⁻¹ AND no broad O–H peak at 3230–3670 cm⁻¹ → product is alkene (cyclohexene), not alcohol (cyclohexanol)” (ms bank §5; master summary §12.1) — presence + absence together close the argument.
  • Elimination style (2013 §C): rule candidates X, Y, Z out by name: “does not show a broad peak at 3230~3670 cm⁻¹ corresponding to cyclohexanol” (ms bank §5); failure mode was “not able to present clearly how compounds X, Y and Z can be eliminated by making reference to the given characteristic ranges” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Absence also clears triple bonds: “absence of absorption peaks at around 2070~2250 cm⁻¹ … does not contain C≡C” (ms bank §5).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Always quote the range beside the bond (“strong absorption peak at around 1700 cm⁻¹ → C=O” (ms bank §5)) — a bare number or a bare bond name each half-lose the mark.

XV (d)

IR — comparing two spectra (cellulose vs chitin style)

$$\text{same} = \text{shared groups (O–H, C–H, C–O)};\quad \text{different} = \text{N–H / C=O of the amide}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • 2016 §C asked the similarity and difference between cellulose and chitin IR spectra — two-thirds managed it (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3): both show the broad O–H band; chitin additionally shows N–H (3350–3500) and amide C=O because its units carry –NHCOCH₃ (master summary §2 bonding bank: “Chitin consists of strong hydrogen bonds formed between C=O and N-H groups”).
  • Answer format: one line for the shared band(s) “both show…”, one for the diagnostic extra band “only chitin shows… corresponding to…”. The comparison clause carries the mark.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Bonding vocabularies again: IR reports bonds in the molecule; hydrogen bonding (why O–H is broad and shifted) is the intermolecular add-on — mention it only when the question asks about breadth/shift.
XV (d)

MS — the basics, capped on purpose

$$\text{M(g)} \xrightarrow{-e^-} \text{M}^+\text{(g)} \to \text{sorted by } m/z \to \text{spectrum}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Enough principle: vaporised molecules are ionised (electrons knock an electron out, M → M⁺ + e⁻), some fragment; positive ions are separated by m/z (mass-to-charge) and counted (master summary §1.5).
  • Lighter ions (lower m/z) are deflected more in the field — the sorting picture (master summary §1.5).
  • Everything deeper — source design, magnets, resolution — is “detailed operation procedure … not expected” (Part I overview).
  • Assumption to state when needed: most ions carry z = 1⁺, so the m/z value reads directly as the ion’s (relative) mass.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Relative quantities in MS are peak heights (abundance) — don’t mix chromatography vocabulary (“area under the peak”) into a §C MS line unless the question is chromatographic.
XV (d)

MS — the molecular ion gives Mᵣ (it is not “the tall peak”)

$$m/z_{\max} = M_r \quad (\text{the } \text{M}^+ \text{ peak, } z = 1)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The bank line is one sentence: “m/z of the molecular ion” = relative molecular mass (ms bank §1/§11); also “Molecular ion peak (M⁺) gives relative molecular mass” (master summary §1.5).
  • The base peak (tallest) is simply the most stable fragment — often not M⁺. Identifying Mᵣ from the tallest peak is this engine’s signature error; find the highest-mass significant peak (ignoring tiny isotope satellites M+1, M+2) instead.
  • Fragments are lighter pieces of the SAME molecule: every peak’s m/z is below M⁺, and neutral pieces may be lost without a trace on the spectrum.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • When two isomer-like liquids must be told apart by MS, the answer is different molecular-ion m/z or different fragment set — “not able to suggest how X and Y can be differentiated from their mass spectra” was 2016’s biggest §C blank (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).
XV (d)

MS — fragments: R⁺, RCO⁺, C₆H₅CH₂⁺ — all with a plus

$$\text{m/z } 91 \to \text{C}_7\text{H}_7^+ \quad 105 \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CO}^+ \quad 77 \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_5^+$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The Part I triad to recognise: R⁺ (alkyl cation: 15 → CH₃⁺, 29 → C₂H₅⁺, 43 → C₃H₇⁺) · RCO⁺ (acylium: 43 → CH₃CO⁺, 105 → C₆H₅CO⁺) · C₆H₅CH₂⁺ (benzyl, 91) (Part I right column); banked sentences: “m/z = 91 suggested the presence of C₇H₇⁺”, “m/z = 105 corresponds to C₆H₅CO⁺”, “77 … C₆H₅⁺” (ms bank §1).
  • Banking rule printed in caps: [Must show plus sign] (ms bank §1) — “C₇H₇” is a radical-neutral formula, not what the detector counted.
  • 2014’s m/z = 43 in a ketone: CH₃CO⁺ (acylium) — “recognise the chemical species responsible for signals at m/z = 43 and 134 to be positively charged” was half the answer (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Many candidates failed to put a positive charge onto the chemical species with m/z = 108 and 91” (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3) — the charge is the difference between deduction and dictation.
  • Where two ions share a mass (43 = C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺), use the molecule in front of you and say which fits the parent.
XV (d)

MS — isotope fingerprints: Cl 3 : 1, Br 1 : 1

$$^{35}\text{Cl}:^{37}\text{Cl} = 3:1 \Rightarrow M : M{+}2 = 3:1 \\ {}^{79}\text{Br}:^{81}\text{Br} = 1:1 \Rightarrow M : M{+}2 = 1:1$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • One chlorine: peaks at M and M+2 two units right, heights in 3 : 1 (master summary §1.5). One bromine: M and M+2 nearly equal height (1 : 1) from ⁷⁹Br : ⁸¹Br.
  • Reading tasks go both ways: given heights 99 : 32.5 → “contains one Cl”; given M⁺ 122 & 124 equal → “bromine present, Mᵣ read as the 79-Br peak = 122… state which isotope peak you used”.
  • The M+2 pair applies to every peak containing that halogen — the fragment ion carries its chlorine along.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not average M and M+2 into a decimal Mr when the question wants one molecule — the molecular ion of ³⁵Cl-chloromethane is m/z 78; the 3:1 pairing is the evidence, not a smearing licence. (Weighted Aᵣ thinking belongs to Topic II.)
XV (d)

MS vs IR — which one answers which question

$$\text{“what groups?”} \to \text{IR} \qquad \text{“how heavy / which pieces?”} \to \text{MS}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The 2015 §C item: two liquids X and Y (same functional groups, isomer-class reasoning) — “about sixty percent … were not able to state how X and Y could be distinguished from one another by mass spectrometry but not by infra-red spectroscopy” (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3): same-group isomers give near-identical IR bands, while different structures/weights give different M⁺ and fragment peaks.
  • 2016 §C reused the idea as “differentiated from their mass spectra” (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3) — name the spectral feature that differs.
  • Colorimetry answers neither question — it answers “how much?” for coloured species (card 10).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Randomly suggested instrumental methods such as infra-red spectrometry and mass spectrometry” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3) is the zero-scoring habit; the sentence must be method + measured quantity + expected difference.
XV (d)

Colorimetry — absorbance in, concentration out, via the curve

$$A \propto c \qquad \text{(calibration curve: } A \text{ vs } c \text{ of standards, straight through origin)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The able-to pair, verbatim: “construct a calibration curve by measuring absorbance of standard solutions” and “determine the concentration of a solution using a calibration curve” (Part I right column).
  • Protocol: series of standards of known concentration → measure each absorbance (same filter, same cuvette path) → zero with the solvent as blank → plot A vs c, draw best straight line → measure sample’s A under identical conditions → read c off the line.
  • Banked vocabulary: “colorimetry / use of colorimeter”, “absorbance / colour intensity” (ms bank §11); the proportionality claim the 2016 scheme paid: absorbance is directly related to [Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq)] (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never invent a numerical absorbance rule beyond the curve — reading the graph is the method; extrapolation outside the standard range is a red flag answer.
XV (d)

Colorimetry — filters, dilution, and the word itself

$$\text{solution absorbs colour } X \Rightarrow \text{use filter transmitting } X$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The 2016 §C filter question — “why a blue filter is used” for orange dichromate(VI) solution: orange solutions absorb blue light strongly, so the blue filter passes the wavelengths whose absorption changes most with concentration; “a very small number of the candidates” got it (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).
  • Second half of the same mark scheme line: colorimetry is “more appropriate than volumetric analysis” for a very dilute coloured solution — too little colour change for a trustworthy end point, plenty for the curve. The CP echo from 2014 §A: a suggestion to follow a reaction colorimetrically still failed because students didn’t state the starting solution is colourless (CP 2014 Paper 2 Section A context) — know what the instrument can see.
  • Word on the street: the instrument measures colour; “calorimetry” (heat measurement) was the recorded wrong word in 2016 §B (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q11).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Colorimetry needs a coloured species — colourless analytes first go through a colour-forming reaction (e.g. starch–iodine, Fe–SCN-type steps as the data give them) or stay in a burette.
XV (d)/(e)

GC & GC-MS — name, application, no mechanics

$$\text{GC: separate volatile mix} \;\to\; \text{MS sniff: identify each part}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Two banked application pairs: GC-MS for trace dioxin — written in full “Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry / GC-MS”, reason “can measure more accurately the low level of dioxin” (ms bank §11 · 2012 2 Q3); chromatography for octane content of petrol (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3); blood-alcohol GC appears as a suggested research activity (Part I activities).
  • Depth boundary: separation happens “in a column”, components arrive at different times (retention), and MS identifies them — mechanics, carrier gases and column design are out (not in the (d) list at all).
  • Forensic hook for (e): fingerprints via iodine sublimation and “chemical methods in forensic science” are Part I activities — the analyser role in “providing legal evidence” is the discussable line (Part I right column (e)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Write “gas chromatography”, not “a chromatograph” or “machine” — the 2012 report’s loose-terms complaint (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3).

The instrument chooser — what question is the paper asking?

Match the quantity demanded to the machine that measures it; then write the one-line justification that separates full marks from the “random suggestion” zero (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3).

“What is its Mᵣ?”

Mass spectrometry — read m/z of the molecular ion peak. Justification: “the m/z of the molecular ion gives the relative molecular mass” (ms bank §11).

“Which functional group(s)?”

IR spectroscopy — bands vs the correlation table; also the absence argument (“no broad 3230–3670 → not an alcohol”) (ms bank §5).

“How much (coloured, dilute)?”

Colorimetry + calibration curve — absorbance ∝ concentration; wins over titration at very low level (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3).

“What is in this volatile mix / trace pollutant?”

GC (or GC-MS) — octane in petrol, dioxin at environmental levels (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3; ms bank §11).

“Distinguish two similar molecules”

MS, not IR, when the group set is identical: different M⁺ / fragment pattern (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3). If groups differ, IR is the faster argument.

“One Cl or one Br?”

MS isotope pattern — M : M+2 of 3:1 (Cl) vs 1:1 (Br) (master summary §1.5); IR is silent on isotopes.

Multi-blank Quiz — Instrumental Methods

1. The IR x-axis unit is cm⁻¹ — writing “/cm” was recorded wrong (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3); deeper instrument mechanics are not expected (Part I overview).
2. Banked ranges: alcohol O–H 3230–3670 cm⁻¹ (broad); acid O–H 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ (very broad); C=O 1680–1800 (≈1700); C=C 1610–1680 cm⁻¹ (ms bank §11).
3. The 2021 verification sentence pairs a presence and an absence: the distillate shows C=C absorption at 1610–1680 cm⁻¹ and “does not show a broad peak at 3230–3670 cm⁻¹”, so the product is the alkene, not the alcohol (ms bank §5).
4. In mass spectrometry the molecular ion peak’s m/z gives the relative molecular mass (ms bank §11); the tallest peak is the base peak (most stable fragment), which need not be M⁺.
5. Banked fragments with the mandatory charge: 91 → C₇H₇⁺, 105 → C₆H₅CO⁺, 77 → C₆H₅⁺; the bank’s bracket note reads “[Must show plus sign]” (ms bank §1).
6. Isotope signatures: one Cl gives M : M+2 = 3 : 1; one Br gives 1 : 1 (master summary §1.5); the two peaks sit 2 m/z units apart.
7. Two isomers with the same groups are distinguished by mass spectrometry but not by infra-red spectroscopy, because their IR band sets (same bonds) coincide while M⁺/fragments differ (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).
8. Calibration-curve discipline: measure absorbances of standard solutions of known concentration, plot absorbance against concentration, then read the unknown’s concentration from its absorbance under identical conditions (same filter/cuvette) (Part I right column).
9. For very dilute Na₂Cr₂O₇(aq) the 2016 scheme preferred colorimetry over volumetric analysis, and the blue filter because orange dichromate absorbs blue light (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3); the relation paid was absorbance ∝ [Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq)].
10. Trace-level dioxin is best measured by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), “more accurately … than using gravimetric analysis or volumetric analysis” (ms bank §11); the octane content of petrol likewise chromatography (gas chromatography) (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).
11. The word-pair trap: detecting colour changes more accurately is done by colorimetry — 2016 candidates wrote “calorimetry” — heat, not colour (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q11).
12. Conventional-test limits that push you to instruments: the sample’s own colour masks the end point (red wine vs iodine–starch (ms bank §11)); species present only at low / trace level; and the need for objective, systematic data a machine records better than an eye (Part I overview).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Instrumental Methods

“M⁺ peak” vs “base peak”

M⁺ = the intact ionised molecule (highest significant m/z) → gives Mᵣ. The base peak is the tallest (most stable fragment) and can be far below M⁺. Taking the tallest for Mᵣ understates it silently — a classic §C trap.

C₇H₇⁺ vs C₇H₇

The detector counts ions; the bank shouts “[Must show plus sign]” (ms bank §1). Two Papers, 2012 and 2014, recorded charge-free formulas as the mass-spectrometry failure (CP 2012 & CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).

cm⁻¹ vs “/cm” vs wavelength

Wavenumber = cm⁻¹ (reciprocal cm) (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3); “/cm” reads as a nonsense unit; converting to λ is not asked — read the axis, quote the range.

C=O vs C=C near 1650 cm⁻¹

The two windows touch (C=O 1680–1800; C=C 1610–1680). Decide with the bank’s own pairing habits: a strong ~1700 band reads C=O; a weaker band at ≤1680 with no carbonyl chemistry in play reads C=C — and quote the given table’s range either way.

colorimetry vs calorimetry

One letter, two sciences: colour vs heat. “Calorimetry” as an instrument answer was recorded in 2016 (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q11); energy measurement belongs to Topic VIII’s calorimeter.

IR gives groups vs “IR gives the structure”

“Identification of functional groups” is the strand’s own words (Part I left column); claiming a complete structure from IR ignores every isomer sharing that group set — the 2015 item’s whole point (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).

3:1 vs 1:1 (Cl vs Br) at M+2

Chlorine: M : M+2 = 3 : 1; bromine: ≈ 1 : 1 (master summary §1.5). Swapping them converts 78.5 into “the other halogenoalkane”; a big M+2 with tiny ratio in between may mean Br+Cl or two Cl — say what the heights support.

Instrument as answer vs instrument as evidence

“Use a colorimeter” alone is the 2012 arbitrary-name zero; the paid sentence names what is measured (absorbance vs standards), the expected feature (peak at…, line through origin), and the limitation beaten (CP 2012 Paper 2 Q3; ms bank §11).

2017–2025 addendum · what vibrates, and what you call the machine

IR bands belong to bonds: 2025 records “the absorption peaks are the result of the vibration of a particular functional group instead of chemical bond” as the stated error, with only “a very small number of candidates … able to state the principle in producing absorption peaks in an infra-red spectrum” (CP 2025 2 Q3 (c)(ii)(1)) — one slogan, then straight to the table. Keep the axis vocabulary right: “Some candidates wrongly stated ‘wavelength’ instead of ‘wavenumber’” (CP 2024 2 Q3 (c)(i)), and keep IR’s words inside IR — the examiner’s exemplar note “Wrongly use the term absorption peak in mass spectra” (CP 2022 2 Q3 (a)(ii)). Name the instrument, spell it, and do not answer with the method: “Some candidates gave wrong spelling of ‘colorimeter’”, others “wrongly wrote the name of the method ‘colorimetry’ instead of the name of the instrument” (CP 2024 2 Q3 (c)(iii)(1)).

2017–2025 addendum · the calibration curve is a drawing with two lines

Sketch standards → absorbance, then show the read-off: “Below 1/2 of the candidates : able to sketch a labelled calibration curve and draw two lines on the calibration curve to show how the required concentration can be found” (CP 2022 2 Q3 (c)(ii)(1)). State the relationship completely — “only stated that the absorbance is proportional … without mentioning that it is directly proportional” (CP 2024 2 Q3 (c)(iii)(2)) — and finish the chain back through the dilution factor, which about three-quarters of the same cohort could not do (CP 2022 2 Q3 (c)(ii)(2)). Two reminders of scope: a colorimeter is not a pH meter (it appears in the record as a wrong answer to that question (CP 2022 Paper 1B Q3)), and the fragment lines must be written as ions — 2018’s exemplar note “Missing positive charge” on an otherwise correct deduction (CP 2018 2 Q3 (c)(ii)(2)), 2024’s neutral “HCO” writings (CP 2024 2 Q3 (c)(ii)), and the banked aromatic ion still being missed in 2021: the peak at m/z = 105 “corresponds to C₆H₅CO⁺” (CP 2021 2 Q3 (c)(ii)).

Topic XV Workbench

Five benches for the numbers this elective actually grades: Rf arithmetic, the titre-mean → concentration chain, the back-titration subtraction, reading Mᵣ from a mass spectrum, and the halogen isotope signature. Every output prints the working line first — exactly how the step marks (*) are earned (ms bank §11).

Bench 1

Rf calculator — substance ÷ solvent

Both distances are measured from the baseline. A result above 1 means the two boxes were swapped — the spot can never outrun the front (master summary §8.5). 2017–2025 check-up: under half of 2018’s candidates could say what an Rf value means (CP 2018 2 Q3 (a)(iii)); 2020’s script note asks for the number in the right form — “Better represent the answer in decimals” (CP 2020 2 Q3 (b)(ii)); and in 2025 about three-quarters got the value but “Some candidates did not show the step for the calculation” (CP 2025 2 Q3 (a)(iii)(1)) — the working line above is the mark.

Bench 2

Titre selection → concentration chain

The rough titre is never averaged; a pair is concordant when |Δ| ≤ 0.10 cm³ (school-lab convention — follow the paper if it prints its own rule) (recording discipline: CP 2014/2015/2016 SBA comments). 2017–2025 corroboration of the recording half only: “the reasonable average volume should be written to 2 decimal places” was still being missed in 2025 (CP 2025 2 Q3 (b)(ii)), the 2021 compulsory part made the average itself the hard mark — “Reasonable average volume of hydrochloric acid used = 27.27 cm³” reached “About 1/4 candidates” (CP 2021 Paper 1B Q7) — and 2017’s §C gave the two-decimal average to only about a quarter (CP 2017 Paper 2 context). No 2017–2025 file prints a numeric concordance window, so the 0.10 cm³ rule stays a school-lab convention.

Bench 3

Back-titration — added − excess (aspirin-labelled, illustrative)

Template banked from the scheme: “0.06 × 0.05 = 0.003”, “0.003 − 0.00102 = 0.00198”, “0.00198 × 136 ÷ 2.75 × 100% = 9.79%” (ms bank §11). Aspirin (Mᵣ 180.0, 1 : 1 with NaOH on hydrolysis) is illustrative framing — the IV(f)/XV rulings allow the analysis pattern, not the invented numbers (not_expected.txt IV, XI). 2021 corroboration of the pattern: the same added − excess chain ran in that year’s §C, where “About two thirds of the candidates were able to calculate the percentage by mass of A in the sample” (CP 2021 2 Q3 (c)(iii)(2)), while 2018 and 2019 saw only about a third to a half manage the equivalent percentage work (CP 2018 2 Q3 (b)(iv); CP 2019 2 Q3 (b)(iii)).

Bench 4

Mass-spectrum reader — M⁺ → Mᵣ, m/z → fragment

Banked: “m/z of the molecular ion” = relative molecular mass (ms bank §11); 91 → C₇H₇⁺, 105 → C₆H₅CO⁺, 77 → C₆H₅⁺ (ms bank §1) — the plus sign is mandatory [Must show plus sign]. 2017–2025 successors of that rule: the 2018 exemplar note “Missing positive charge” on an otherwise right deduction (CP 2018 2 Q3 (c)(ii)(2)); 2024, where the species at m/z = 29 had to be written as an ion and a neutral or doubled-sign answer was recorded (CP 2024 2 Q3 (c)(ii)); and 2021, where “the peak at … corresponds to C₆H₅CO⁺” was the mark the deduction turned on (CP 2021 2 Q3 (c)(ii)). On reading Mᵣ: 2023 shows the two steps split — about ⅔ got the molecular formula from M⁺, just under half the structural formula from the fragments (CP 2023 2 Q3 (c)(i)).

Bench 5

Isotope-pattern recogniser — Cl 3 : 1 · Br 1 : 1

Signature table: M : M+2 ≈ 3 : 1 → one Cl · ≈ 1 : 1 → one Br · M+2 tiny → neither (just ¹³C satellites) (master summary §1.5). If M is given, the bench also reports “M and M+2 are 2 units apart — ³⁵Cl/³⁷Cl (or ⁷⁹Br/⁸¹Br)”. Why this bench exists: 2019’s §C deduction turned on exactly that reading — “Some candidates failed to recognise the m/z peaks corresponding to 140 and 142 are due to the presence of the two isotopes of chlorine” (CP 2019 2 Q3 (c)(ii)).

Why this matters — the exam chain

§C runs the benches in order: purification paragraph → Bench 1 (spot ÷ front, no units) → titration table → Bench 2 (drop the rough, mean the concordant pair, c·V chain) → “insoluble/slow” twist → Bench 3 (added − excess) → spectra: Bench 4 M⁺/fragments (plus signs!), Bench 5 the M+2 halogen signature — the step-mark layout the schemes use (ms bank §11).

🫗 Titration rules card — recording, rinsing, concordance, calculation

Recording

  • Every burette reading and titre to 2 d.p. (estimate the last digit) — SBA complaint ×3 (CP 2014/2015/2016).
  • Table: trial | initial | final | volume used. Whole numbers still get 2 d.p.: “0.00”, “25.00”.
  • Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level; consistent top-reading is systematic and cancels in the difference (master summary §18.2).

Rinsing & set-up

  • Burette ← titrant · pipette ← its own solution · conical flask ← distilled water only (ms bank §4).
  • Analyte-rinsed flask → extra moles → titre too high; water-rinsed burette → diluted titrant → too high; jet air bubble → too high; NaOH loaded with CO₂ → too high (master summary §18.2).
  • Fill above zero, drain the jet, set 0.00 or record start.

Concordance

  • Rough first (fast), then accurate repeats dropwise near the end, white tile, swirl.
  • Concordant = titres within 0.10 cm³ (school-lab convention) — average only the concordant pair(s); the rough is NEVER in the mean.
  • Mean to 2 d.p.; carry unrounded; final answers typically 3 s.f. with correct units (mg dm⁻³ if asked (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3)).

End points (say both colours)

  • Phenolphthalein: colourless → pink (CP 2013 Paper 1B Q4) · methyl orange: red → orange, never “red to yellow” (ms bank §4; CP 2012 Paper 1B Q7).
  • KMnO₄ self-indicating: pale yellow → pale pink · iodine into starch/thiosulfate: colourless → dark blue (initial colour required (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3)).
  • Requirements of any titratable reaction: visible change · significantly complete · fast (ms bank §11).

The calculation chain

  • n = cV on the known side → balanced equation ratio → other side → c or %. Step marks per line (ms bank §11).
  • Back titration: reacted = added − excess (bank: 0.003 − 0.00102 = 0.00198 → 9.79%).
  • Gravimetric: mass of dry ppt → n via Mᵣ; name the method when asked (CP 2014 Paper 2 Q3).

Purity (the (b)/(c) bridge)

  • “Determine the melting point or boiling point … examine the purity” (Part I right column).
  • Pure solid: sharp mp at data-book value; impure: lower, over a range. Pure liquid: constant b.p.; mixture: range.
📈 IR / MS quick-read card — what to write when the spectrum appears

IR — bands (Data-Book ranges)

  • O–H alcohol 3230–3670 broad · O–H acid 2500–3300 very broad · N–H 3350–3500 · C–H 2840–3095
  • C=O 1680–1800 (≈1700) · C=C 1610–1680 · C≡C 2070–2250 · C≡N 2200–2280 (ms bank §11)
  • Sentence shape: “absorption at … → … bond present” AND “no broad peak at 3230–3670 → not the alcohol” (ms bank §5).
  • Unit: cm⁻¹, never “/cm” (CP 2013 Paper 2 Q3). IR = functional groups (Part I) — not Mᵣ.

MS — peaks (write the +!)

  • M⁺ peak m/z = Mᵣ (ms bank §11); tallest peak = base peak ≠ necessarily M⁺.
  • Fragments: 15 CH₃⁺ · 29 C₂H₅⁺ · 43 CH₃CO⁺/C₃H₇⁺ · 77 C₆H₅⁺ · 91 C₇H₇⁺ · 105 C₆H₅CO⁺ — Part I list: R⁺, RCO⁺, C₆H₅CH₂⁺ (Part I right column).
  • Isotopes: Cl → M : M+2 = 3 : 1; Br → 1 : 1; partner peak always 2 m/z up (master summary §1.5).
  • Same-group isomers: identical IR families, different MS — the 2015 §C discriminator (CP 2015 Paper 2 Q3).

Colorimetry — the curve card

Standards of known c → absorbance each → plot A vs c → straight line (∝) → sample’s A ↔ its c, same filter/cuvette (Part I right column). Orange dichromate uses a blue filter (absorbs blue) and beats titration when very dilute (CP 2016 Paper 2 Q3). Vocabulary: “absorbance / colour intensity”; the instrument is a colorimeter — calorimetry measures heat (ms bank §11; CP 2016 Paper 1B Q11).

☑ Master trap checklist (20) — tick each off aloud

☑ Rf = substance ÷ solvent front, from the baseline; a value > 1 means you inverted it.

☑ Rf has NO units and takes sensible s.f. (0.60, not 0.60 cm) (CP 2013).

☑ Chromatography set-up: pencil line, spots above solvent level, lid on, front marked while wet-dry-dry.

☑ Every fragment formula carries a plus sign: C₇H₇⁺ not C₇H₇ (ms bank §1).

☑ Mᵣ = m/z of the MOLECULAR ion; the tallest (base) peak can be a fragment.

☑ Cl 3 : 1, Br 1 : 1 at M : M+2, two mass units apart — never swapped.

☑ IR claims bonds in ranges (“strong ~1700 → C=O”); it does not give Mᵣ or a full structure.

☑ Quote the ABSENCE too: no 3230–3670 band → not the alcohol (ms bank §5).

☑ cm⁻¹ for wavenumber; colorimetry (not calorimetry) for colour measurement.

☑ Colour-word discipline: lilac · orange→green paper · pale yellow→pale pink · colourless→dark blue.

☑ Burette values to 2 d.p.; concordant pair (≤ 0.10) only in the mean; rough excluded — always.

☑ Rinses: burette+pipette with their solutions; conical flask with water ONLY; water-in-flask = no titre effect.

☑ Systematic vs random: consistent meniscus mis-reading cancels in differences; bubbles do not.

☑ Back titration: reacted = added − excess; a negative number means you flipped it.

☑ Answer in the unit the question demands (mg dm⁻³ ≠ mol dm⁻³) — “the unit must be correct”.

☑ NaOH is NOT a primary standard (deliquescent + CO₂); state BOTH properties if both are wanted.

☑ Work-up order: separating funnel → drying agent (anhydrous MgSO₄) → distillation; solids end at recrystallisation + mp.

☑ Fractional distillation sorts by boiling point; reflux drawings go vertical; neither is sealed.

☑ Test answers: reagent 1 mark + observation WITH comparison 1 mark; gases tested with damp papers, solids dissolved first.

☑ Instrument answers name the machine + what it measures + the expected feature — “randomly suggested” earns nothing.
