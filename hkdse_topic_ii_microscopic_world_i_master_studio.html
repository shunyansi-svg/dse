Topic II (a) Atomic structure

Elements, atoms, symbols and the three subatomic particles

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • An element is a substance made of atoms with the same proton number; it cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. An atom is the smallest unit of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction.
  • Compare relative charges (p: +1, n: 0, e: −1) and relative masses (p ≈ n ≈ 1, e ≈ 1⁄1836 — negligible).
  • Atomic number Z = proton number = electron number in a neutral atom; mass number A = nucleons = protons + neutrons.
  • Interpret and use symbols such as $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$: A on top, Z underneath.
  • Deduce p / n / e in atoms and ions: ²³Na⁺ → 11p, 12n, 10e; ¹⁶O²⁻ → 8p, 8n, 10e; neutrons = A − Z.
  • Classify elements as metals, non-metals or metalloids from their properties (some elements, e.g. silicon, show both characteristics).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

In ion questions the mark for electrons is lost when the charge is ignored: Na⁺ has 10 electrons, not 11. “Mass number = total mass of the atom” is NOT ACCEPT — A is a whole-number count of nucleons. Write ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ the other way round and every downstream count collapses. “An atom is neutral because it has the same number of protons and electrons” — saying “neutrons” earns nothing.

Topic II (a) Isotopes & relative atomic mass

Isotopes, isotopic masses and Aᵣ based on carbon-12 = 12.00

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons (e.g. ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl).
  • Isotopes of an element have identical electronic arrangements → almost identical chemical properties; different masses → slightly different physical properties.
  • Relative atomic mass Aᵣ: the mean mass of an atom of the element compared with 1⁄12 of the mass of one atom of carbon-12.
  • Calculate from isotopic composition: $A_r = \dfrac{\sum(\text{isotopic mass}\times\text{abundance})}{100}$ — chlorine: $\dfrac{35\times75+37\times25}{100}=35.5$.
  • Explain why Aᵣ can be a decimal while the mass number of any single atom is always a whole number.
  • Identify isotopes among given particles: same Z, different A (compare full $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ symbols).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The definition must mention atoms of the element and the ¹²C = 12 comparison — “average mass” alone caps the mark. Candidates quote 35.5 as the mass of one chlorine atom — rejected; it is the weighted mean over the natural mixture. Chemical-property questions about isotopes: answer with “same number of electrons / same electronic arrangement” — “same neutrons” or “same mass” are common wrong reasons noted by examiners.

Topic II (a) Electronic arrangement

Electronic arrangement of atoms (up to Z = 20) and the octet rule

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Fill shells in order 2, 8, 8, 2: K(19) = 2,8,8,1 and Ca(20) = 2,8,8,2 — not 2,8,9 or 2,8,10.
  • Represent arrangements with electron diagrams (rings with • or × for each shell, 1st shell holds max 2).
  • Valence electrons = electrons in the outermost shell; they determine chemical behaviour.
  • Noble gases He (2), Ne (2,8), Ar (2,8,8) are stable: full outer shell → octet rule (dulet for He).
  • Atoms gain / lose electrons to reach noble-gas arrangements: Na → Na⁺ (2,8), Cl → Cl⁻ (2,8,8), O → O²⁻ (2,8), Mg → Mg²⁺ (2,8).
  • Deduce arrangements of atoms and simple ions with Z ≤ 20 and state which noble gas they match.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

CP records wrong outermost-shell electrons and charge for ions such as O²⁻ (CP 2015 Q10) — check which particle the question asks for, then draw its arrangement. An ion diagram must show square brackets and the charge; without them the diagram cannot earn the mark. “All noble gas atoms have octet outer shells” is wrong — helium’s stable arrangement is 2. Sub-shells / orbitals are not required anywhere in Topic II (2,n notation only).

Topic II (b) Periodic Table I

The Periodic Table: arrangement, groups and periods from electronic structure

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Modern arrangement: elements in order of ascending atomic number (not mass number).
  • Period number = number of occupied electron shells; group number (I, II, III) = number of valence electrons — define both terms precisely.
  • Locate an element from its electronic arrangement and vice versa: 2,8,7 → period 3, group VII → chlorine.
  • Group VII elements have 7 valence electrons; group 0 elements have stable arrangements (hence “inert”); He sits in group 0 with arrangement 2.
  • Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons.
  • The Periodic Table is a systematic summary — appreciate why chemists use it to organise elements.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Arranged in order of atomic mass” is a classic rejected statement. When asked “to which group does X belong?”, the mark requires the reasoning “same number of valence electrons as …” — a bare group number without justification scores the location mark only. Groups 4–7 transition columns use Roman numerals I, II, … VII, 0 in HKDSE papers; “group 17” is understood but answer in the Table’s own notation.

Topic II (b) Groups I, II, VII, 0

Family chemistry: reactivity of Groups I, II, VII and 0 — and predicting unfamiliar elements

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Group I (Li, Na, K): soft metals; with water → alkaline solution + hydrogen; reactivity increases down the group.
  • Group II (Mg, Ca): react with dilute hydrochloric acid → salt + hydrogen; calcium reacts more vigorously than magnesium — reactivity increases down the group.
  • Group VII (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂): coloured diatomic non-metals; reactivity (oxidising power) decreases down the group — displacement: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.
  • Group 0: monatomic, unreactive — stable electronic arrangements.
  • Explain trends: further down, outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus, more shielded → weaker attraction → easier to lose (metals) / harder to attract an electron (non-metals).
  • Predict properties of unfamiliar elements (e.g. rubidium, astatine, francium) from group patterns + the down-group trend, with comparisons.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The classic error in this strand: “halogens become more reactive down the group”. Fluorine and chlorine are the reactive ends. For metal–water reactions the observation marks want specific wording: floats / moves rapidly / fizzes / dissolves / phenolphthalein turns pink in the alkaline solution — “it reacts” earns nothing. Prediction answers must follow “similar to … but more/less … because …”; copying sodium’s facts onto rubidium without the trend clause is capped.

Topic II (c, d) Metallic bonding

The simple model of metallic bond and the properties of metals

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Metallic bond: the electrostatic attraction between delocalised electrons and the positive metal ions arranged in a giant (lattice) structure.
  • General properties of metals: good conductors of electricity and heat, malleable / ductile, high melting and boiling points, shiny, sonorous.
  • Conduction: delocalised electrons are mobile and carry charge through the lattice.
  • Malleability: layers of metal ions can slide over each other without breaking the (non-directional) metallic bonds.
  • Strength of the bond (hence melting point, hardness) increases with more delocalised electrons / smaller ions: e.g. Al vs Na — aluminium conducts better and has a higher melting point.
  • Relate each use (cookware, wiring, structural alloys) back to the corresponding property.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Metallic bonds form between metal atoms by sharing electrons” is NOT ACCEPT — the attraction is between ions and delocalised electrons. For malleability, “the bonds are non-directional / layers slide” scores; “the atoms are arranged regularly” alone does not. Say “mobile / delocalised electrons carry charge” — never “free ions carry current in the solid”, a persistent wrong model from Candidate Performance reports.

Topic II (e) Ionic bonds

Electron transfer: ions, ionic compounds, formulae and names

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Ionic bond: the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when electrons are transferred from a metal atom to a non-metal atom.
  • Draw electron diagrams of cations and anions ([ Na⁺ ] [ :Cl: ]⁻ style) showing transferred electrons and charges.
  • Predict ion charges from group number: Group I → +1, II → +2, III → +3; VI → −2, VII → −1.
  • Polyatomic ions: OH⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, NH₄⁺ — recognise names, formulae and charges.
  • Construct formulae by balancing charges: Mg²⁺ + Cl⁻ → MgCl₂; Na⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Na₂SO₄; interpret formulae as ion ratios.
  • Name ionic compounds from ions (sodium chloride, copper(II) sulphate) and describe common ion colours in solution: Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ pale green, Fe³⁺ yellow/brown, MnO₄⁻ purple, Cr₂O₇²⁻ orange.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Electron diagrams without square brackets and charges cannot earn the mark — CP logged a “covalent-like” diagram handed in for ionic Na₂O (CP 2015 Q10). “Ionic bond = attraction between atoms” is NOT ACCEPT; the word ions is required. Write charges as 2+ / 2− (magnitude before sign). Formula errors “MgCl” and “NaSO₄” are the most common wrong answers in formula-construction items.

Topic II (e) Covalent bonds

Shared pairs: single, double, triple and dative covalent bonds — naming and mass calculations

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Covalent bond: the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the two bonded atoms; formed between non-metal atoms.
  • Draw electron diagrams for H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄, CO₂ — single, double (two shared pairs) and triple (three shared pairs) bonds, with all lone pairs shown.
  • Dative covalent bond: the shared pair comes from one atom only — describe NH₄⁺ formation from NH₃ + H⁺ and H₃O⁺ (the only examples required).
  • Names & formulae of covalent compounds using prefixes: dinitrogen tetroxide N₂O₄, carbon monoxide CO, sulphur dioxide SO₂; interpret formulae as atom ratios.
  • Formula mass (ionic substances, e.g. NaCl) vs relative molecular mass (molecular substances, e.g. H₂O) — define, distinguish, and calculate from Aᵣ values.
  • Communicate ideas with correct symbols: every atom in the formula counted, e.g. (NH₄)₂SO₄ contains 2 N, 8 H, 1 S, 4 O.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

CP records most candidates unable to draw the CO₂ electron diagram (CP 2014), and lone-pair omissions in Cl₂O/PCl₃ diagrams (CP 2015 Q10; 2016 Q4). In a dative-bond diagram the arrow (→) shows electron origin only — after formation it is identical to any covalent bond. Use “formula mass” for NaCl — calling it a “molecule of sodium chloride” loses the definition mark. Aᵣ/Mᵣ are unitless; “Mr of water = 18 g” is NOT ACCEPT (that is molar mass).

Topic II (f–i) Structures & properties

Giant ionic · simple molecular · giant covalent · giant metallic — structure ↔ property ↔ application

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Giant ionic (NaCl, CsCl): lattice of ions, each ion surrounded by ions of opposite charge; high mp/bp, hard but brittle (like-charges forced together on displacement → repulsion), conducts only when molten / in solution.
  • Simple molecular (CO₂, I₂): strong covalent bonds within molecules but weak van der Waals’ forces between molecules → low mp/bp, non-conductors.
  • Giant covalent: diamond (4 bonds/C, tetrahedral — hardest, insulator), graphite (3 bonds/C, hexagonal layers + one delocalised electron per carbon — conducts along the layers, slippery; sublimes above ≈3600 °C), quartz SiO₂ — all very high melting (“millions of strong covalent bonds must be broken”).
  • Giant metallic: see card 6 — mobile electrons, malleable.
  • The master question when melting/boiling: which bonds / which forces are overcome? Never “covalent bonds break when iodine boils”.
  • Deduce structure type from physical data (mp, conductivity, solubility, hardness) and explain applications (diamond cutting, graphite electrodes/lubricant, copper wiring, salt electrolysis).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

A commonly reported fatal line “the covalent bonds between the molecules are weak” — between molecules the forces are van der Waals’, and covalent bonds are intramolecular. “Graphite has a simple molecular structure” is a recorded wrong answer (candidates who wrote it scored zero on data-interpretation items). Explain, don’t list: a property with no structure link earns no explanation mark. For conductivity specify where the mobile electrons come from (delocalisation in metals/graphite) — “it has electrons” is NOT ACCEPT.

🚫 Out of scope for Topic II (Notes-for-Teachers depth caps — do not test here)

Electronic arrangement beyond Z = 20; sub-shells / orbitals; molecular shapes and bond angles; polarity and hydrogen bonding — all Topic VI. Of van der Waals’ forces this topic only requires you to recognise they exist between molecules and to use the given comparison “greater molecular size → stronger vdW” (e.g. CS₂ vs CO₂); the general strength factors and their classification are Topic VI. Dative covalent is required only via H₃O⁺ and NH₄⁺. (scope wording: Part I of the 2018 supplement, pp. I-4…I-7 — Topic II has no Part II depth rows)

II (a)

🎯 Learning Goals

💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never quote A as “the mass of the atom” — it is a nucleon count (unitless).

II (a)

Isotopes

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Isotopes: same proton number, different nucleon (neutron) number.
  • Same electronic arrangement → almost identical chemical properties; different mass → slightly different physical properties.
  • Identify isotope pairs among particles given as full $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ symbols (match Z, differ A; watch ionic charges — same Z and A but different charge is an ion pair, not isotopes).

Atom Arithmetic — one hub, four routes

Every Topic II particle question is a short trip through Z, A and charge. Master the hub and 1A becomes mechanical.

Route 1 · From symbol to counts

$^{A}_{Z}\text{X}^{c+}$ → p = Z, n = A − Z, e = Z − c. Example ³²S²⁻: 16p, 16n, 18e.

Route 2 · From counts to identity

Z alone names the element; A only picks the isotope. Two particles with same Z, different A → isotopes. Same A different Z → different elements, same nucleon count.

Route 3 · From Z to arrangement

Layer 2 → 8 → 8 → (2): write “2,8,x”; last number = valence electrons = group I/II/…VII; number of shells = period.

Route 4 · From arrangement to ion

1, 2 or 3 valence e⁻ → lose → X⁺/X²⁺/X³⁺; 5, 6, 7 → gain → X³⁻/X²⁻/X⁻; 8 (or He 2) → stays. All ions match a noble gas.

Multi-blank Quiz — Atomic Structure

1. A proton has relative charge +1 and relative mass 1; an electron has relative mass 1/1836.
2. The atomic number of an element is the number of protons in one of its neutral atoms.
3. The mass number of an atom is the total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in its nucleus.
4. An atom of phosphorus-31 (Z = 15) contains 15 protons, 16 neutrons and 15 electrons.
5. An oxide ion ¹⁶O²⁻ contains 8 protons and 10 electrons — the two extra electrons come with the 2− charge.
6. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
7. Isotopes of an element have almost identical chemical properties because they have the same electronic arrangement (same number of electrons).
8. Relative atomic mass compares the mean mass of an atom of the element with 1/12 of the mass of one atom of carbon-12.
9. Chlorine is 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl, so Aᵣ = 35.5 — obtained as (35×75 + 37×25) ÷ 100.
10. The electronic arrangement of potassium (Z = 19) is 2,8,8,1 — the third shell accepts only 8 electrons before the fourth starts.
11. Mg²⁺ has arrangement 2,8, the same as the noble gas neon; its electron diagram must be drawn with square brackets and the charge.
12. Noble gases are unreactive because their atoms have a stable octet (full outer shell); helium’s stable arrangement is 2 — a dulet, not an octet.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Atomic Structure

Mass number vs relative atomic mass

A is a whole-number count (p+n) in one atom. Aᵣ is the weighted mean over the natural isotopes — can be a decimal. Writing “Aᵣ of ³⁵Cl = 35.5” mixes the two.

Isotopes vs ions

Isotopes differ in neutrons, same charge balance. Ions differ in electrons. ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl: isotopes. Cl⁻ and Ar (18 e⁻): isoelectronic — neither isotopes nor an ion pair of the same element.

²³Na vs ²³Na⁺

Identical nucleus (11p, 12n) — different electron count (11 vs 10) and completely different properties: sodium atom reacts violently with water; Na⁺ sits happily in seawater.

Arrangement of Cl vs Cl⁻

Cl: 2,8,7 — one short of octet. Cl⁻: 2,8,8 + brackets + charge. Exam stems name the exact particle; drawing the wrong one is CP’s top reported slip in this topic.

Weighted mean vs plain average

(35+37)/2 = 36 is wrong for chlorine: abundances are 75/25, so Aᵣ = 35.5. Weighting by abundance is the whole point of the calculation.

Octet for every noble gas?

Helium (2) is stable with a full first shell; “all noble gases have 8 valence electrons” is the statement examiners reward zero for. Say “stable (duplet/octet) electronic arrangement”.

  • For “why do Na and K react similarly?”, the mark is on “same number of valence electrons” — “same number of shells” is a wrong answer.
II (b)

Period number = occupied shells

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Count occupied shells in the arrangement to get the period (within Z ≤ 20).
  • Down a period the valence count climbs 1 → 8 across; down a group the shell count climbs.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Keep group/period reasoning separate in answers: group from valence, period from shells — swapping them is the standard zero.
II (b)

Electronic structure ↔ position (two-way)

  • Deduce likely ion charge from group; verify with noble-gas arrangement after loss/gain.
💡 Examiner Tips

II (b)

Group I — lithium, sodium, potassium

$$2\text{Na}(s)+2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)\to 2\text{NaOH}(aq)+\text{H}_2(g)$$

  • Soft, low-density metals; stored under oil; each atom has one valence electron → M⁺ ions, MOH alkaline solutions.

  • Observation marks need two senses: movement + fizzing + shrinking/disappearing + (with litmus) colour change. “Exothermic” alone is not an observation.

  • Reactivity increases down the group (same shielding argument as Group I).

II (b)

Group VII — chlorine, bromine, iodine

$$\text{Cl}_2(aq)+2\text{KBr}(aq)\to 2\text{KCl}(aq)+\text{Br}_2(aq)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Diatomic coloured non-metals (Cl₂ pale green-yellow, Br₂ reddish-brown, I₂ grey-black solid / violet vapour); each needs one electron → X⁻.
  • Reactivity — the ability to attract an electron into the outer shell — decreases down the group: outer shells larger → the nucleus attracts an incoming electron less strongly.

💡 Examiner Tips

Group 0 — helium, neon, argon

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Monatomic and unreactive: full outer shell — no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons.

II (b)

Predicting unfamiliar elements

$$\text{Rb (Group I, period 5)}:\ \text{like Na/K but MORE reactive};\ 2\text{Rb}+2\text{H}_2\text{O}\to 2\text{RbOH}+\text{H}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Template: “similar to X because same group (same valence e⁻); more/less reactive than X because of the down-group trend.”
  • Apply to astatine (At, group VII: solid, coloured, less reactive than iodine, forms At⁻) and caesium (Cs: soft, explosive with water, most reactive).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Full marks need BOTH halves: analogy and direction of the trend with the shielding reason. Copying sodium’s observations onto rubidium with no comparison is capped at half.

Arrangement → Address: read any element’s postcode

Two questions, one direction each: “how many shells?” gives the street (period); “how many outside?” gives the house number (group).

From arrangement to position

2,8,2 → 3 shells → period 3; 2 valence → group II → Mg. 2,8,6 → period 3 group VI → S. 2,8 → period 2 group 0 → Ne.

From position to expected behaviour

Group II → loses 2 e⁻ → X²⁺ → chloride XCl₂, oxide XO. Group VII → gains 1 e⁻ → X⁻ → hydrogen compound HX (the halide-ion test belongs to a later topic).

Down a group — metals

More shells → valence electron further + more shielded → lost more easily → more reactive (Group I, Group II).

Down a group — halogens

More shells → nucleus attracts an incoming electron less strongly → reactivity decreases; the displacement direction Cl₂→Br₂→I₂ is fixed in this order.

Same-period neighbours

Across a period, valence electrons rise 1→8 while shell count stays — metals (left) → metalloids → non-metals (right); their oxides’ acid–base behaviour changes too, but that comparison belongs to Topic XII.

Multi-blank Quiz — Periodic Table

1. In the modern Periodic Table, elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, not (relative) atomic mass.
2. An element with arrangement 2,8,1 is in period 3 and group I; it is sodium.
3. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence (outer-shell) electrons.
4. The group number for groups I, II and VII equals the number of valence electrons; the period number equals the number of occupied electron shells.
5. Potassium with water: it floats, moves rapidly on the surface, fizzes and finally dissolves; the solution turns red litmus paper blue because KOH (alkaline) forms along with hydrogen.
6. Reactivity of Group I metals increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus , more shielded, so it is lost more easily.
7. Chlorine water added to potassium bromide solution turns the solution orange/red-brown because bromine is displaced: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.
8. Halogen reactivity decreases down group VII because the atoms attract an incoming electron less strongly as the shells get larger.
9. Helium is placed in group 0 even though it has only 2 valence electrons — its first shell is already full
10. Rubidium (below potassium) reacts with water more vigorously than potassium, forming RbOH and H₂ — same group, so same behaviour, but stronger down the trend.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Periodic Table

Group vs period

Group = column = valence electrons. Period = row = shells. For “3 shells, 2 valence”: period 3 group II — reversing them lands you on the wrong element every time.

Similarity reason

Li & Na react alike — because same valence electrons (1). Not “same number of electrons” (Li has 3, Na 11) and not “same shells” (2 vs 3). The MS phrase is “same number of valence electrons”.

Trend direction: metals vs halogens

Metals: reactivity increases down the group (outer electron further from the nucleus and more shielded → lost more easily). Halogens: reactivity decreases (an incoming electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded → attracted less strongly).

Displacement wording

Cl₂ displaces Br⁻ from solution — say “more reactive halogen displaces less reactive halide (ion)”, not “displaces bromine”. The halide is the species in solution; bromine is the product.

Atom vs ion arrangement in MC stems

“X²⁺ has arrangement 2,8”: the ion has 10 electrons, so the atom had 12 → Z = 12 (magnesium). Recover Z before reading the position; treating the ion as the atom shifts everything.

Atomic number vs mass number ordering

The Table orders by Z (proton count). Argon (18) sits before potassium (19) even though Ar has the larger mass number — proof mass order would break the group patterns.

II (e)

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Dative covalent bond: a shared pair in which both electrons come from one atom (the donor’s lone pair); H⁺ contributes an empty shell.
  • Show NH₄⁺ with an arrow N→H on the donated bond, brackets and the overall + charge.
  • After formation the dative bond is identical to any other covalent bond (all four N–H equivalent).

  • “Different kind of bond” is wrong — MS accepts “same once formed”. The arrow is bookkeeping for the electron’s origin only, and its direction is examinable: donor → acceptor (N→H in NH₄⁺); a reversed arrow scores nothing.

  • Metal atoms release their valence electrons → positive ions in a giant lattice immersed in mobile, delocalised electrons.
  • Metallic bond = electrostatic attraction between the metal ions and the delocalised electrons.
  • Explains: conductivity (mobile e⁻), malleability (layers slide, bonds non-directional), high mp (strong attraction).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Compare Na vs Al: Al supplies more delocalised electrons per ion + higher charge → stronger bond, better conductivity, higher mp (classic 1B data item).
II (e)

Formula mass vs relative molecular mass

$$M_r(\text{H}_2\text{O})=2(1)+16=18\ (\text{relative molecular mass})$$ $$\text{formula mass of NaCl}=23+35.5=58.5\ (\text{no discrete molecules})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Define and distinguish: formula mass (ionic or network substances, summed over the formula unit) vs relative molecular mass (a molecule).
  • Carry out calculations including hydrates: CuSO₄·5H₂O = 63.5+32+64+5(18) = 249.5.
  • Both are unitless ratios; molar mass = same number, g mol⁻¹.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Relative molecular mass of sodium chloride” is loose language examiners watch for — use formula mass for ionic compounds.
  • Count every atom: (NH₄)₂SO₄ = 2N + 8H + S + 4O — bracket-first discipline.

Which bond forms? — the decision route

Ask where the electrons can go. Metals give them, non-metals want them, and two non-metals compromise by sharing.

Metal + non-metal → ionic

Electrons transferred; oppositely charged ions attract in a giant lattice. NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂.

Non-metal + non-metal → covalent

Neither side loses; pairs shared. One, two or three pairs = single/double/triple. H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, O₂.

One atom donates the whole pair → dative

Lone-pair donor + recipient with empty shell: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺; H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺. Identical to a normal covalent bond after formation.

Metal + metal → metallic

Valence electrons delocalise across the whole lattice: attraction between the metal ions and the electron sea.

The bond-strength ladder you must not confuse

Strong: ionic / covalent / metallic bonds (within the lattice or molecule). Weak: van der Waals’ forces, which act only between molecules and are what melting/boiling overcomes in simple molecular substances — Engine D applies this ladder to melting points.

Multi-blank Quiz — Bonding

1. An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed by transfer of electrons.
2. When magnesium reacts with oxygen each Mg atom loses 2 electrons and each O atom gains 2 electrons, so MgO contains Mg²⁺ and O²⁻.
3. Electron diagrams of ions must be drawn with square brackets and the charge shown outside them.
4. The formulae: calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)₂; aluminium oxide Al₂O₃; iron(III) sulphate Fe₂(SO₄)₃.
5. Aqueous Cu²⁺ is blue, Fe²⁺ is pale green, and Cr₂O₇²⁻ is orange.
6. A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of both atoms.
7. A molecule of oxygen contains a double bond (two shared pairs); nitrogen contains a triple bond.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Bonding

Transfer vs share

Ionic: electrons move from metal to non-metal. Covalent: pairs are shared between non-metals. “NaCl shares electrons between Na and Cl” is NOT ACCEPT; “H₂O forms hydrogen ions when it bonds” confuses bonding with acidity.

Bond between ions vs between molecules vs atoms

Ionic bonds join ions; covalent bonds join atoms in a molecule; metallic bonds join ions to the electron sea. No bond is ever “between molecules” — that is a van der Waals interaction.

Dative bond vs “stronger bond”

The dative bond differs only in the source of the pair; after formation it is indistinguishable from the other three N–H bonds. It does NOT “break easily to give back H⁺” as a weaker bond — NH₄⁺ loses H⁺ by reaction, not by bond-type weakness.

Valence electrons vs ion charge

Sulphur has 6 valence electrons, but the ion is 2− (needs two more), not 6−. Metal ions: charge = valence count lost (Al 3 → Al³⁺).

Formula mass vs molecular mass

Use “formula mass” for ionic solids (NaCl has no molecules); “relative molecular mass” only where molecules exist (H₂O, CO₂). Both unitless.

Lone pairs in diagrams

Shared pair ≠ all the electrons. Cl₂: 7 + 7 valence electrons = one bond pair plus three lone pairs on each chlorine. Missing lone pairs cost marks even when the bond dots are right (CP 2015 Q10; CP 2016 Q4).

Metallic “sharing” vs covalent sharing

Metals delocalise electrons across the whole lattice; covalent pairs are localised between two atoms. “Metal atoms share electrons with their neighbours” describes diamond, not copper.

The Melting Question — ask what the particles actually are

Every mp/bp/conductivity item reduces to: name the structure, name the forces, say which ones the heat must overcome.

Giant ionic (NaCl, CsCl)

Melting overcomes ionic bonds across the lattice → very high mp. Solid: fixed ions, no conduction. Molten/aqueous: mobile ions → conducts.

Simple molecular (CO₂, I₂)

Melting/boiling overcomes only weak van der Waals’ forces between molecules → low mp; covalent bonds untouched. Never conducts.

Giant covalent (diamond, graphite, quartz)

Melting must break millions of strong covalent bonds → extremely high mp. Conducts only where mobile electrons exist (graphite).

Giant metallic (Na, Cu, Al)

Melting overcomes the metallic bonds between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons → usually high mp. Conducts in both solid and liquid states via delocalised electrons.

Identify an unknown from data

Two families only — simple (molecular: mp tracks intermolecular forces) vs giant (ionic / covalent / metallic: mp tracks bond strength). Then read the evidence. mp > 500 °C & conducts only molten → giant ionic. mp < 300 °C, insoluble-but-organic-solvent or gaseous → simple molecular. Insoluble hard solid, mp > 1500 °C → giant covalent. Shiny malleable conductor → metallic. (Examiners reward stating the reading and the reason.)

Multi-blank Quiz — Structures & Properties

1. In NaCl crystals each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions, and melting NaCl overcomes the ionic bonds — the electrostatic attractions between ions.
2. Solid NaCl does not conduct because the ions cannot move — they are fixed in the lattice; molten NaCl conducts because mobile ions are present.
3. CsCl is brittle because relative movement of the ions brings ions of the same charge close together, causing repulsion.
4. Dry ice sublimes at −78 °C because only weak van der Waals’ forces between the CO₂ molecules are overcome; the covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact.
5. CS₂ is a liquid while CO₂ is a gas at room temperature because CS₂ molecules are larger, so the van der Waals’ forces between the molecules are stronger.
6. Iodine dissolves in hexane but not in water; its crystal particles are held together by intermolecular (van der Waals') forces — a simple molecular structure.
7. Diamond is hard because each carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds in a tetrahedral network; it does not conduct because there are no free/delocalised electrons.
8. Graphite conducts because one electron per carbon is delocalised between its hexagonal layers, and feels slippery because the layers slide over each other.
9. SiO₂ (quartz) is a giant covalent network while CO₂ is simple molecular — so quartz is a hard, high-melting solid.
10. Aluminium has a higher melting point than sodium because more delocalised electrons per ion make the metallic bond stronger.
11. When a metal wire is drawn out, the layers of ions shift without breaking the metallic bonds — the MS wording for malleability.
12. From data “mp 801 °C, conducts only when molten”, the structure is giant ionic; the mobile charge carriers when molten are ions, not electrons.
13. In graphite the gap between layers (≈ 0.335 nm) is more than twice the in-plane C–C distance (≈ 0.142 nm) — so the bonding is strong within each layer but only van der Waals’ forces act between the layers.
14. Graphite is slippery because layers slide (weak vdW overcome), yet it sublimes above ≈3600 °C because escaping particles require breaking the covalent bonds within the layers — softness and sublimation answer different questions; the delocalised electrons move along the layers only.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Structures

Break bonds vs break forces

Giant structures: heat breaks the strong bonds (ionic/covalent/metallic). Molecular solids: heat breaks only the weak intermolecular forces. Naming the wrong level is the CP’s most-repeated zero.

CO₂ vs SiO₂

Both “dioxides”, both covalent bonded — yet CO₂ is simple molecular (gas, −78 °C) and SiO₂ is giant covalent (solid, 1713 °C). Silicon, unlike carbon, builds 4-bond networks through bridging oxygens.

Diamond vs graphite conduction

Both pure carbon, both giant — diamond uses all 4 valence electrons in bonds (no carriers), graphite keeps one per atom delocalised along the layers.

Graphite: giant vs “simple molecular layers”

Its layers are giant covalent sheets; only the spaces between sheets rely on vdW. Calling the whole structure simple molecular loses the identification mark (CP 2013).

Who carries the current?

Metals & graphite: (delocalised) electrons. Molten/aqueous ionic compounds: ions. Aqueous salt solution conducting via “free electrons from the salt” — rejected in CP 2015 Q7 / CP 2016 Q2.

Hard but brittle vs malleable

Ionic lattices: hard (strong attractions) yet brittle (like-charge repulsion after shift). Metals: malleable (bonding non-directional, ions slide in the electron sea). “Ionic compounds are malleable” reverses the exam story.

Melting NaCl: bonds overcome are…

Ionic bonds / electrostatic attractions between ions — not “ionic molecules” and not any covalent bond. “Intermolecular forces between NaCl molecules” is NOT ACCEPT: no NaCl molecules exist.

Slippery vs low sublimation temperature

Graphite’s softness measures how layers slide; its ≈3600 °C sublimation measures how hard it is to break in-plane covalent bonds. “Weak forces between layers, so graphite should melt easily” confuses the two questions — exactly the bond-vs-intermolecular error RSC classroom notes flag for CO₂ too.

Graphite’s carriers: ions? sea? electrons?

Not ions (it is an element). “Sea of electrons around positive ions” is metallic-bonding language. Right wording: one delocalised electron per carbon, mobile within the layers — conductivity is directional, along the planes.

vdW vs “hydrogen bonds” in simple molecules

Topic II attributes intermolecular attractions to van der Waals’ forces. Hydrogen bonding is Topic VI: if an ice item appears, use the MS stock wording (“open structure”, “hydrogen bond is directional”) without extending it.

4 · Formula & naming rules in one breath▾

Ionic: write ion charges → cross so total + = total − → lowest whole ratio → brackets for polyatomics when count > 1. MgCl₂, Na₂SO₄, Ca(OH)₂, Al₂O₃, Fe₂(SO₄)₃. Name = cation (variable metal: Roman numeral) + anion.

Covalent: prefixes carry the counts — mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-… Carbon monoxide CO, carbon dioxide CO₂, dinitrogen tetroxide N₂O₄, sulphur dioxide SO₂, phosphorus trichloride PCl₃. Water and ammonia keep their common names.

Mass up: bracket-first: (NH₄)₂SO₄ = 2N + 8H + S + 4O; hydrate CuSO₄·5H₂O adds 5 × 18.0 = 90.0. Ionic solids quote formula mass; molecules quote relative molecular mass; both unitless.
