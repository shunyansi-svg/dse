Syllabus Master Overview

Topic V Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(d))

Every Students should learn / be able to strand of Fossil Fuels and Carbon Compounds, with HKEAA watch-outs from mark-scheme language and Candidates’ Performance reports.

Topic V (a) Petroleum & fractions

Coal, petroleum and natural gas; petroleum as a mixture and its separation by fractional distillation

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Describe the origin of fossil fuels: coal, petroleum and natural gas from the remains of ancient plants and marine organisms, compressed over millions of years — all non-renewable sources of carbon compounds.
  • Petroleum (crude oil) is a mixture of hydrocarbons — mostly alkanes of different chain lengths; it is separated industrially by fractional distillation, which sorts molecules by boiling-point range (a physical change, no new substance).
  • Gradation across the fractions — colour, viscosity, volatility and burning characteristics — as carbon atoms per molecule increase: bp ↑, viscosity ↑, volatility ↓, harder to ignite, smokier flame. Reason wording: larger molecules → stronger van der Waals’ forces (“a smaller relative molecular mass … so … a lower boiling point”).
  • Know each fraction’s major use: refinery gas, petrol, naphtha (feedstock), kerosene (paraffin), diesel/light oil, lubricating oil/fuel oil, bitumen (road surfacing, roofing).
  • Explain the demand mismatch: market wants more of the small-molecule fuel fractions → heavy fractions are converted by cracking (Engine A, cards 5–6).
  • Recognize petroleum’s economic importance as a source of aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g. benzene) — recognition level only.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Propene (and most alkenes) are not present in crude oil — “fractional distillation of naphtha gives propene” was a recorded wrong answer; the route is cracking, then distillation to isolate (CP 2013 Q6). Fractions are mixtures: each boils over a range, never at one fixed temperature. Separation is by boiling point — writing “melting point of the alkanes” earns nothing (CP 2014 Q6).

Topic V (a) Burning & consequences

Heat change during combustion of hydrocarbons; pollution from burning fossil fuels; impact and measures

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Recognize combustion as an exothermic reaction: hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, every species with its state symbol, e.g. $\text{CH}_4(g)+2\text{O}_2(g)\to\text{CO}_2(g)+2\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$.
  • Complete vs incomplete combustion: limited air → carbon monoxide (toxic) or soot: $2\text{CH}_4(g)+3\text{O}_2(g)\to 2\text{CO}(g)+4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$.
  • Name the combustion products with their harms: CO₂ — greenhouse gas/global warming; SO₂ (from sulphur impurities) and NOx — acid rain; CO — toxic gas; smoke/particulates — respiratory problems.
  • Suggest measures for reducing air pollutants and match each to its source: catalytic converters on vehicles; flue-gas desulphurization (scrubbers) at power stations; low-sulphur fuels; unleaded petrol; LPG substitution.
  • Evaluate the impact of fossil-fuel use on quality of life and the environment — benefits (domestic fuels, raw materials for synthetic polymers and fibres) against air pollution, acid rain and global warming (topic overview wording).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

A measure must fit the machine it treats: examiners logged “installing scrubbers in vehicles or installing catalytic converters in factories”, and energy switches “not related to fossil fuel usage (e.g. using nuclear energy)” (CP 2012 Q10). CO₂ causes the enhanced greenhouse effect, not acid rain; and “dioxins from burning fossil fuels” was a recorded false correlation (CP 2012 Paper 2 context). Missing state symbols on combustion equations was specifically flagged (CP 2014 Q6).

Topic V (b) Systematics of carbon

Carbon’s unique combination power; homologous series; structural formulae and systematic naming (≤ 8 carbon atoms)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Explain the large number and diversity of carbon compounds: carbon is tetravalent and catenates — strong C–C bonds build chains, branches and rings, plus single/double bonds to many elements.
  • Homologous series (state it word-perfect): a family of compounds with the same general formula and functional group, showing a gradation in physical properties and similar chemical properties.
  • The four Topic V series: alkanes $C_nH_{2n+2}$ · alkenes $C_nH_{2n}$ · alkanols $C_nH_{2n+1}OH$ · alkanoic acids $C_nH_{2n+1}COOH$.
  • Draw structures in different notations — displayed, condensed, skeletal — “the use of different notations … is expected” (NTF-2018 II-B V (b)).
  • Name alkanes, alkenes, alkanols and alkanoic acids with chains not more than eight carbon atoms (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-, hept-, oct-), including locants, halogen substituents and multiple same-type groups (propane-1,2,3-triol; 3,3-dichloropropene; 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol) (NTF-2018 II-B V (b)).
  • Distinguish saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons from a structural formula: only C–C single bonds vs at least one C=C.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Order of priority of principal functional groups is not expected — you will never have to choose the suffix when two different groups appear (NTF-2018 II-B V (b)). Names must carry position numbers: “butene” when but-1-ene is drawn loses the mark — few candidates produced the fully correct systematic name in (CP 2014 Q3). Beyond oct- is off-syllabus: formulas still balance, but the naming is not examinable.

Topic V (c) Reactions

Petroleum as a source of alkanes; alkane combustion and substitution; cracking; alkene addition reactions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Alkanes (methane → octane, $C_nH_{2n+2}$): saturated, relatively unreactive; combustion and substitution with chlorine/bromine under UV light are the two taught reaction types.
  • Describe the monosubstitution of methane with chlorine using suitable diagrams or equations — the step form $\text{CH}_4+\text{Cl}\cdot\to\cdot\text{CH}_3+\text{HCl}$ is explicitly acceptable (NTF-2018 II-B V (c)); reactions occur in more than one step and involve free radicals (topic overview).
  • Cracking = breaking long-chain hydrocarbons into smaller ones over a hot catalyst: $\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18}(g)\to\text{C}_2\text{H}_6(g)+2\text{C}_3\text{H}_6(g)$ — a mark-scheme example; know the lab procedure and how to test the gaseous products.
  • Alkenes ($C_nH_{2n}$, functional group C=C): addition across the double bond — with bromine (decolourises bromine water) and with potassium permanganate solution.
  • Carry out the chemical tests for unsaturation: shake with bromine water — orange/brown → colourless — or with acidified KMnO₄(aq) — purple → colourless (MS bank wording).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Reagent wording is marked: “bromine liquid or bromine” was rejected for the test — write bromine water (CP 2014 Q3); state the colour change from orange/brown to colourless, not the reverse (CP 2016 Q3). The chain-carrying particle is the bromine radical Br• — calling it a bromide ion lost marks (CP 2015 Q6); excess methane is the condition that favours CH₃Cl (CP 2015 Q6). The industrial importance of cracking must be phrased as producing more useful smaller molecules (CP 2012 Q2(a)(ii)).

Topic V (d) Addition polymers

Monomers, polymers and repeating units; addition polymerisation; structures, properties and uses of five common addition polymers

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Synthetic polymers are built from small molecules called monomers; a long chain is a polymer; the bracketed pattern that repeats is the repeating unit.
  • Alkenes from cracking undergo addition polymerisation — no other product, no atom lost: $n\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2(g)\to\big[\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-}\big]_n(s)$.
  • Deduce the repeating unit from a monomer and the monomer from a given section of polymer — both directions are syllabus skills; the backbone carbons come only from the C=C.
  • The five named polymers: polyethene, polypropene, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polystyrene, Perspex (PMMA) — uses related to properties, properties related to structure (e.g. thermoplastic softening on heating).
  • Relate the disposal problems of plastics to their durability and non-biodegradability; know reduce / reuse / recycle and incineration-versus-landfill trade-offs (topic overview “the disposal problem for plastics”).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Never polymerise the benzene ring: “the carbon–carbon double bonds in benzene and phenyl group … will not undergo addition polymerisation” (NTF-2018 II-B V (d)) — in polystyrene only the side-chain C=C opens. Brackets and n are marked strictly: “wrongly added ‘n’” (CP 2014 Q3), “omission of n” (CP 2016 Q5); spell the process addition polymerisation — “addiction”/“additional” were recorded (CP 2016 Q5), and styrene → polystyrene mislabelled “condensation polymerisation” was common (CP 2015 Paper 2 context).

🚫 Out of scope for Topic V (Notes-for-Teachers depth caps — do not test here)

Condensation polymerisation and any deeper treatment of polymer properties → Topics XI / XIV (topic overview: they “are included in Topic XI … and Topic XIV”). Benzene/aromatics: only “recognise petroleum as an economic source of aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g. benzene)” — explaining benzene’s stability is not expected (NTF-2018 II-B V (d)). Naming: carbon chains more than eight atoms and order of priority of principal functional groups are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B V (b)). Reaction mechanisms beyond the taught free-radical steps for methane + chlorine, and all chemistry of halogenoalkanes, alcohols, acids, esters, carbonyls, Markovnikov addition, geometric/optical isomerism → Topic XI. Manufacture conditions of LDPE/HDPE → Topic XI. Calorimetry and standard enthalpy change of combustion → Topic VIII. Allotropes of carbon (graphite, diamond) → Topic II. Gas calculations use r.t.p., Vm = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ — 22.4 dm³ mol⁻¹ applies at s.t.p.; HKDSE gas-volume work here is set at r.t.p. (24.0).

V (a)

Fossil fuels and their origin

$$\text{coal · petroleum · natural gas} \;=\; \text{NON-renewable}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Fossil fuels formed from the remains of ancient plants and marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years — describe the origin, do not invent chemistry into it.
  • Coal is mostly carbon; petroleum (crude oil) is a mixture of hydrocarbons; natural gas is mainly methane — all three are the syllabus’s starting sources of carbon compounds.
  • They are non-renewable: consumption vastly outpaces formation — the values-and-attitudes strand asks students to “value the need for the conservation of the Earth’s resources”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Describe the origin of fossil fuels” = ancient organic remains + long time + heat and pressure. Naming the fuels alone is the first half of the answer.
  • “Petroleum” and “crude oil” are the same mixture; a “fraction” is a part of it separated by boiling range — never call a fraction a pure compound.
V (a)

Petroleum & fractional distillation

$$\text{crude oil} \xrightarrow{\text{FD (physical)}} \text{fractions by boiling range}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Petroleum = a mixture of hydrocarbons (mostly alkanes of different chain lengths) plus small amounts of other organic compounds.
  • Industrial separation: vaporise, then fractional distillation — molecules condense at different heights as the column cools upward; each fraction has a boiling-point range, not a single point.
  • Fractional distillation is a physical change: no bonds broken between atoms, no new substance — say exactly that.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The separation variable is boiling point: “separation based on the melting point of the alkanes” is a recorded wrong answer (CP 2014 Q6).
  • Fewer carbon atoms → weaker van der Waals’ forces → lower boiling range → collected nearer the top of the column.
V (a)

Gradation in properties of the fractions

$$\uparrow C\ \Rightarrow\ \uparrow b\!p,\ \uparrow \text{viscosity},\ \downarrow \text{volatility}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Relate colour, viscosity, volatility and burning characteristics to the number of carbon atoms per molecule (syllabus wording — all four named).
  • Down the fractions: darker, more viscous, less volatile, harder to ignite, smokier flame; boiling range rises.
  • Cause wording: bigger molecules → stronger van der Waals’ forces between them → more energy to vaporise.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Accepted comparison pattern from the MS bank: “G has a smaller relative molecular mass than F, so G has a lower boiling point” — always name both substances and the direction (CP 2014 Q6: half the candidates omitted the bp ↔ molecular size/chain length link).
  • Volatility and viscosity run in opposite directions — swapping them is the classic self-inflicted zero (see Trap Pairs).
V (a)

Uses of the distilled fractions & demand

$$\text{demand(small molecules)} > \text{supply by FD alone}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Light → heavy: refinery gas (bottled gas, H₂ feedstock) · petrol (motor fuel) · naphtha (petrochemical feedstock, solvents) · kerosene/paraffin (jet fuel, heating, lamps) · diesel/light oil (diesel engines) · lubricating oil / fuel oil (machinery lubrication, ships and power-station burners) · bitumen (road surfacing, roofing).
  • Explain the demand: market wants more petrol/short-chain fuels than straight distillation supplies, while heavy fractions pile up — hence cracking (card 4).
  • Link use ↔ property each time: volatile + flammable = fuel for engines; viscous + waterproof = bitumen.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Naphtha → propene by fractional distillation” was a recorded wrong answer (CP 2013 Q6): propene must be made by cracking naphtha, then isolated by distillation.
  • Give the use and the property it rests on; a bare use-list earns only half.
V (a, c)

Cracking — what it is and why industry needs it

$$\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_{22}(g)\to\text{C}_{8}\text{H}_{18}(g)+\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{4}(g)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Cracking = thermal decomposition of large, less useful hydrocarbons into smaller, more useful ones — alkanes and alkenes come out; a chemical change.
  • Conditions: vapour of a heavy fraction passed over a hot (porcelain) catalyst / very hot aluminium oxide at high temperature — or steam cracking industrially.
  • Industrial importance, in the bank’s own words: “To produce alkenes / To produce smaller hydrocarbons from larger hydrocarbons / To convert heavy oil to petrol”.
  • A mark-scheme sample: $\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18}(g)\to\text{C}_2\text{H}_6(g)+2\text{CH}_3\text{CH}{=}\text{CH}_2(g)$ — atoms must balance exactly both sides.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Explain importance “according to their abilities of producing ‘more’ useful alkenes or alkanes with lower molecular masses” — the CP report’s exact expectation (CP 2012 Q2(a)(ii)).
  • Cracking is not a separation method: “confused catalytic cracking and fractional distillation… wrongly stated this reaction can separate useful fractions” (CP 2016 Q3).
  • When asked to balance a cracking equation, check the formula first: “failed to give the correct molecular formula of octane” (CP 2016 Q3) — C₈H₁₈, not C₈H₁₆.
V (c)

Laboratory cracking of a petroleum fraction

$$\text{kerosene vapour}\xrightarrow{\text{hot porcelain}}\text{alkanes}+\text{alkenes}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Set-up: kerosene soaked in glass wool at one end; broken unglazed porcelain strongly heated in the middle (the catalyst); gas collected over water and tested.
  • Where cracking happens: on the hot porcelain surface — heating the glass wool only vaporises the kerosene.
  • Porcelain is used broken: larger surface area → more catalytic contact → faster cracking (this exact wording was tested twice).
  • Test products with bromine water / acidified KMnO₄ — both decolourise if alkenes are present (Engine B).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Candidates did not know alkenes might be produced… which could decolourise acidified KMnO₄(aq)” (CP 2012 Q17, MC); the wrong reason for broken porcelain — “can be heated more efficiently” — was recorded (CP 2016 Q3).
⏳ Safety line

Remove the delivery tube from the water before stopping heating — otherwise water is sucked back into the hot boiling tube and it cracks; “sucking back of water into the hot boiling tube” was a marked step (CP 2016 Q3(d)). Flammable vapours + open flame: light only when instructed.

V (a, c)

Combustion of hydrocarbons is exothermic

$$\text{CH}_4(g)+2\text{O}_2(g)\to\text{CO}_2(g)+2\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Recognize combustion as an exothermic chemical reaction — heat and light released; this is why we burn fuels at all.
  • Complete combustion products: carbon dioxide + water. Balancing recipe for CxHy: C → x CO₂, H → (y/2) H₂O, then O₂ = x + y/4 (double everything if a ½ appears).
  • Every species carries a state symbol; burning gives water vapour, so H₂O(g) fits a flame equation.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Many candidates did not write down the state symbols of the reactants and products” — flagged verbatim in the report (CP 2014 Q6). States are a mark, not decoration.
  • Never “balance” by editing subscripts: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O balanced by inserting coefficients only (2,7 → 4,6).
V (a, c)

Incomplete combustion — CO and soot

$$2\text{CH}_4(g)+3\text{O}_2(g)\to 2\text{CO}(g)+4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Limited air supply → carbon monoxide (toxic, binds haemoglobin) or even free carbon (soot): $\text{CH}_4(g)+\text{O}_2(g)\to\text{C}(s)+2\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$.
  • Why heavier fractions burn smokier: higher carbon-to-hydrogen proportion → more unburnt carbon as particulates.
  • Recognize the warning signs: yellow luminous flame, soot deposits, poor ventilation around gas appliances.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • State which product forms from which shortage: CO = “insufficient oxygen”; soot = even less. Pair CO with toxic, CO₂ with greenhouse — swapping them splits your mark in two.

Pollution from burning fossil fuels

$$\text{SO}_2,\ \text{NO}_x \to \text{acid rain};\quad \text{CO}_2 \to \text{global warming}$$
🎯 Learning Goals

  • Match gas → harm: SO₂ (from sulphur impurities in fuels) and NOₓ (high-temperature engines) → acid rain; CO₂ → enhanced greenhouse effect; CO → poisoning; smoke/particulates → respiratory damage; lead compounds (past) → nervous-system harm, now removed via unleaded petrol.

The Refinery, top to bottom — four windows onto one crude

Every strand-(a) exam story walks this line: evaporate → sort by boiling range → collect → crack the backlog. Learn the windows and the questions become bookkeeping.

Window 1 · Evaporate

Crude oil is heated (~400 °C range) and its vapours enter the column. Nothing reacts — physical change only.

Window 2 · Sort by boiling range

Column hot at the bottom, cool at the top. Short molecules stay gaseous and rise; long molecules condense low down. Each tray draws off one fraction.

Window 3 · Match fractions to uses

gas · petrol · naphtha · kerosene · diesel · lubricating/fuel oil · bitumen — properties (volatility, viscosity, burning) explain each use.

Window 4 · Crack the surplus

Heavy fractions + demand shortage → thermal decomposition over hot catalyst → alkanes + alkenes (→ polymers, Engine D).

Multi-blank Quiz — Fossil Fuels, Fractions & Cracking

1. Coal, petroleum and natural gas are fossil fuels: formed from ancient organic remains over millions of years, so they are non-renewable. Natural gas consists mainly of methane.
2. Petroleum is separated into fractions by fractional distillation, a physical change that sorts the hydrocarbons by their boiling-point range — never by melting point (CP 2014 Q6).
3. Down the fractions (more carbon atoms): boiling point increases, viscosity increases, volatility decreases, and the flame becomes smokier because of the higher carbon-to-hydrogen proportion.
4. The reason heavier fractions boil higher is stronger van der Waals’ forces between the larger molecules; the MS comparison wording names the smaller relative molecular mass of the lower-boiling substance explicitly.
5. Bitumen’s job is road surfacing / roofing; kerosene is the fraction used in jet engines; naphtha matters mainly as the feedstock that is cracked to make alkenes.
6. Cracking is thermal decomposition of large hydrocarbons over a hot (porcelain) catalyst; its products are smaller alkanes and alkenes — the feedstock of Engine D.
7. In the lab set-up the kerosene sits in the glass wool; cracking occurs at the (hot) unglazed porcelain, which is broken up to give a larger surface area. Before stopping heating, remove the delivery tube to prevent sucking back of water (CP 2016 Q3).
8. Complete combustion of propane: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g) — every symbol carries its state.
9. Match gas to harm: carbon monoxide → toxic (poisons haemoglobin); sulphur dioxide → acid rain; carbon dioxide → enhanced greenhouse effect / global warming.
10. Catalytic converters belong on vehicles (exhaust); scrubbers belong in power plants (flue gas) — mixing the two device–source pairs was a named CP failure (CP 2012 Q10). Using unleaded petrol / LPG cuts lead and particulate emissions at source.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Fossil Fuels & Cracking

Cracking vs fractional distillation

FD sorts what is already in crude — physical change, no new molecules. Cracking makes new molecules by thermal decomposition — chemical change. Propene is a cracking product, not a distillation find (CP 2013 Q6; CP 2016 Q3).

Vaporisation vs cracking in the lab tube

Heating the glass wool only vaporises kerosene; cracking needs the vapour to meet the hot porcelain catalyst. “A reaction occurs at the glass wool” was the MC trap (CP 2012 Q17).

Volatility vs viscosity

Heavier fractions: less volatile (harder to vaporise) yet more viscous (thicker). The two adjectives run in opposite directions — swap them and both marks die together.

Boiling point vs melting point in separation

Distillation separates by boiling point. “Separation based on the melting point of the alkanes” is the verbatim CP-logged error (CP 2014 Q6).

Greenhouse gas vs acid-rain gas vs toxic gas

CO₂ → global warming (not acid rain). SO₂/NOₓ → acid rain. CO → toxic. One gas, one harm; the paper tests the pairing, not the prose (CP 2012 Q10).

Catalytic converter vs flue-gas scrubber

Converter = vehicle exhaust device; scrubber = power-plant chimney device. The report recorded both swaps in the same script (CP 2012 Q10). “Using nuclear energy” is not a combustion-emission measure.

Fraction vs pure substance

Each fraction is still a mixture of hydrocarbons with a boiling range. Writing “petrol boils at 100 °C” treats a mixture as a compound; say “over the range…”, or name the individual hydrocarbon instead.

Addition — what the double bond is for

$$\text{addition: two molecules}\to\text{one, across C=C}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Addition reaction: the C=C opens; the two new σ bonds form on the two carbons that shared it — one product, nothing else.
  • Bank wording: “Bromination (addition of bromine) reaction occurs to the C=C bonds” — the double bond is the reactive site, not the whole molecule.
  • Counting atoms in an addition: C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ — every reactant atom survives in the product (contrast with substitution, where HBr leaves).
  • This same opening, chained thousands of times, is addition polymerisation — Engine D.

Saturated vs unsaturated — decide, then prove

Multi-blank Quiz — Alkanes & Alkenes

1. Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons of general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; they are relatively unreactive because their C–C and C–H bonds are strong and non-polar.
2. Complete combustion of butane: 2C₄H₁₀(g) + 13O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 10H₂O(g); with limited air the dangerous product is carbon monoxide (CO).
3. Methane reacts with chlorine by substitution in the presence of UV light; the gaseous products are chloromethane and HCl.
4. A chlorine radical is written Cl· and carries an unpaired electron; calling the reactive particle a chloride ion was a logged error — radicals are not ions (CP 2015 Q6).
5. To maximise CH₃Cl yield, use an excess of methane; left running with excess chlorine the mixture keeps substituting to CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃ and CCl₄.
6. Ethene is unsaturated because it contains a carbon–carbon double bond; its displayed formula is CH₂=CH₂ and its general-series formula is CₙH₂ₙ.
7. The reagent for the unsaturation test must be named bromine water — “bromine” or “bromine liquid” was rejected (CP 2014 Q3); the positive observation runs orange (brown) → colourless, and the negative sample shows no change.
8. Acidified potassium permanganate with an alkene fades from purple to colourless; the same ion in alkaline medium instead gives a brown precipitate — the bank’s wording “MnO₄⁻/OH⁻” row.
9. Propene + bromine gives 1,2-dibromopropane (CH₃CHBrCH₂Br): addition places the two Br atoms on the two former double-bond carbons; all reactant atoms appear in the single product.
10. Substitution differs from addition because substitution replaces an atom (H) and expels HX, while addition joins two molecules across the C=C; alkanes undergo substitution with halogens, alkenes undergo addition.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Substitution vs Addition

Substitution vs addition

Substitution: alkane + halogen, UV, one H swapped, HX leaves — two products. Addition: alkene, C=C opens, everything sticks — one product. Name the class first, then pick the reaction type.

Radical (Br•) vs ion (Br⁻)

The chain carrier has an unpaired electron and no charge. “Wrongly stated the given bromine atom / radical as a bromide ion” — CP 2015 Q6. Charge notation and dot notation are different species entirely.

Excess methane vs excess chlorine

Want monosubstitution? Excess methane (fewer Cl₂ encounters per CH₄). Excess chlorine drives further substitution to CCl₄. Half the candidates had it backwards or absent (CP 2015 Q6).

Bromine water vs “bromine” vs “bromine liquid”

The test reagent is bromine water, Br₂(aq). Bare “bromine” names the elemental liquid — rejected as reagent wording (CP 2014 Q3). Write the solution, not the element.

Bromine colour change vs permanganate colour change

Bromine: orange/brown → colourless (reverse direction recorded wrong, CP 2016 Q3). Acidified KMnO₄: purple → colourless; alkaline KMnO₄: brown precipitate. Two tubes, three vocabularies — never mix the rows.

Complete vs incomplete combustion

Oxygen plentiful → CO₂ + H₂O. Oxygen limited → CO (toxic) or C (soot, smoky flame). The products, the hazards and the balanced coefficients all change — pick the regime from the wording first.

“It decolourises” vs a full test answer

A 2-mark test = correct reagent + correct observation(s) with comparison (bank mark allocation). “Decolourises” alone skips the initial colour and the negative sample — the incomplete-description error of CP 2012 Q2(c)(ii).

The Name → Structure Decoder — four moves, any name

Every Topic V name (up to 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol) decodes by the same four moves. Run them in order and you never “guess” a structure.

Move 1 · Root = carbon count

pent- → five carbons in the parent chain. Lay the chain before you add anything.

Move 2 · Suffix = saturation / family

-ane single bonds · -ene has C=C · -ol has –OH · -oic acid has –COOH (it owns C1).

Move 3 · Locants = positions

but-2-ene: double bond starts at C2; propan-1-ol: –OH on C1. Number from the end that serves the suffix group / C=C.

Move 4 · Prefixes = substituents; finish with H

2-bromo…, 3,3-dichloro… — attach them, then give every carbon four bonds (fill with H). Check: Cₙ valence done.

Worked decodes

  • 2-methylpropane: propane chain + methyl on C2 → CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃, C₄H₁₀.
  • pent-2-ene: C₅, C=C from C2 → CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃, C₅H₁₀.
  • propan-2-ol: C₃ + OH on C2 → CH₃CHOHCH₃, C₃H₈O.
  • 3,3-dichloropropene (NTF example): C₃, C=C from C1, two Cl on C3 → Cl₂CHCH=CH₂.
  • 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol (NTF example): pent chain, C=C at C3, Br at C2, OH at C1 — suffix –ol wins the numbering end.

Multi-blank Quiz — Naming & Structures

1. Carbon compounds are numerous because carbon is tetravalent (four covalent bonds) and shows catenation — strong C–C bonds form chains, branches and rings.
2. A homologous series is a family with the same general formula and same functional group, a gradation in physical properties and similar chemical properties.
3. Successive members differ by the unit –CH₂–, so their relative molecular masses differ by 14; the alkanols in this series are recognised by the hydroxyl (–OH) group.
4. General formulae: alkenes CₙH₂ₙ; alkanoic acids CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH; the naming ceiling is 8 carbon atoms (NTF-2018 II-B V (b)).
5. Roots in order: meth, eth, prop, but (4 C), pent, hex (6 C), hept, oct (8 C).
6. CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ is named 2-methylpropane: the longest chain is propane and the substituent is a methyl group on C2.
7. CH₃CH=CHCH₃ is but-2-ene (molecular formula C₄H₈); its partner but-1-ene differs in the position of the double bond — a position-isomer pair (full isomer theory: Topic XI).
8. Name the alcohol CH₃CHOHCH₃: propan-2-ol; name CH₂=CHCOOH: propenoic acid — the name most candidates could not write (CP 2016 Q5).
9. In 3,3-dichloropropene the two chlorines sit on carbon 3 and the double bond starts at carbon 1; in 2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol the –OH fixes the numbering end, so Br is on carbon 2.
10. The –COOH carbon always counts as C1, so CH₃CH₂COOH is propanoic acid and needs no locant on the acid group.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Naming & Formulae

-ane vs -ene vs -yl

propane = the C₃ molecule, saturated; propene = C₃ with a C=C; propyl = a –C₃H₇ arm attached to something else. Suffix changes the species, not just the sound.

“butene” vs but-1-ene / but-2-ene

From C₄ up, the double bond has a place — the locant is part of the name. A stem that draws the C=C must be answered with it; a bare “butene” caps at Topic II scripts (CP 2014 Q3 naming).

Molecular vs structural vs displayed formula

C₄H₁₀ tells you atoms; CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ tells you connectivity; the displayed form shows every bond. Asked for a structure, a molecular formula is not an answer — and it cannot distinguish the C₄H₁₀ isomers.

Homologous series vs “a group of similar compounds”

Loose wording fails the definition marks: same general formula, same functional group, physical gradation, chemical similarity — the definition is four clauses, not a vibe.

-ol suffix vs hydroxy prefix

One –OH is the suffix (propan-2-ol). Only when a second, different-priority group is present does –OH go prefix (2-bromopent-3-en-1-ol) — and priority tables are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B V (b)); examinable names always tell you which ending owns the tail.

Counting the acid carbon out of the name

Ethanoic acid = CH₃COOH = two carbons (the COOH carbon counts); propanoic acid = three. Trimming the –COOH carbon from the count turns every acid name one root short.

Engine D · Strand (d)

Addition Polymers Inspector — from one double bond to a plastic

Monomer, repeating unit, and the five plastics the syllabus actually names — plus the disposal reckoning that comes with them.

Pick a term

V (d)

Monomer · polymer · repeating unit

$$\text{many monomers} \to \text{one giant polymer chain}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Monomer: a small molecule that joins repeatedly to form a polymer — in this topic, an alkene such as ethene CH₂=CH₂.
  • Polymer: the very large molecule built from many monomers; synthetic polymers are built up from small molecules called monomers (syllabus wording).
  • Repeating unit: the smallest bracketed pattern that, repeated n times, rebuilds the chain — for polyethene, –CH₂–CH₂–.
  • n is a very large number — it is written outside the brackets, never inside a structure.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Three separate vocabulary marks live here — answer with the word the stem asks for: the monomer is ethene; the polymer is polyethene; –CH₂–CH₂– in brackets is the repeating unit.
V (d)

Addition polymerisation — the double bond unzips into a chain

$$n\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2(g)\to\big[\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-}\big]_n(s)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Alkenes “can undergo addition reactions” and therefore addition polymerisation: thousands of monomers add together across their C=C with no other product.
  • Atoms are conserved: the polymer contains every atom of every monomer — nothing is eliminated (contrast the condensation type: “small molecules (e.g. H₂O) would be eliminated” — Topic XI).
  • Describe it with a chemical equation, conditions included when asked — high temperature, high pressure and catalyst (exact industrial detail is Topic XI depth).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Some candidates omitted the reaction conditions for the polymerisation process” (CP 2013 Q6) — one line: “high temperature, high pressure, catalyst”.
  • Spell the term exactly: addition polymerisation — “addiction”/“additional polymerisation” were recorded (CP 2016 Q5); the bank insists on correct spelling of polymerisation words.
V (d)

Polyethene (polyethylene)

$$\big[\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-}\big]_n$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Monomer: ethene; the syllabus’s flagship polymer — the topic overview even points students to “the discovery of polyethene and the development of addition polymers”.
  • Properties → uses: flexible, tough, waterproof, good electrical insulator, low density → carrier bags, bottles, film, cable insulation.
  • Chemically inert (non-polar C–C/C–H backbone, no reactive groups) — the very property that makes it useful and that makes disposal hard.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Relate each use to a property and the property to the structure — that three-link chain is what “uses related to physical properties, in turn related to structures” (topic overview) demands.
V (d)

Polypropene (polypropylene)

$$n\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHCH}_3(g)\to\big[\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}(\text{CH}_3)\text{-}\big]_n(s)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Monomer: propene, CH₂=CHCH₃ — obtained by cracking naphtha (Engine A), never “found in crude oil” (CP 2013 Q6).
  • The –CH₃ hangs on every second backbone carbon as a side branch; it must still appear inside the brackets.
  • Harder and stiffer than polyethene → ropes, crates, car trim, plastic “hinge” articles.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The full route asked in 2013: naphtha → cracking → propene separated by fractional distillation → polymerise with conditions; skipping any stage lost marks (CP 2013 Q6).
V (d)

Polyvinyl chloride — PVC

$$n\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHCl}(g)\to\big[\text{-CH}_2\text{-CHCl-}\big]_n(s)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The syllabus names the polymer “polyvinyl chloride”: its monomer is chloroethene (vinyl chloride), CH₂=CHCl.
  • The C–Cl bond keeps chlorine in the repeating unit — nothing is knocked off when the double bond opens.
  • Rigid and durable (pipes, window frames) or flexible with additives (cable insulation, hosepipe, shower curtains).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Keep the chlorine on the right carbon: chloroethene is CH₂=CHCl — the Cl sits on a double-bond carbon, otherwise the polymer would not carry it.
  • Disposal of PVC carries a chlorine-specific caution (acidic gases on burning) — note it, do not elaborate beyond the syllabus.
V (d)

Polystyrene — the phenyl test case

$$\text{styrene}=\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}{=}\text{CH}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Monomer: styrene (phenylethene) — a vinyl group on a benzene ring; repeating unit [–CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–]ₙ.
  • Only the side-chain C=C opens. The depth note is explicit: the double bonds in benzene and the phenyl group “will not undergo addition polymerisation” (NTF-2018 II-B V (d)) — the ring stays intact, drawn exactly as it was.
  • Foamed: disposable cups, packaging, floats; the topic’s worked-preparation example (“performing an experiment to prepare an addition polymer, e.g. polystyrene”).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Drawing styrene from a polymer section was half-passed (CP 2015 Paper 2 context) — keep the hexagon with its alternating bonds untouched and put the polymer handle on the vinyl arm only.
V (d)

Perspex — PMMA, the shatterproof glass

$$n\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C}(\text{CH}_3)\text{COOCH}_3(l)\to \text{PMMA}(s)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Perspex is poly(methyl methacrylate), PMMA; the monomer is methyl 2-methylpropenoate, CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃ — a mark-scheme polymerisation equation of exactly this kind is in the bank.
  • Two side groups (–CH₃ and –COOCH₃) hang on one backbone carbon; the ester group rides along unreacted — addition polymerisation needs only the C=C.
  • Transparent, rigid, shatter-resistant → lenses, displays, taillight covers, “glass” substitute; the bank’s thermoplastic definition applies: “becomes soft when heated, becomes hard when cooled”, and “PMMA is not a thermosetting plastic because its molecules are held by weak van der Waals’ forces”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The systematic name of the acid part “propenoic acid” was failed by most candidates who met it (CP 2016 Q5) — drill ester-style names: alkyl + alk-2-enoate, reading left to right.
V (d)

Deducing monomer ⇄ repeating unit — both directions

$$\big[\text{-CH}_2\text{-CHCl-}\big]_n(s) \;\xrightarrow{\text{back}}\; \text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHCl}(g)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Polymer → monomer: take the two backbone carbons of the repeating unit (bonds sticking out of the brackets), restore the C=C between them — that alkene is the monomer. Both skills are syllabus lines (“deduce the repeating unit …” / “deduce the monomer from a given section of a formula”).
  • Atom audit: the monomer must contain exactly the atoms of the repeating unit — same numbers, nothing removed.
  • Ring guard: if a phenyl/benzene group appears, it was never part of the opening — exclude it from locant hunting (NTF-2018 II-B V (d)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Incorrect structural formulae of monomers were “common” in the reports (CP 2012 Paper 2 context) — practise converting bracket forms back until the C=C lands in the right place every time.
V (d)

Plastics in service — and the disposal problem

$$\text{inert (good fuel)} \;=\; \text{non-biodegradable (bad litter)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The STSE framing of the whole topic: “the pollution problem arising from using leaded petrol and diesel; and the disposal problem for plastics … assess the risks and benefits … before actually using applications of science and technology”.
  • Why disposal bites: addition polymers are chemically inert and non-biodegradable → landfill space, litter, wildlife harm; incineration recovers energy but flue gases need handling (PVC’s chlorine especially).
  • The response menu: reduce consumption, reuse, recycle (sorted, re-melted — thermoplastics soften on heating, the bank’s definition), recover energy from waste; plus the values line “to appreciate the versatility of synthetic materials and the limitations of their use”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Evaluation questions want both sides: benefit of the material and limitation of its use — one-sided praise scores in the lowest band. Deeper polymer-degradation chemistry (biodegradable polyesters etc.) is Topic XI/XIV territory.

The Polymer Highway — forward, and the reverse lane

Forward: cracking feeds monomers, monomers feed polymers, uses follow properties. Reverse (the deduction skill): polymer sections back to the alkene that built them.

Lane 1 · Feedstock

Heavy fraction —crack→ light alkene (ethene, propene) —separate by FD→ pure monomer.

Lane 2 · Polymerise

n monomers, high temperature + high pressure + catalyst: each C=C opens and links; nothing else forms.

Lane 3 · Use the property

Inert + light + mouldable → bags, pipes, cups, lenses…; thermoplastic behaviour enables re-melt recycling.

Reverse lane · Deduce the monomer

Read one bracketed unit → its 2 backbone C ← restore C=C → monomer. Rings and side groups move unchanged.

Multi-blank Quiz — Addition Polymers

1. A monomer is a small molecule that joins repeatedly into a polymer; the bracketed pattern [–CH₂–CH₂–] with its n outside is the repeating unit.
2. In addition polymerisation the monomers add across the C=C, there is only one product, and no small molecule is eliminated — elimination of e.g. H₂O marks the condensation type (Topic XI).
3. The equation: n CH₂=CH₂ → [–CH₂–CH₂–]n; when the process is described, give the conditions high temperature, high pressure, catalyst (CP 2013 Q6).
4. PVC’s monomer is chloroethene (vinyl chloride), CH₂=CHCl; every chlorine of every monomer ends up on the backbone of the polymer because addition conserves all atoms.
5. Polystyrene’s monomer is styrene (phenylethene); the double bonds of the benzene / phenyl ring never undergo addition polymerisation — the ring is copied unchanged into every repeat (NTF-2018 II-B V (d)).
6. Perspex is PMMA; its monomer methyl 2-methylpropenoate carries two side groups, –CH₃ and –COOCH₃, on the same backbone carbon, and PMMA is a thermoplastic — soft on heating, hard on cooling (bank wording), not thermosetting.
7. To deduce the monomer from [–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–]ₙ: take the two backbone carbons and restore one C=C between them → propene, CH₂=CHCH₃.
8. Two ‘n’ disasters recorded by examiners: “wrongly added ‘n’ in their drawings” (CP 2014 Q3) and “the omission of n” (CP 2016 Q5) — n goes once, outside the brackets.
9. Addition polymers resist disposal because their chains are chemically inert / non-biodegradable; the management menu is reduce, reuse, recycle (thermoplastics can be re-melted) and, last, energy recovery with proper flue-gas treatment.
10. The three-link answer pattern for any polymer use: use ← physical property ← structure (chain length, side groups, intermolecular forces) — exactly the “uses related to properties, in turn related to structures” of the topic overview; mislabelling polystyrene’s formation as “condensation polymerisation” was a common wrong name (CP 2015 Paper 2 context).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Addition Polymers

Addition vs condensation polymerisation

Addition: one product, no atom lost, monomer needs a C=C. Condensation: bifunctional monomers join with elimination of a small molecule (e.g. H₂O). The reports caught the confusion both ways (CP 2012 Paper 2 context; CP 2016 Paper 2 context) — Topic V assesses only the addition kind.

Repeating unit vs full chain vs monomer

Same atoms, three drawings: monomer CH₂=CHCl has the double bond; the repeating unit is the bracketed –CH₂–CHCl– fragment; the polymer is that bracket with n. Drawing the monomer inside the brackets (double bond and all) is the “wrongly added ‘n’” family of error (CP 2014 Q3).

Vinyl C=C vs benzene C=C

The polymerising double bond is the side-chain (vinyl) one. Benzene/phenyl rings “will not undergo addition polymerisation” (NTF-2018 II-B V (d)) — and why they don’t (aromatic stability) is not expected either; just never crack a ring open.

“Polymerisation” vs “addiction/additional polymerisation”

The term is addition polymerisation, spelled as in the bank (“condensation polymerisation — spelling required”). CP recorded ‘addiction’ and ‘additional’ (CP 2016 Q5); a misspelt reaction type is an unearned mark.

Thermoplastic vs thermosetting

Thermoplastic: softens on heating, hardens on cooling — re-mouldable, recyclable; all five Topic V polymers are of this family. Thermosetting: sets hard and cannot be re-softened — that behaviour belongs to cross-linked (condensation) systems in Topics XI/XIV. PMMA being thermoplastic is bank language: chains held by weak van der Waals’ forces.

Monomer count vs repeating-unit count

n monomers → one chain of n repeating units: from the same n, never “half a unit” — deducing the monomer from a bracket whose pattern is CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂ means reading two 2-carbon repeats (2 ethenes), not one 4-carbon “butene”.

Recycling vs dumping “because plastic is inert”

Inertness cuts both ways — it is the reason the material is durable and the reason litter persists. An “evaluate” answer needs the risk–benefit pairing from the STSE note, not a one-sided defence or a one-sided condemnation.

7 · Famous Misconceptions — flip your answer before an examiner does▾

Each correction is grounded in the HKDSE record used by this studio: Candidates’ Performance reports 2012–2025 (2018–25 via briefing slides), the mark-scheme keyword bank, the 2018 Notes for Teachers, or the C&A Guide strand wording itself. No card here is classroom folklore without a source line.

The wrong ideaExam-true correctionSource
“Cracking separates petroleum into fractions.”Distillation sorts; cracking makes new smaller molecules by thermal decomposition.CP 2016 Q3; CP 2013 Q6
“The kerosene cracks while the glass wool is heated.”Heating the wool only vaporises the kerosene; the reaction occurs on the hot unglazed porcelain (the catalyst).CP 2012 Q17 (MC)
“Propene can be distilled out of naphtha.”Alkenes are essentially absent in crude oil; crack the naphtha, then distil the product mixture to isolate propene.CP 2013 Q6
“More unsaturated / bigger molecules burn cleaner.”Higher carbon proportion → more unburnt carbon → smokier flame; clean blue flames come from the lightest fuels with sufficient air.Syllabus V (a) burning characteristics; Engine A card 7
“The tube goes colourless because the bromine evaporated.”The bromine reacts — addition occurs to the C=C bonds; evaporation does not explain an instant fade in a stoppered tube.MS bank: “Bromination (addition of bromine) reaction occurs to the C=C bonds”
“Decolourisation can be reported backwards — colourless to brown.”Observation runs from the reagent’s own colour: orange/brown → colourless. Direction earns the mark.CP 2016 Q3
“A free radical is just an ion minus its charge.”A radical carries an unpaired electron and no charge; Br· and Br⁻ are different particles with different roles.CP 2015 Q6
“Polymerising ethene releases H₂O / HCl.”Addition polymerisation has one product and loses no atoms; elimination of a small molecule (e.g. H₂O) defines the condensation type (Topic XI).MS bank condensation wording; CP 2012 Paper 2 context
“Styrene polymerises through its benzene ring.”Only the vinyl C=C opens; benzene/phenyl double bonds will not undergo addition polymerisation (and their stability is not to be explained).NTF-2018 II-B V (d)
“Put n inside the brackets / on the monomer side.”n sits outside the brackets of the polymer formula — and it must be there at all: stray ‘n’ and missing ‘n’ were both logged.CP 2014 Q3; CP 2016 Q5
“One mole of gas occupies 22.4 dm³ at room temperature.”HKDSE convention: Vm = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at r.t.p.; 22.4 belongs to s.t.p. and breaks every volume answer in this topic.syllabus calculation convention (this studio, Solver Bench 1)
“CO₂ is one of the acid-rain gases; SO₂ warms the climate.”Pair each gas to its harm: SO₂/NOₓ → acid rain; CO₂ → enhanced greenhouse effect; CO → toxic; smoke → respiratory.Syllabus V (a); CP 2012 Q10 framing
“Petrol boils at 100 °C — it is a substance.”Every fraction is a mixture with a boiling range; only pure compounds have fixed boiling points.Syllabus V (a) composition & separation
“Heat makes alkanes react with bromine/chlorine.”Ultraviolet light (sunlight) starts the radical chain — in the dark nothing happens; “gave ‘heat’ as the answer” is the logged mis-set condition, and the type to name is substitution, spelled right.CP 2019 Q5(b); CP 2019 Q5(c)
“The repeating unit is just the monomer with brackets round it.”The C=C must be opened into single backbone bonds inside the brackets — “Gave the structure of the monomer” and “Included ‘n’ in the answer” were logged together in 2021, and “added ‘n’ in the drawing” again in 2025.CP 2021 Q4(c)(ii); CP 2025 Q4(b)(ii)
“‘Rising temperatures’ (or ‘melting ice’) is an environmental problem.”State the consequence the temperature change brings — sea levels flooding low-lying land, polar ice caps melting; answers without the consequence, and “irrelevant environmental problems, such as acid rain”, were logged.CP 2025 Q4(c)
6 · Master Trap Checklist (cross-cutting)▾
☐ Cracking = chemical decomposition; distillation = physical sorting — never swap the verbs.
☐ Lab cracking: reaction at the porcelain, vaporisation at the glass wool; broken pieces = surface area.
☐ Fraction properties grade with carbon number — and the reason is van der Waals’ forces, naming both substances.
☐ Separation variable is boiling point (not melting), and a fraction has a range, not a point.
☐ Combustion equations: coefficient balance + state symbols on every species — both CP-logged mark-killers.
☐ Gas volumes at r.t.p. use 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹; cm³ ÷ 1000 first.
☐ Gas→harm pairing: CO₂ greenhouse · SO₂/NOₓ acid rain · CO toxic · smoke respiratory; dioxins ≠ fossil fuels.
☐ Measures match machines: converter→car, scrubber→power plant, unleaded/LPG→fuel itself.
☐ Homologous-series definition = four clauses; teach them like a rhyme.
☐ Roots stop at oct- (8 C); locants start at but- (4 C) for both -ene and branches.
☐ The –COOH carbon is C1 of the acid; propenoic acid is an examinable name.
☐ Substitution: UV light + a radical (X·) + HX expelled; excess methane for mono-substitution.
☐ Addition: one product across the C=C; 1,2-dibromo pattern for propene.
☐ Bromine water: orange/brown→colourless; acidified KMnO₄: purple→colourless; always give the negative comparison.
☐ Electron diagrams of Br₂/Cl₂ steps: every lone pair drawn (CP 2012 Q15).
☐ Polymer formulas: brackets around the unit, n outside and once, monomer C=C shown as opened.
☐ Reverse deduction: 2 backbone carbons per unit; atom-audit monomer vs repeating unit.
☐ Benzene/phenyl rings never polymerise and never get their stability explained.
☐ Spell the process: addition polymerisation — and state conditions (high T, high P, catalyst) when a route is asked.
☐ Evaluate plastics two-sided: benefit of the material and the disposal limitation (inert, non-biodegradable; reduce/reuse/recycle).
☐ Say “C=C double bond” when justifying unsaturation — “only stated that propene is an unsaturated molecule without mentioning it has a C=C double bond” (CP 2017 Paper 1B context).
☐ Substitution answer audit: UV light (not heat), the word “substitution” spelled right, mono-substituted product unless told otherwise — all three logged as 2019/2025 slips (CP 2019 Q5(b)–(c); CP 2025 Q4(b)(i)).
☐ Name the medium with every bromine/dichromate/permanganate answer — “use aqueous bromine”, unstated acid conditions, and “K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺” as an alkene test were all logged (CP 2019 Q3(a)(i); CP 2018 Q4(c)(ii); CP 2022 Paper 2 Q3(a)(i)).
☐ Fractional distillation: naming it is the easy mark, explaining it is the lost one — different boiling-point ranges of a mixture (both kerosene and petroleum are mixtures) (CP 2024 Q5(a)).
☐ Cracking-lab reason rows: suck-back explained by only ~half, and only a quarter justified the cold-catalyst case — the porcelain is present but unheated, and kerosene simply vaporises (CP 2024 Q5(b)(ii), Q5(b)(iv)).
☐ Route questions: sequence, cracking equation and bromine-addition equation were the 2020 clean-up — write all three with states (CP 2020 Q8).
