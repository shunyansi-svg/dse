Step 1 — Worked example: the whole chain, in order

Every line below is a line an examiner can credit. The chain is always: volumes → total mass → q → moles of water → ΔH.

Question 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide. The extrapolated temperature rise is 6.80 degrees C. Calculate the enthalpy change of neutralisation. Assume density 1.00 g cm⁻³ and c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Step 1 - total volume, then total mass V_total = 25.0 + 25.0 = 50.0 cm³ m = V_total x density = 50.0 cm³ x 1.00 g cm⁻³ = 50.0 g The mass is the TOTAL solution, not the acid alone. Using 25.0 g here is the single most common error. Step 2 - energy transferred q = m x c x dT = 50.0 g x 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ x 6.80 K = 1421 J dT is the RISE (final minus initial), and it is the SAME number in K and in degrees C. Never add 273. Step 3 - moles of water formed HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, a 1:1 reaction in both the acid and the base. n(H2O) = c x V = 1.00 mol dm⁻³ x (25.0 / 1000) dm³ = 0.0250 mol Both solutions are 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³, so both give 0.0250 mol. The solution is NOT in excess here. Step 4 - enthalpy change per mole dH = -q / n = -1421 J / 0.0250 mol = -56 840 J mol⁻¹ = -56.8 kJ mol⁻¹ The MINUS sign matters: neutralisation is exothermic. Divide by 1000 to convert J to kJ. Sense check The accepted value is about -57.3 kJ mol⁻¹, so -56.8 is close but slightly less exothermic, which is exactly what heat loss to the surroundings does. A value MORE exothermic than -57.3 is impossible for this reaction and means an error in the mass or the moles. Assumptions being made: density and specific heat capacity of the solution are taken as those of water; the reaction goes to completion; heat loss is negligible; the cup, lid and thermometer absorb no heat.
