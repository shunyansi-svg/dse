Topic XIV (a) Cellulose · Chitin

Naturally occurring polymers

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • "explain the properties of cellulose and chitin in terms of their structures" and "compare structural features of cellulose and chitin" (Part I columns) — the two biopolymers the columns name, and nothing else at this depth.
  • Cellulose: unbranched chains of glucose units linked by condensation; the chains run parallel and hydrogen-bond into strong fibres. Exam line to reproduce: "Cellulose consists of numerous hydroxyl groups that can interact strongly with water molecules" (ms bank §5) — hence cotton and paper absorb water.
  • Chitin: the nitrogen-containing counterpart built into arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls; "Chitin consists of strong hydrogen bonds formed between C=O and N-H groups of the adjacent chains" (ms bank §5) — chains already satisfied, so it does not take up water.
  • Identify the two from spectra: chitin's amide side-groups add C=O and N–H absorptions cellulose lacks — an IR comparison examined in Paper 2 (CP 2016 Q3).
  • Relate back to the Compulsory Part: starch and cellulose are both glucose-based condensation polymers; proteins are natural polyamides, so the amide link −CO−NH− foreshadows nylon. Glucose structure must be recall-accurate: "A few candidates drew the following incorrect structures for glucose" (CP 2013 Q2).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The comparison answer the schemes reward (2021-style marking): cellulose –OH forms hydrogen bonds with water → absorbs water; chitin forms C=O···N–H hydrogen bonds between adjacent chains → cannot absorb water (master summary §2.4). Call them intermolecular hydrogen bonds — claiming covalent bonds between the chains scores nothing. Do not describe chitin as a protein: it is a polysaccharide whose monomers carry the amide group. Structure→property answers must pair the forces with the architecture: "Many candidates explained the difference only in terms of intermolecular interactions, but ignored the structural features of the two polymers." (CP 2012 Q2)

Topic XIV (b) Addition · Condensation · Processing

Synthetic polymers and plastics

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • "explain the terms “thermoplastics” and “thermosetting plastics”" (Part I columns) — in scheme wording: soft-when-heated/hard-when-cooled versus "cannot be softened or melted again by heating once set hard" (ms bank §5).
  • "describe the characteristics of addition polymers using examples like PTFE, PMMA and cyanoacrylate" and the condensation family "poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET), nylon, Kevlar and urea-methanal" (Part I columns). Addition: the C=C opens, nothing is lost — "nCH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃ → PMMA (addition polymerisation)" (ms bank §5). Condensation: bifunctional monomers only — "Monomers should be bifunctional" (ms bank §5, OCR read) and "Small molecules are eliminated" (ms bank §5).
  • "deduce the type of polymerisation reaction for a given monomer or a pair of monomers", "deduce the repeating unit" and "write equations for the formation of addition and condensation polymers" (Part I columns) — brackets with 'n' outside, bonds crossing the bracket edge; e.g. "hexanedioic acid + hexane-1,6-diamine → amide bonds + H₂O" (master summary §11.2).
  • "explain the properties of polymers in terms of their structures" (Part I columns) across the named comparisons: HDPE vs LDPE ("HDPE is less branched than LDPE" (ms bank §2)), nylon vs Kevlar, and vulcanisation — sulphur S–S cross-links giving rubber that is "rigid with some flexibility" (CP 2012 Q2).
  • "recognise the applications of polymeric biomaterials" and "describe the process of making biodegradable plastics using PLA as an example" (Part I columns) — polylactide from lactic acid; Biopol (polyhydroxybutyrate, PHB) is the named biopolymer of the activities column, and "PHB is biodegradable" (ms bank §10).
  • "relate the choice of fabrication processes to the properties of plastics and the uses of their products" (Part I columns): injection moulding, blow moulding, extrusion moulding, vacuum forming and compression moulding.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Structure→property answers need both the branching picture and the packing consequence: HDPE (hard bottle) is "more linear, pack closely" while LDPE (soft bottle) is "highly branched, cannot pack closely" (master summary §11.3). Thermosetting hardness must name cross-links — "Cross-linkages / covalent bonds / disulphur linkages are formed between polymer chains" so "polymer chains cannot slip easily over each other" (ms bank §2); intermolecular forces alone were rejected: "Many candidates explained the difference only in terms of intermolecular interactions, but ignored the structural features of the two polymers." (CP 2012 Q2). Kevlar rigidity = rigid benzene rings + intermolecular hydrogen bonds — not cross-linking (CP 2016 Q2), with "Aromatic stacking: interatomic distance between the benzene groups of adjacent polymer molecules" as the scheme's extra credit idea (ms bank §5). Monomer deduction from a condensation polymer stayed hard: "About seventy percent of the candidates were not able to make deduction and hence draw the structures of the two monomers of PBT. Moreover, many candidates wrongly stated the type of polymerisation involved as 'addition polymerisation'." (CP 2016 Q2) Fabrication recall tripped a crowd: "About two thirds of the candidates provided wrong answers including making the plastic bottle through compression moulding, giving PVC / PS / PP as types of PE, and drawing incorrect structures of lactic acid." (CP 2014 Q2) And PMMA is a trick classic — "PMMA is not a thermosetting plastic because its molecules are held by weak van der Waals' forces" (ms bank §5).

Topic XIV (c) Packing · Unit cells · Alloys

Metals and alloys

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • "describe the close-packed and open structures of metals" (Part I columns): close packing illustrated by hexagonal and cubic close-packed structures — "A-B type packing" for zinc (hcp) versus "A-B-C type packing" for copper (ccp) (ms bank §2) — and the open structure illustrated by the body-centred cubic lattice of iron.
  • "identify the unit cell and determine the coordination number of a given metallic structure" (Part I columns): both close packings share "coordination number = 12" (ms bank §2); the open body-centred cubic cell has coordination number 8.
  • Tally atoms per cell with the scheme's arithmetic: "1 + 8 × 1/8 = 2" for the body-centred cell and the recorded ccp tally "12 × (1/4) + 1 = 4" (ms bank §2) — corner ⅛, edge ¼, face ½, interior 1, reduction shown.
  • "recognise that alloys are formed by the introduction of other elements into metals" (Part I columns) — carbon in iron (steel), chromium and nickel in stainless steel, zinc in copper (brass) (ms bank §3).
  • "explain the differences in properties (e.g. hardness and conductivity) between metals and alloys" and "relate the uses of alloys (e.g. steel and brass) to their properties as compared with the pure metals" (Part I columns) via the size-mismatch picture: foreign atoms of different size disrupt the regular layers making it "more difficult for the layers of atoms to slide over one another" (ms bank §3).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The reduction step is markable on its own: "Number of atoms = 1 + 8 × ⅛ = 2 [Reduction process must be shown]" (ms bank §2) — a bare "2" can lose the method marks (the 2012 iron tally, CP 2012 Q2). Name the structure before counting: gold is cubic close-packed with CN 12, while 'body-centered cubic' and CN "13" were the recorded wrong answers (CP 2016 Q2). Malleability wording is non-negotiable: "When an external force is applied, aluminium ions will shift but without breaking the metallic bonds" (ms bank §3) — ions shift, new metallic bonds form, the bonds are non-directional (master summary §2.3); never write "bonds break when hammered". Hardness chains need the size contrast explicit: "Size of carbon atom differs from that of iron atom, introducing carbon into iron makes iron become harder" (ms bank §3), and for corrosion resistance "Introducing chromium into iron makes iron become corrosion resistant" (ms bank §3) — stainless answers must actually name the alloying elements: "Many candidates were not able to suggest the elements other than iron found in stainless steel, and hence failed to explain the properties of stainless steel." (CP 2012 Q2)

Topic XIV (d) Liquid crystals · Nanomaterials

Synthetic materials in modern life

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • "describe the chemical structures and different phases of organic liquid crystals" (Part I columns): on heating, crystalline → smectic (molecules in aligned layers) → nematic (molecules aligned but free-flowing) → isotropic liquid.
  • "identify the structural features of substances that exhibit liquid-crystalline behaviour" (Part I columns) — the scheme triple: "rigid central part, a polar group and a long alkyl chain" (ms bank §2); all three must appear in the answer.
  • "relate the uses of liquid crystals to their properties" (Part I columns): display pixels switch between clear and dark as applied voltage re-orients the molecules between polarised filters; explain the pixel going black through the molecular realignment.
  • "describe nanomaterials as organic or inorganic materials that have particle sizes up to 100 nm" (Part I columns) — the size ceiling is the definition, not a suggestion.
  • "state the uses of nanomaterials" (Part I columns) — the activities column names drug delivery, photodynamic therapy, high-definition phosphors and catalysts; the STSE column adds "The safety and toxicity of new materials, such as nanomaterials, should be considered when they are developed and used." (Part I columns)
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Phase descriptions are where marks die: only about ten percent handled the smectic arrangement and the black-pixel explanation (CP 2015 Q2), and even in the year examiners called it, "A few candidates were able to demonstrate a good understanding of the phases of liquid crystals." (CP 2013 Q2) State the misconception explicitly: "A few candidates wrongly stated that a liquid crystal would become a liquid/gas at very low temperatures." (CP 2013 Q2) — cooling toward the crystalline phase is what happens, not vaporisation. Device questions test vocabulary: "Many candidates failed to demonstrate a correct understanding of the term 'power efficiency'." (CP 2013 Q2) For nanomaterials, "up to 100 nm" (Part I columns) must be quoted with the organic-or-inorganic range, and recall of liquid-crystal molecular formulae is capped out of scope (NTF-2018 II-B XIV (d)) — this is the one strand with a Part II depth ruling.

Topic XIV (e) Principles · Practices · Evaluation

Green chemistry

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • "describe the relation between sustainable development and green chemistry" (Part I columns) — the values column frames it: material resources are finite, and green chemistry reduces or eliminates harm in production processes.
  • Know the principles of green chemistry (Part I columns) and use them as an organising grid: waste prevention, safer synthesis and solvents, energy efficiency, renewable feedstocks, and designing for degradation of products.
  • "understand the green chemistry practices in the production of synthetic materials including the use of less hazardous chemical synthesis and safer solvents and auxiliaries" (Part I columns) — the activities column's examples: supercritical carbon dioxide or water in place of organic solvents, and phase-transfer catalysis in making hexanedioic acid; scheme wording "Less hazardous chemicals are used" (ms bank §10).
  • Use atom economy as a green metric: mass of desired product ÷ total mass of reactants × 100%; an addition polymerisation that retains every reactant atom can be credited as "atom economy of the reaction is 100%" (ms bank §10), and the bank's green pluses read "It has a higher atom economy" / "produces less waste" (ms bank §10).
  • "evaluate processes for the production of synthetic materials using the principles of green chemistry" (Part I columns) — a verdict per principle applied to the named process; e.g. "Not green if synthesis uses highly toxic HCN / toxic CH₃OH / highly corrosive H₂SO₄" (master summary §11.5).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Atom economy is not yield: "Many candidates were unable to differentiate between the terms 'atom economy' and 'reaction yield'." (CP 2013 Paper 2 §C context) and "Many candidates were unable to calculate the atom economy." (CP 2013 Paper 2 §C context) Understand the metric's assumption: "The calculation of atom economy is based on a 100% completed reaction. Most reactions do not go to completion" (ms bank §10). Evaluations must stay tethered to the process named in the question — the standing verdict: "Many candidates just raised a few general principles of green chemistry, without making reference to the methanol production technology as required from the question." (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context) Discussion-quality credit is real: the schemes carry a "Communication mark for green chemistry discussions" (ms bank §10). Bioplastic evaluations need both edges: "Corn starch is a renewable resource" balanced against "Use of corn starch may reduce the food supply / Growing more corn crops may lead to deforestation" (ms bank §10).

Biopolymers Inspector: Cellulose, Chitin & the Natural Giant Molecules

Pick a term

XIV (a)

Nature’s polymers

$$\text{polymer} = -[\text{unit}]_n-$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Polymer = giant molecule of many repeating units joined by covalent bonds; monomer = the molecule they come from.
  • Four families: cellulose, starch (glucose), proteins (amino acids), chitin (N-containing glucose derivative) — cell walls, grains, muscle, exoskeletons, fungi.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • ‘Many atoms’ scores nothing: write repeating units joined; forces act between whole chains, and show at least three units (master summary §11.1).
XIV (a)

Cellulose — straight chains, fibre

$$\beta\text{-glucose} \xrightarrow{\ 1,4\ }\ (\text{C}_6\text{H}_{10}\text{O}_5)_n$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Every second unit flipped → straight, unbranched chains; H-bonds within and between chains bundle them into fibres → tensile strength.
  • –OH absorbs water; locked chains never dissolve, and enzymes cannot hydrolyse β-1,4, so cellulose passes through as roughage.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • ‘No –OH, so insoluble’ is a self-goal. Recall: “A few candidates drew the following incorrect structures for glucose” (CP 2013 Q2).
XIV (a)

Chitin — chain to chain

$$\text{N-acetylglucosamine} \to \text{C}_8\text{H}_{13}\text{NO}_5$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The amide side group gives chitin N.
  • Each unit gives C=O and N–H: “Chitin consists of strong hydrogen bonds formed between C=O and N-H groups of the adjacent chains” (ms bank §5) — tough, rigid, insoluble.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Score = group + location: C=O···N–H between adjacent chains.
XIV (a)

Cellulose vs chitin — water stories

$$\text{O-H}\cdots\text{O-H} \quad \text{vs} \quad \text{C=O}\cdots\text{H-N}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Same: giant glucose-derived chains, H-bonded. Different: –OH versus amide side groups (C=O, N–H) with N present.
  • Water: cellulose –OH bonds with water → absorbs it; chitin’s bonds run between adjacent chains → cannot absorb it (master summary §2.4), compared in (2021 Paper 2 §C context).
  • IR: chitin shows extra C=O, N–H absorptions (CP 2016 Q3).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Bank: “Cellulose consists of numerous hydroxyl groups that can interact strongly with water molecules” (ms bank §5).
  • “about two thirds of the candidates were able to state the similarity and difference between the infrared spectra of cellulose and chitin” (CP 2016 Q3) — state both.
XIV (a)

Starch recap — opposite shape

$$\alpha\text{-glucose} \to \text{amylose} + \text{amylopectin}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • α-1,4 links curl the chain — amylose coiled, amylopectin branched: no fibre packing, exposed –OH bonds with water. Blue-black with iodine; enzymes fit α-1,4 not β-1,4.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • ‘Both are glucose’ earns structure marks only — the mark is α versus β and the shape.
XIV (a)

Proteins recap — the amide link

$$\text{H}_2\text{NCHRCONHCHR'COOH}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Amino acids are bifunctional; linking eliminates water and forms the amide (peptide) link –CO–NH–, and hydrolysis reverses it.
  • Transfer: nylon’s amide link is the one in a dipeptide.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Keep the order –CO–NH–; proteins are condensation polymers, not addition.
XIV (a)→(b)

Condensation idea, from nature

$$\text{HOOC-R-COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N-R'-NH}_2 \to -[\text{CO-R-CO-NH-R'-NH}]_n-$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • A monomer needs two reactive ends; one end caps the chain.
  • Bank recipe: “functional groups at the two ends of the monomers react repeatedly to form amide bonds” and “Small molecules are eliminated” (ms bank §5) — logic reused in nylon, Kevlar.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Most candidates did not demonstrate the understanding that the formation of condensation polymers would involve the elimination of small molecules.” (CP 2013 Q2) — name it, with formula.
XIV (b)

Nylon-6,6 — four CH₂, not six

$$-[\text{CO(CH}_2)_4\text{CONH(CH}_2)_6\text{NH}]_n-$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH (adipic acid monomer)” (ms bank §5) + 1,6-diaminohexane; “hexanedioic acid + hexane-1,6-diamine → amide bonds + H₂O” (master summary §11.2).
  • Amide links form, water eliminated: FOUR –CH₂– between the two C=O, SIX between the two N.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • ‘6,6’ counts whole monomers; carboxyl carbons are not –CH₂–. “In fact, there are only four, instead of six, -CH2 groups between the two C=O bonds in the repeating unit of nylon-6,6.” (CP 2016 Q31)
  • A distractor claimed “The polymerisation in forming it is a hydrolysis process.” (CP 2016 Q31) — formation eliminates water.
XIV (a)+(b)

Addition versus condensation

$$\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 \to -[\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2]_n- \qquad \text{A-A} + \text{B-B} \to \text{chain} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Addition: C=C opens; every atom stays in the chain, no small molecule lost.
  • Condensation: two bifunctional monomers; each linkage eliminates a small molecule.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “many of them were not able to distinguish between condensation polymerisation and addition polymerisation” (CP 2012 Q2).
  • Spelling counts: “condensation (†) polymerisation” (ms bank §5); “NOT accept: ‘additional polymerisation’ (spelling)” (master summary §11.1).
XIV (b)

Monomer deduction — snap

$$-[\text{CO-R-CO-NH-R'-NH}]_n- \ \xrightarrow{\ \text{snap}\ }\ \text{HOOC-R-COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N-R'-NH}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Find the linkage (amide –CO–NH–, ester –CO–O–), then snap: carbonyl takes back –OH, N (or O) takes back H.
  • Copy the middle unchanged; brackets and n stay on the polymer.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Weakness: “Many candidates were not able to give the correct structural formulae of the monomers. Incorrect structural formulae like the following were common” (CP 2012 Q2) — a link atom on the wrong fragment.

The natural → synthetic polymer ladder

Rung 1 · Monomer → linkage

β-glucose → 1,4 glycosidic (cellulose); α-glucose → coiled (starch); amino acid → amide (peptide); N-acetylglucosamine → 1,4 + amide side group (chitin). Synthetic: diacid + diamine → amide; diacid + diol → ester.

Rung 2 · Chain shape → property

Straight, unbranched → parallel chains → bundles → fibre, tensile strength, no flow on heating. Coiled or branched → cannot pack. Synthetic: branching → hardness.

Rung 3 · Forces → water behaviour

Ask who bonds with whom. Cellulose –OH H-bonds with water → absorbs it. Chitin C=O···N–H H-bonds between adjacent chains → water excluded (master summary §2.4). Same force, opposite result.

Rung 4 · Evidence check — IR

Similar: both giant –OH polymers → broad –OH absorption, C–O fingerprint. Different: chitin shows C=O, N–H; cellulose neither (CP 2016 Q3).

Multi-blank Quiz — Naturally Occurring Polymers

1. A polymer is a giant molecule of many repeating units, each from a monomer.
2. Cellulose = β-glucose units joined by 1,4 links; every second unit is flipped, so the chains are straight and unbranched.
3. Cellulose strength comes from hydrogen bonds within and between chains; its hydroxyl (–OH) groups bind water, so it absorbs but does not dissolve.
4. Chitin = N-acetylglucosamine units, so it contains nitrogen; H-bonds form between its C=O and N-H groups on adjacent chains.
5. Cellulose and chitin spectra are similar (both giant –OH polymers); chitin adds C=O and N-H absorptions.
6. Cellulose –OH bonds with water, so it absorbs water; chitin’s C=O···N–H runs between adjacent chains, so it cannot.
7. Starch = α-glucose polymer: coiled amylose, branched amylopectin; blue-black with iodine.
8. Enzymes split starch’s α-1,4 links, not cellulose’s β-1,4 links, whose chains are locked by hydrogen bonds between chains.
9. Proteins = condensation polymers of amino acids, joined by the amide (peptide) link, eliminating water; hydrolysis reverses it.
10. Nylon-6,6 has four –CH₂– between the two C=O groups, six between the two N; links: amide.
11. In condensation polymerisation two bifunctional monomers react with elimination of small molecules.
12. In addition polymerisation the C=C opens and no small molecule is lost; to deduce monomers, snap at the linkage.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Biopolymers

Cellulose vs starch — ‘both are glucose’ is lazy

Opposite architecture from the same monomer: cellulose is β-glucose, straight unbranched chains; starch is α-glucose, a helix plus branches. The mark is α versus β plus shape.

‘Chitin is just cellulose with nitrogen in it’

Almost right, worthless: name the amide side groups (C=O, N–H) and say strong H-bonds form between adjacent chains. Nitrogen alone is not an explanation.

‘Hydrogen bonds’ — WITH what, BETWEEN what?

Unlocated force, unmarked force: cellulose –OH bonds with water, chitin C=O···N–H bonds between chains. “Many candidates explained the difference only in terms of intermolecular interactions, but ignored the structural features of the two polymers.” (CP 2012 Q2)

Nylon-6,6: six CH₂ versus four CH₂

Digits count whole monomers, not –CH₂– runs: four between the C=Os, six between the Ns. “there are only four, instead of six, -CH2 groups between the two C=O bonds in the repeating unit of nylon-6,6.” (CP 2016 Q31)

Condensation versus concentration

Condensation = linking with elimination of a small molecule; concentration = mol dm⁻³. Spelling counts: “condensation (†) polymerisation” (ms bank §5), “NOT accept: ‘additional polymerisation’ (spelling)” (master summary §11.1).

Brackets and n — the drawing that never scores

Repeat unit inside brackets, bonds crossing the wall, n outside, at least three units when units are asked (master summary §11.1). Mirror error: a deduced monomer has no brackets and no n.

‘Natural polymers are not really polymers’

They qualify on the nylon definition: giant molecules of repeating units, built with elimination of water. Use strand (b) discipline — linkage, type, repeat unit (Part I columns).

Multi-blank Quiz — Smart Materials & Green Chemistry

1. Liquid-crystalline behaviour needs a rigid central part, a polar group and a long alkyl chain.
2. The smectic phase = molecules in layers aligned within each; the nematic phase keeps the direction but positions are free.
3. On heating: smectic → nematic → isotropic liquid; orientational order dies at the clearing point.

7. Four named nano uses: drug delivery, photodynamic therapy, high-definition phosphors, catalysts.
8. AE = Mᵣ of desired product ÷ ΣMᵣ of all reactants, × 100%.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Smart Materials & Green Chemistry

Formula cards vs real LC study

Out of scope: “Recall of molecular formulae of substances that exhibit liquid-crystalline behaviour is not expected.” (NTF-2018 II-B XIV (d)) In scope: features, ladder, uses↔properties.

Liquid/gas when very cold?

“A few candidates wrongly stated that a liquid crystal would become a liquid/gas at very low temperatures.” (CP 2013 Q2) Cold drives the ladder toward the crystalline solid.

Smectic vs nematic swap

Smectic = stacked layers, aligned within each; nematic = direction only. Only ~10% described smectic (CP 2015 Q2).

Molecules fixed in place?

A liquid crystal flows — molecules translate and rotate; only orientation is crystal-like. Fixed positions belong to the solid.

Any small particle?

Two clauses (Part I columns): sizes up to 100 nm, organic or inorganic, and properties different from the bulk.

Green = recyclable? AE = yield?

Recycling treats waste after it exists; green chemistry prevents it at design stage. AE is fixed by the equation (100%-completion basis); yield is what a run delivered (CP 2013 Paper 2 §C context).

Slogan green vs process green

Generic green promises score nothing: “Many candidates just raised a few general principles of green chemistry, without making reference to the methanol production technology as required from the question.” (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context)

Topic XIV Materials Workbench

Five benches covering the number-and-language questions Paper 2 electives keep asking: the unit-cell tally with the reduction shown, the coordination-number register, the monomer ↔ repeat-unit pairing, the liquid-crystal feature checklist and the atom-economy calculation. Type, press, and read the working exactly as the mark scheme wants it — the reduction written out, the forces named, the bracket drawn with n outside. Run order that mirrors the exam: Bench 1–2 for a printed crystal diagram, Bench 3 for a printed repeat unit, Bench 4 for a named new material, Bench 5 whenever a green-chemistry part (e) appears.

Bench 1

Unit-cell tally — reduction shown, not just the total

Read the given cell and enter how many spheres sit on each site type. The bench writes the fractional reduction for every site and refuses a total that is not a whole number — a shared atom counted wrongly always shows up.

Caption: the MS requires the reduction written out — its own line reads “Number of atoms = 1 + 8 × ⅛ = 2 [Reduction process must be shown]” (ms bank §2). The output shows which tally you reproduced — the bank records “1 + 8 × 1/8 = 2” (body-centred) and “12 × (1/4) + 1 = 4” (its CCP tally, ms bank §2); the familiar corner + face form 8 × ⅛ + 6 × ½ = 4 is the school-standard derivation of the same four atoms, not a bank line. CP 2012 Q2 on the iron cell: “Some candidates correctly calculated the number of iron atoms in a unit cell. However, they failed to present the deduction process.” — and the recorded wrong tally “⅛×8 + 1 = 3” is worth reading closely: the expression itself reduces to 2, not 3, so the line contradicts its own arithmetic — exactly the kind of slip Bench 1 refuses to output. Use the simple-cubic preset (8 × ⅛ = 1) as the control case before testing the harder diagrams. The shown-reduction complaint of 2012 has successors across 2017–2025: copper returned a count of 13 in 2017 (CP 2017 2 Q2), aluminium drew the brute count 14 with the working hidden in 2022 (CP 2022 2 Q2), and the 2025 titanium slide still marks bare answers down — "A small number of candidates only gave the answer without any deduction." (CP 2025 2 Q2) The bench's rule is the examiner's rule.

Bench 2

Coordination decider — name → CN in one register

Pick the structure type and the bench returns the stacking wording, the neighbour count and the matching cell tally — the three facts the 2012 / 2015 / 2016 triple asks in order.

Bank wording: “coordination number = 12” for both close-packed types, “A-B-C type packing” (Cu, ccp) vs “A-B type packing” (Zn, hcp) (ms bank §2). Body-centred cubic is the open structure — CN 8, not 12 (iron at room temperature is the standard example, and the same iron cell reappears in Bench 1). Register check for ionic lattices: NaCl is quoted 6 : 6 (each Na⁺ surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ and vice versa) — “13” never exists in any of these registers; it was the recorded gold-lattice error (2016 2 Q2). Drill protocol: read the answer aloud with the stacking phrase first and the number second (“cubic close-packed, A-B-C stacking, coordination number 12”) — that sentence order is what earns both marks. The 2017–2025 decks keep the ask honest: iron's coordination number passed only about a third of candidates in 2024 (CP 2024 2 Q2), titanium's about the same in 2025 (CP 2025 2 Q2), and the stainless-steel iron atom again about a third in 2021 (CP 2021 Paper 2 context).

Bench 4

Liquid-crystal verdict — does the molecule have mesogen features?

Tick the structural features the drawn molecule shows. The bench returns the same verdict paragraph the mark scheme awards: features present → rod-like molecule with a dipole and a fluid tail → a liquid-crystalline phase between the melting and clearing temperatures.

Bench 5

Atom economy — the green-chemistry metric from the equation

Load a preset or type formula masses. Everything is read off the balanced equation:

$\text{atom economy} = \dfrac{M_r(\text{desired product})}{\sum M_r(\text{all reactants})} \times 100\%$ — note the bank lines: “Atom economy = 58/76 = 76.32%” and “The calculation of atom economy is based on a 100% completed reaction. Most reactions do not go to completion” (ms bank §10). It is read from the EQUATION, so it is NOT the same as percentage yield — candidates who could not separate the two terms lost the comparison mark (CP 2013 Paper 2 §C context). The ethene preset shows the bank’s other line, “atom economy of the reaction is 100%” (ms bank §10) — addition polymerisations waste no atoms, which is exactly why the comparison sentence writes itself. For the evaluation half: “It has a higher atom economy” / “produces less waste” / “Less hazardous chemicals are used” (ms bank §10) — and each line must be pinned to the named process, not recited as a general principle.

Why this matters — the exam chain

Paper 2 §C crystal question chain (it has run iron → copper → gold, i.e. (2012 2 Q2) → (2015 2 Q2) → (2016 2 Q2)): name the structure (use the exact stacking wording from Bench 2) → give the coordination number (Bench 2 — 12 close-packed, 8 body-centred, never 13) → tally the atoms with the reduction written out (Bench 1 — the bare total earns nothing if the deduction is missing). Practise all three steps aloud on a printed cell diagram until the sentence order is automatic. The chain did not stop in 2016 — copper 2017 (2017 2 Q2), platinum 2020 (CP 2020 Paper 2 context), aluminium 2022 (2022 2 Q2), silver 2023 (2023 2 Q2), iron 2024 (2024 2 Q2), titanium 2025 (2025 2 Q2).

Polymer question chain (Bench 3): read the repeat unit → decide addition vs condensation from whether a small molecule left → split at the linkage to recover the monomer structures → re-draw with n outside the bracket. The same knowledge returns as MC asking you to count atoms inside a repeat unit — (2016 1 Q31), where four-vs-six CH₂ was the whole question.

LCD question chain (Bench 4): tick the three structural features → walk the phase ladder (crystalline → smectic → nematic → isotropic) → explain one pixel: no voltage → the aligned molecules let light through; voltage applied → they reorient and the pixel appears black (asked in (2013 2 Q2) and (2015 2 Q2)).

Green evaluation (Bench 5): calculate atom economy for each route → name the 100% route or the higher-% route → judge hazard and waste of the actual reagents, referencing the named process every time. A correct number with a generic green slogan is half an answer.

4 · Liquid crystals · nano · green

Structure: “liquid crystal behaviour” needs a “rigid central part, a polar group and a long alkyl chain” (ms bank §2); organic LCs only.

Phases: heat: solid → smectic (layers, aligned within) → nematic (aligned, no layers) → isotropic (random); cooling reverses.

Pixel: unpowered twist guides light; “when voltage is applied” molecules align, twist unwinds, light blocked → black; little energy per switch → power-efficient (CP 2015 Q2; CP 2013 Q2).

Guard NTF-2018 II-B XIV (d): “Recall of molecular formulae of substances that exhibit liquid-crystalline behaviour is not expected.” Structures, phases, uses↔properties ARE. Cold end crystalline - never “liquid/gas at very low temperatures” (CP 2013 Q2).

Nano: “organic or inorganic materials that have particle sizes up to 100 nm” (Part I columns) - cap not floor; huge surface-to-volume ratio. Uses: drug delivery · photodynamic therapy · high-definition phosphors · catalysts. STSE: “The safety and toxicity of new materials, such as nanomaterials, should be considered when they are developed and used.”

Principles: waste prevention · atom economy · less hazardous syntheses · safer solvents · energy efficiency · renewables · catalysis.

Practices: supercritical CO₂/water solvents; Ziegler–Natta catalysis for PE - “Less hazardous chemicals are used” (ms bank §10); corn-starch PLA - “Corn starch is a renewable resource”, yet “Use of corn starch may reduce the food supply / Growing more corn crops may lead to deforestation” (ms bank §10); Biopol - “PHB is biodegradable”, “PHB undergoes hydrolysis in the presence of acids / alkalis” (ms bank §10).

AE: (Mᵣ desired ÷ ΣMᵣ reactants) × 100% · “Atom economy = 58/76 = 76.32%” · “The calculation of atom economy is based on a 100% completed reaction. Most reactions do not go to completion” (ms bank §10). AE ≠ yield (CP 2013 Paper 2 §C context); addition polymerisations are 100%-AE.

Bite the process: “Many candidates just raised a few general principles of green chemistry, without making reference to the methanol production technology as required from the question.” (CP 2012 Paper 2 §C context)
