Topic VII Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(e))

Topic VII (a) Cells in daily life

Primary and secondary cells — choosing a cell by its characteristics

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Distinguish primary and secondary cells: a primary cell cannot be recharged (reactants consumed / products escape); a secondary cell (accumulator) can be recharged — its cell reaction is reversible when current is forced back through it.
  • Describe the characteristics of the six named cells: zinc-carbon cell, alkaline manganese cell, silver oxide cell, lithium ion cell, nickel metal hydride (NiMH) cell and lead-acid accumulator — voltage, rechargeability, size, capacity, price.
  • Typical voltages: zinc-carbon / alkaline manganese / silver oxide ≈ 1.5 V (primary); lithium ion ≈ 3.6–3.7 V (rechargeable); NiMH ≈ 1.2 V (rechargeable); lead-acid ≈ 2 V per cell, 12 V battery (rechargeable).
  • Justify uses for particular purposes: button silver oxide cell in watches (small, steady voltage); alkaline manganese in high-drain devices (higher capacity); lithium ion in phones/laptops (light, rechargeable, high voltage); NiMH in hybrid cars (rechargeable, high capacity); lead-acid in car starting (large current, cheap); zinc-carbon in clocks/remotes (cheapest, low drain).
  • Understand the environmental impact of using dry cells: heavy-metal disposal, landfill, why rechargeable cells and recycling reduce waste.
  • Depth cap: structures and working principles of these commercial cells are NOT expected — only the characteristics above (NTF-2018 II-B VII (a)).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Do not invent cell innards: the exact ruling is “Describing structures and working principles of zinc-carbon cell, alkaline manganese cell, silver oxide cell, lithium ion cell, nickel metal hydride (NiMH) cell and lead-acid accumulator are not expected” (NTF-2018 II-B VII (a)). Answers should be characteristics-shaped: “rechargeable / not rechargeable, voltage, capacity, size, price”. Lead-acid application questions given information still defeated candidates (“more than half … weak understanding about lead-acid accumulators”, CP 2015 Q4) — practise reading the stem, not memorising diagrams.

Topic VII (b) Simple chemical cells

Two electrodes, one electrolyte — and the half-cell upgrade with a salt bridge

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Build and describe a cell of two metal electrodes in an electrolyte (e.g. zinc and copper strips in dilute sulphuric acid); measure the voltage with a voltmeter connected across the electrodes.
  • The more reactive metal is the negative electrode (anode): it is oxidised and dissolves, e.g. $\text{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$; at the positive electrode (cathode) reduction occurs, e.g. $2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{H}_{2}\text{(g)}$.
  • Electrons flow in the external circuit from the negative electrode (anode) to the positive electrode (cathode); current in the solution is carried by migrating ions, never by electrons.
  • Explain the problems of the simple single-electrolyte cell: the electrolyte reacts directly with the more reactive metal, so the cell “runs down” even when switched off and the voltage falls quickly.
  • Build metal–metal ion half cells joined by a salt bridge / porous device: functions are to complete the electrical circuit by ion migration and to keep the two solutions from mixing directly.
  • Write the half equations and overall cell equation (e.g. $\text{Zn(s)} + \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}$); explain differences in voltage between cells by the distance between the two metals in the electrochemical series — wider gap, larger voltage.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Two CP-recorded killers: the salt bridge / lemon “allows electrons to pass through” (wrong — ions migrate through the bridge; electrons only in the wire, CP 2012 Q3), and the half-equation written at the wrong electrode — “wrongly wrote that Cu²⁺(aq) is reduced to Cu(s)” where copper was being oxidised (CP 2012 Q3). A voltmeter measures voltage — CP notes candidates “using a voltmeter to measure the current of a circuit” (CP 2015 Q5). Electrode size and electrolyte volume change capacity, not the voltage.

Topic VII (c) Redox concepts

Redox in three registers — oxygen/hydrogen, electron transfer, oxidation numbers

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Identify oxidation and reduction from all three bases required by the syllabus: (i) gain or loss of oxygen / hydrogen atom(s), (ii) gain or loss of electron(s), (iii) change in oxidation number — OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).
  • Assign oxidation numbers to atoms in elements and compounds using the taught rules: element = 0; monatomic ion = charge; H is +I (−I in metal hydrides); O is −II (−I in peroxides such as H₂O₂); Group I +I, Group II +II, Al +III, F −I; the sum equals the charge on the species.
  • Use changes to classify agents: oxidation-number increase → substance oxidised → reducing agent; oxidation-number decrease → substance reduced → oxidising agent. The MS pattern: “Oxidation number of Mn decreases / changes from +4 to +3 ∴ MnO₂(s) is the oxidising agent” (ms bank).
  • Describe the chemistry of the common oxidising agents named in the syllabus: acidified $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}/\text{H}^{+}$ (purple → colourless), acidified $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}/\text{H}^{+}$ (orange → green), $\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)}$ (yellow → pale green), $\text{Cl}_{2}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{HNO}_{3}\text{(aq)}$ of different concentrations, conc. $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}\text{(l)}$.
  • Describe the common reducing agents: $\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{Zn(s)}$ — and what they are changed into ($\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$, $\text{I}_{2}$, $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$).
  • Construct the ECS trend: strong reducing agents (metals) at the top of the series with their ions below; the lower a metal ion sits, the stronger its oxidising power; reducing power of the metal decreases down the series.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The agent is the whole reactant species, named with its own ON change — “identifying oxidising/reducing agent with oxidation number change” is the mark pair (ms bank). “Quite a number of candidates wrongly suggested Zn as the oxidising agent and wrongly wrote: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻” — that equation shows Zn is the reducing agent (CP 2016 Q8). Concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulphuric acid are oxidising agents: “wrongly stated that hydrogen gas would be produced in their reactions with copper” (CP 2014 Paper 1B context). Reagent names carry “acidified” — omitting it was recorded (CP 2014 Q14).

Topic VII (c) Balancing redox

Balancing equations for redox reactions — half equations or oxidation-number changes

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Write ionic half equations for reduction and oxidation, with charges and state symbols — e.g. $\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)} + \text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$ and $2\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{I}_{2}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$.
  • Half-equation recipe (acidic): balance the atom undergoing redox; balance O by adding $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$; balance H by adding $\text{H}^{+}$; balance charge by adding $\text{e}^{-}$. Example: $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}\text{(aq)} + 8\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 5\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 4\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$.
  • Combine halves: scale so electrons lost = electrons gained, add, cancel $\text{e}^{-}$ — the overall ionic equation never contains free electrons.
  • Oxidation-number method: find ON changes per atom × atoms exchanged → cross-multiply to make electrons transferred equal → balance the rest by inspection (e.g. $\text{Cl}_{2}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^{-}\text{(aq)} + \text{I}_{2}\text{(aq)}$, Cl 0 → −I, I −I → 0).
  • Work in alkaline / neutral media where the syllabus needs it — the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell uses $\text{OH}^{-}$ half equations, never $\text{H}^{+}$ there.
  • Read what is asked: ionic half equation ≠ overall ionic equation ≠ complete (molecular) equation; some candidates “just wrote down the half equation for the reduction of permanganate ion, but did not give the ionic equation for the reaction” (CP 2014 Q9).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The mark sits on “correct half-equation with state symbols” (ms bank) — a chargeless or stateless half-equation can lose outright. “Some candidates gave a complete equation instead of an ionic equation” (CP 2012 Q7): answer exactly the form named. Spectators stay out: $\text{K}^{+}$, $\text{NO}_{3}^{-}$ do not appear in “acidified potassium permanganate” half-equations. Never alter subscripts to balance — only coefficients.

Topic VII (d) Cells: inert & fuel

Redox in chemical cells — zinc-carbon, inert electrodes, hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Zinc-carbon cell: know it as the cheapest 1.5 V primary cell (zinc case, graphite rod) and its uses; its internal paste chemistry is not expected — when the question supplies the electrode equations, apply your concepts to them (NTF-2018 II-B VII (a), (d)).
  • Chemical cells with inert electrodes: construct and explain cells such as $\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}^{3+}$ or $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}/\text{Mn}^{2+}$ pairs in separate beakers with platinum (or graphite) electrodes and a salt bridge; the electrode is a conductor and electron-transfer surface only — it does not react.
  • Predict changes at each half cell and write the half equations and overall ionic equation for an inert-electrode cell, e.g. $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ reduced at the positive electrode while $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is oxidised at the negative electrode; use the given electrochemical series for the direction.
  • Hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell (alkaline electrolyte): negative: $\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$; positive: $\text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 4\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$; overall: $2\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$ — both taken verbatim from the MS bank.
  • State the pros and cons of a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell: continuous power while fuel is supplied, high efficiency, quiet, only water emitted (no $\text{CO}_{2}$, CO, $\text{NO}_{x}$) vs. cost, storage/flammability of $\text{H}_{2}$, and the environmental cost of making the hydrogen itself.
  • With given information, “apply the concepts of electrochemistry to solve problems involving more complicated chemical cells” (NTF-2018 II-B VII (d)).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

A fuel cell is not a “rechargeable battery”: it runs as long as gas is fed; the “advantage” mark is “only water is emitted … do not emit pollutant gases such as CO₂, CO and NOₓ” (CP 2013), and hydrogen-sourcing questions demanded an environmental comment — “answers merely stating the fact would receive no marks” (CP 2013). In inert-electrode cell stoichiometry the ratio is the mark: “the mole ratio of ‘Fe²⁺(aq) to Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq)’ in the overall equation is ‘6:1’” (CP 2015 Q16).

Topic VII (e) Electrolysis

Electrolysis — decomposition by electricity, preferential discharge, plating & copper purification

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte (molten or aqueous) by direct current; an electrolytic cell converts electrical energy → chemical energy — the reverse of a chemical cell. Describe the materials needed: container, electrodes (inert or active), electrolyte, d.c. supply, wires.
  • Name the parts correctly: electrode joined to the positive terminal = anode (oxidation); electrode joined to the negative terminal = cathode (reduction); cations → cathode, anions → anode — shown in migration experiments (coloured ions on wet filter paper / gel).
  • In aqueous electrolysis list all four ion species (metal ion, $\text{H}^{+}$, non-metal ion, $\text{OH}^{-}$ from water) before choosing products — “some candidates … omitted H⁺ and OH⁻ in their answers” (CP 2015 Q7).
  • Predict preferential discharge from the three syllabus factors: position in the electrochemical series (lower cation discharged; $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ beats $\text{H}^{+}$, but $\text{H}^{+}$ beats $\text{Na}^{+}$), concentration of ions (concentrated $\text{Cl}^{-}$ → $\text{Cl}_{2}$; dilute → $\text{OH}^{-}$ → $\text{O}_{2}$), and nature of the electrode (a copper anode dissolves; a mercury cathode gives sodium amalgam).
  • Master the named examples: dilute $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ (essentially water: $\text{H}_{2}$ at cathode, $\text{O}_{2}$ at anode, 2:1 by volume); $\text{NaCl}$ solutions of different concentrations (dilute → $\text{H}_{2}$ + $\text{O}_{2}$, solution turns alkaline near the cathode; concentrated/brine → $\text{H}_{2}$ + $\text{Cl}_{2}$, NaOH forms); $\text{CuSO}_{4}$ solution (Cu deposited + $\text{O}_{2}$ with inert electrodes; blue fades, solution acidifies).
  • Industrial applications: electroplating (object = cathode, plating metal = anode, electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal) and purification of copper (impure copper anode, pure copper cathode, $\text{CuSO}_{4}$(aq)); state anodic/cathodic reactions, overall reaction, observable changes in solution, and the environmental impact of the electroplating industry.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Use the MS register verbatim: “$\text{Cl}^{-}$(aq) ions are discharged at the anode to give $\text{Cl}_{2}$(g)”; “$\text{H}^{+}$(aq) ions are discharged at the cathode” (ms bank) — ions are discharged, metals are deposited, and sacrificial magnesium is oxidised, never “preferentially discharged” (CP 2012 Q9). Observation answers must pair electrode and solution: candidates “wrongly stated the expected changes at the electrodes … without referring to the observable changes that would occur in the solution” (CP 2015 Q7). Name brine as concentrated sodium chloride solution (CP 2013), and the product of iodide discharge as “$\text{I}_{2}$ which dissolves in KI(aq) to give brown $\text{I}_{3}^{-}$ ions” — not a brown solid or gas (CP 2013; ms bank).

Oxidation Numbers & Agents Inspector

The accounting system behind every redox mark: assign the numbers, spot the change, name the agent — in the HKEAA’s own register.

Pick a concept

VII (c)

Oxidation — three definitions, one event

$$\text{Oxidation} = \begin{cases} \text{gain of oxygen / loss of hydrogen} \\ \text{loss of electron(s)} \\ \text{increase in oxidation number} \end{cases}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The syllabus requires identification on all three bases: gain/loss of oxygen/hydrogen atom(s), gain/loss of electron(s), and change in oxidation number.
  • $\text{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$: zinc loses electrons — oxidised — its oxidation number rises 0 → +II.
  • In $\text{CuO} + \text{H}_{2} \rightarrow \text{Cu} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ hydrogen gains oxygen (old view) and its oxidation number rises 0 → +I — the definitions must be kept in step.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Quote the basis the question names (“in terms of oxidation number…”) — answering on the wrong register is a classic zero.
  • The CP general comments single out confusion of the basic concepts themselves (CP 2012 general comments) — rehearse all three wordings per reaction you meet.
VII (c)

Reduction — three views, mirrored

$$\text{Reduction} = \begin{cases} \text{loss of oxygen / gain of hydrogen} \\ \text{gain of electron(s)} \\ \text{decrease in oxidation number} \end{cases}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • $\text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cu(s)}$: copper(II) ion gains electrons — reduced — oxidation number +II → 0.
  • “Oxidation number of Mn decreases / changes from +4 to +3 ∴ $\text{MnO}_{2}$(s) is the oxidising agent” — the MS pattern pairs the decrease with the agent name (ms bank).
  • Reduction always happens at an electrode in cells and electrolysis: cathode — “Red Cat” (reduction at the cathode in both cell types).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A negative oxidation-number change still counts as a decrease: “changes from +3 to +2” is reduction of iron (ms bank: “The oxidation number of iron changes from +3 to +2”).
  • Never write “is reduced to” with the wrong particle: read the stem — CP recorded $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ “reduced” at an electrode where oxidation actually occurred (CP 2012 Q3).
VII (c)

Oxidation number — what it is

$$\text{ON} = \text{the charge an atom would have if bonding electrons were assigned to the more electronegative partner}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • An oxidation number is a book-keeping charge (may be signed like a charge but is not one) — it lets you track electron ownership through a reaction.
  • Values are written with sign before number (+II, −I) for teaching; ionic charges are written number-then-sign (Ca²⁺, $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$).
  • The sum rule converts formulas into arithmetic: Σ(oxidation numbers) = 0 for a neutral species, = ionic charge for an ion.
  • Values >0 rising ⇒ oxidised; falling ⇒ reduced — the same number is your redox detector and your mole-ratio source.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “To help students understand … the concept of redox reactions is introduced” — the syllabus treats oxidation number as a tool, so show the working with signs, exactly as the MS bank does: “−1 + 2 = +1” for N in an oxide (ms bank).
VII (c)

ON rules — the taught set

$$\text{element } = 0;\quad \text{ion } = \text{charge};\quad \text{H } + \text{I},\ \text{O } - \text{II};\quad \Sigma = \text{charge}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Atoms in the free element have oxidation number 0: Na(s), $\text{Cl}_{2}$(aq), $\text{O}_{2}$(g), diamond.
  • Monatomic ions: oxidation number = ionic charge — $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is +III, $\text{Cl}^{-}$ is −I.
  • H is +I in compounds (exception: −I in metal hydrides such as NaH); O is −II in compounds (exception: −I in peroxides such as $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}_{2}$).
  • Fixed metals and fluorine: Group I +I, Group II +II, Al +III, F always −I; other halides are −I unless combined with O or a more electronegative halogen.
  • Sum rule: total = 0 for a compound; total = the ion’s charge for a polyatomic ion — this closes the equation for the unknown atom.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Determining oxidation numbers of atoms in elements and compounds” is a listed learning activity — expect one quick mark per species, so drill the defaults until automatic.
  • Variable oxidation numbers are real and examinable: “Copper exhibits variable oxidation numbers … +1 in $\text{Cu}_{2}\text{O}$ to +2 in $\text{CuSO}_{4}$” (ms bank).
VII (c)

ON worked examples — the exam shapes

$$x + 4(-2) = -1 \;\rightarrow\; x = +7 \quad (\text{Mn in MnO}_{4}^{-})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$: 2x + 7(−II) = −II ⇒ Cr = +VI. $\text{Fe}_{2}\text{O}_{3}$: Fe = +III (rust’s iron is already fully oxidised — “the oxidation number of iron changes from +3 to +2” therefore signals reduction (ms bank)).
  • $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}_{2}$: peroxide O = −I (H stays +I). NaH: hydride H = −I because Na is +I.
  • MS working you should copy: “the oxidation number of N in $\text{HONH}_{2}$(aq) is −1 → oxidation number of N in this oxide = −1 + 2 = +1” — i.e. N₂O (ms bank).
  • After $\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}$ oxidation to $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$, S stays +IV → +VI: two electrons per sulfite — the ratio feeds every stoichiometry mark.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Carry the signs in every step and state the answer “the oxidation number of Mn is +7” — bare “7” can lose the method mark.
  • Use the Solver tab Bench 1 to check assignments with full working before you trust your answer in class.
VII (c)

Oxidising agent

$$\text{oxidising agent} = \text{gains electrons} = \text{is reduced} = \text{its ON decreases}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition word-perfect: an oxidising agent oxidises another substance while itself being reduced (it accepts electrons).
  • Spot it by the decreasing number: $\text{MnO}_{2}$ with Mn(+IV) → Mn(+III/II) is “the oxidising agent” (ms bank); $\text{Fe}^{3+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}$ marks $\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)}$ as the agent (CP 2016 Q8 context).
  • “It oxidises” refers to what it does to something else; the agent always undergoes the opposite change itself.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The mark pairs agent + evidence: “1 mark for identifying oxidising/reducing agent with oxidation number change” (ms bank) — name the species and quote the decrease.
  • “Quite a number of candidates wrongly suggested Zn as the oxidising agent and wrongly wrote: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻” — that very equation (number rising) proves Zn is the reducing agent (CP 2016 Q8).
VII (c)

Reducing agent

$$\text{reducing agent} = \text{loses electrons} = \text{is oxidised} = \text{its ON increases}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition: a reducing agent reduces another substance while itself being oxidised (it donates electrons).
  • Syllabus list: $\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$, Zn(s) — each shows a rising number: S +IV→+VI, I −I→0, Fe +II→+III, Zn 0→+II.
  • Metals are reducing agents because they release electrons — “Mg higher than Cu in ECS → Mg releases electrons” (ms bank summary).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Sodium sulphite is a reducing agent, sodium sulphate is not (S already +VI, nothing left to give) — the key idea behind the 2013 MC (CP 2013 Q22).
  • Explain colour changes through the agent language: $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ turns acidified permanganate colourless because $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is oxidised and “$\text{VO}_{2}^{+}$ received 2 electrons from $\text{SO}_{2}$”-style electron book-keeping earns the mark (teaching example in this style; the CP-verified bookkeeping item is the 2016 MnO₂ +4→+3 titration, CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
VII (c)

Common oxidising agents — the colour ledger

$$\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}\text{(aq)} + 8\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 5\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 4\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Acidified $\text{KMnO}_{4}$: purple $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ (Mn +VII) → almost colourless $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ (+II) — 5 electrons per Mn.
  • Acidified $\text{K}_{2}\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}$: orange $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$ (Cr +VI) → green $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ (+III) — 6 electrons per dichromate.
  • $\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)}$ (yellow) → $\text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$ (pale green): one-electron agent. $\text{Cl}_{2}\text{(aq)}$ (pale green) → $\text{Cl}^{-}$: halogen displacement agent.
  • $\text{HNO}_{3}$ of different concentrations and concentrated $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ are oxidising acids: with copper they give brown $\text{NO}_{2}$ / choking $\text{SO}_{2}$ — never hydrogen.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Say “acidified potassium permanganate / dichromate” — omission was a recorded error (CP 2014 Q14); positive tests read “purple → colourless” and “orange → green” (ms summary F.3).
  • Concentrated sulphuric acid and concentrated nitric acid with copper: candidates “wrongly stated that hydrogen gas would be produced” (CP 2014 Paper 1B context) — dilute $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ with a metal above hydrogen gives $\text{H}_{2}$; concentrated oxidising acids give reduced S / N products instead.
⚠️ Safety line

Concentrated $\text{HNO}_{3}$ / $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ and $\text{NO}_{2}$ / $\text{SO}_{2}$ evolved from them are corrosive and toxic — demonstrations belong in a fume cupboard, with dilute-acid microscale alternatives where the syllabus activities allow.

VII (c)

Common reducing agents — the partner ledger

$$\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{SO}_{4}^{2-}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Sulfite → sulfate: S +IV → +VI gives 2 $\text{e}^{-}$ — sulphite solution decolourises acidified permanganate; sulfate does not react (top of its range).
  • Iodide → iodine: $2\text{I}^{-} \rightarrow \text{I}_{2} + 2\text{e}^{-}$; the liberated $\text{I}_{2}$ “dissolves in KI(aq) to give brown $\text{I}_{3}^{-}$ ions” (ms bank).
  • $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{e}^{-}$: pale-green iron(II) fades to yellow as it reduces $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$ to green $\text{Cr}^{3+}$.
  • Zn(s) (0 → +II) is the syllabus metal reducing agent — it also powers cells as the anode; carbon monoxide’s role (Fe₂O₃ → Fe) is the Topic III echo.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Distinguishing sulphite from sulphate: “acidified potassium permanganate solution” is the accepted reagent (CP 2013 Q22) — describe the colour change both ways (test tube comparison).
  • A substance is a reducing agent only if its element can climb: name the element and its two numbers.
VII (c)

Oxidising power & reducing power — the ECS ladder

$$\text{K} \ldots \text{Zn} \ldots \text{Cu} \ldots \text{Ag}: \ \text{reducing power} \downarrow,\quad \text{oxidising power of the ions} \uparrow$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Top metal = strongest reducing agent (most willing to release electrons); its ion = weakest oxidising agent. Bottom metal’s ion is the stronger oxidising agent.
  • The syllabus outcome: “construct a general trend of the reducing power of metals and the oxidising power of metal ions” and “relate the trends … to their positions in a given electrochemical series”.
  • Feasibility prediction: a redox reaction is favoured when the stronger reducing agent meets the stronger oxidising agent — e.g. Zn reduces $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ because Zn sits lower (as a metal) and $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is above $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ as an ion.
  • Use stronger / weaker — the MS bank explicitly rejects “better oxidising agent”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Confused ‘reducing power’ with ‘oxidising power’, and arranged the species in the reversed order” (CP 2012 Q3) — decide first which property the sentence asks for, then read the ladder once.
  • Relative oxidising power of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ vs $\text{H}^{+}$ decided the 2015 plating item’s cathode product — learn the comparison habit (CP 2015 Q7).

The Redox Judgement Hub — four passes over one equation

Every Paper 1B redox part is the same route: classify the event, name the agent, read the numbers, decide feasibility. Master the passes and nothing new can surprise you.

Pass 1 · Oxygen/hydrogen register

Gained oxygen or lost hydrogen → oxidised. Lost oxygen or gained hydrogen → reduced. Fast and safe for $\text{CuO} + \text{H}_{2}$-type reactions; fails silently for electron-only redox such as $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+}$.

Pass 2 · Electron register

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain. Write the two half-equations beside the equation — the species losing $\text{e}^{-}$ is oxidised; what is written as $\rightarrow + n\text{e}^{-}$ is an oxidation.

Pass 3 · Oxidation-number register

Assign ON to every atom with the rules table; anything that rises is oxidised, anything that falls is reduced. Sum rule with signs: $x + 4(-2) = -1 \Rightarrow x = +7$. The bank’s own working style.

Pass 4 · Agent & feasibility

Species reduced = oxidising agent; species oxidised = reducing agent. Feasibility: stronger reducing agent + stronger oxidising agent → reaction, judged from the given electrochemical series (syllabus overview: “feasibility … predicted by comparing … positions in the electrochemical series”).

Multi-blank Quiz — Oxidation Numbers & Agents

1. Oxidation is the loss of electrons and the increase in oxidation number; reduction is the gain of electrons (OIL RIG).
2. The syllabus accepts redox identification on three bases: gain/loss of oxygen or hydrogen atoms, gain/loss of electrons, and changes in oxidation numbers.
3. Oxidation numbers: any free element is 0; a monatomic ion equals its charge; in compounds H is +I and O is −II.
4. Two famous exceptions: oxygen is −I in the peroxide H₂O₂ (hydrogen stays +I), and hydrogen is −I in metal hydrides such as NaH.
5. In $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ the oxidation number of Mn is +VII (+7), found from $x + 4 \times$ −II = −I.
6. In $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$ chromium is +VI; in the reduction product $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ it is +III; the solution turns from orange to green.
7. The MS bank finds nitrogen in HONH₂ at −1 and in the oxide produced at +1, identifying the gas as N₂O.
8. If Mn’s oxidation number “changes from +4 to +3”, the manganese has been reduced and $\text{MnO}_{2}$(s) is the oxidising agent — the mark needs both the agent name and the ON change (ms bank).
9. Writing Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ proves zinc is the reducing agent, because its oxidation number increases — calling Zn “the oxidising agent” was a CP-recorded error (CP 2016 Q8).
10. Down the electrochemical series from K to Ag, the reducing power of the metals decreases while the oxidising power of the metal ions increases; write stronger, never “better” (ms bank).
11. Copper + concentrated nitric acid gives brown NO₂, not hydrogen — concentrated $\text{HNO}_{3}$ and concentrated $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ are oxidising agents (CP 2014 Paper 1B context); and reagent names must include the word acidified (CP 2014 Q14).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Oxidation Numbers & Agents

“Is oxidised” vs “is the oxidising agent”

Opposite sentences about opposite substances. The species whose number rises is oxidised — and it is the reducing agent. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻: zinc is oxidised, zinc is the reducing agent (CP 2016 Q8).

Reducing power vs oxidising power

Two opposite ladders on one series. Ask “who gives electrons?” (metal, top-strong) or “who takes them?” (ion, bottom-strong) before ordering — CP’s recorded reversal slip (CP 2012 Q3).

Oxidation number vs ionic charge

Same integer, different meaning and notation: the ion is Ca²⁺ (number-then-sign, real charge); the oxidation number is +II (sign-then-number, book-keeping). Writing “Mn⁷⁺” for permanganate’s manganese misstates a covalent atom as a bare ion.

“Oxygen was added” ⇒ always oxidation?

Yes — but the other substance loses, not gains: when iron gains oxygen to rust, iron is the reducing agent corroding; the O₂ is the oxidising agent. Three-register reading (O/H, e⁻, ON) keeps the labels straight (CP 2014 Q3: corrosion is redox).

O always −II · H always +I?

Defaults, with two syllabus-visible exceptions: peroxide $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}_{2}$ gives O = −I; hydride ions in NaH give H = −I. If a “default” makes the sum nonsense, that is your cue an exception is present.

“Discharge” vs “oxidise” vs “deposit”

Ions in electrolysis are discharged; a metal coming out of solution is deposited; a protecting metal such as magnesium is oxidised/corrodes preferentially — “magnesium is preferentially discharged” earned nothing (CP 2012 Q9).

Acid + metal ⇒ hydrogen?

Only non-oxidising acids (dilute $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$, HCl) with metals above hydrogen. Concentrated $\text{HNO}_{3}$ (any concentration — it is an oxidising agent) and concentrated $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ give reduced N / S gases instead; “hydrogen with copper + these acids” is a recorded wrong answer (CP 2014 Q5; CP 2013 Q22 context).

Half-Equations & Redox Balancing

The equation factory of Topic VII: every cell reaction, every electrode product and every titration ratio is two half-equations in a trenchcoat.

Pick a technique

VII (b)(c)

Ionic half equation — the unit of electrochemistry

$$\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)} + \text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)} \qquad 2\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{I}_{2}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • A half equation shows one half of a redox change with electrons written explicitly — reduction puts $\text{e}^{-}$ on the left, oxidation on the right.
  • Syllabus demand: “write a half equation representing the reaction at each half cell of a simple chemical cell”.
  • Every particle keeps its charge and its state symbol: “1 mark for correct half-equation with state symbols” (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Charges must be shown on all ions in half-equations” (ms summary App. A) — a missing charge turns a correct equation into no mark.
  • Electrons are written as $\text{e}^{-}$ (never $\text{e}^{-1}$ or “electrons” in prose inside an equation).
VII (c)

Four balancing stations (acidic medium)

$$\text{atoms} \rightarrow \text{O with } \text{H}_{2}\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H with } \text{H}^{+} \rightarrow \text{charge with } \text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Station 1: balance the atom changing oxidation number (and any other element except H, O).
  • Station 2: each missing O ⇒ one $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ on the deficient side.
  • Station 3: each missing H ⇒ one $\text{H}^{+}$ on the deficient side (acidic medium).
  • Station 4: balance total charge with $\text{e}^{-}$ — the number of electrons equals the oxidation-number change; check both sides’ charges explicitly.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Finish with a 10-second audit: atoms each side, then charge each side. A half equation that fails either is not a half equation.
  • Never “balance H by adding $\text{H}_{2}$” — hydrogen is balanced as $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$ (or $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ in alkaline work).
VII (c)

Reduction worked: permanganate(VII) → manganese(II)

$$\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}\text{(aq)} + 8\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 5\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 4\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mn +VII → +II ⇒ 5 electrons on the left; four oxide ions need 8 $\text{H}^{+}$ to become 4 $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$.
  • Charge audit: left (−1 +8 −5) = +2; right +2 ✓.
  • This is one of the five most-tested half equations and the engine of the “purple → colourless” test (ms summary §7.2; F.3).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Write $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)}$, not “acid”, inside the equation — but keep “acidified potassium permanganate” when naming the reagent (CP 2014 Q14).
VII (c)(d)

Reduction worked: dichromate(VI) → chromium(III)

$$\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}\text{(aq)} + 14\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 6\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}\text{(aq)} + 7\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Two Cr atoms each +VI → +III ⇒ 6 electrons — halving or “+6 each” are the two arithmetic slips examiners logged.
  • Orange → green colour change is the standard positive test for a reducing agent (with the reverse pair for permanganate) (ms summary F.3).
  • The 6:1 stoichiometry against $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ recurs: “the mole ratio of ‘Fe²⁺(aq) to Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq)’ in the overall equation is ‘6:1’” (CP 2015 Q16).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • When combining with the iron half equation $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{e}^{-}$, multiply the iron half by 6 — the dichromate keeps its 6 electrons; the overall never shows them.
VII (c)

Oxidation worked: sulfite → sulfate

$$\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{SO}_{4}^{2-}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Oxidation halves often need $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ on the left (extra oxygen demand) and produce $\text{H}^{+}$ — a tell-tale sign the species is being oxidised.
  • S +IV → +VI ⇒ 2 electrons; sulfite is therefore a 2-electron reducing agent, sulfate a non-agent (S already +VI) (CP 2013 Q22 logic).
  • The $\text{H}^{+}$ produced explains why oxidising sulfite in water acidifies the solution.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Keep the ion charge on both S species ($\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}$, $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$) — dropping charges was the general warning: “charges must be shown on all ions in half-equations” (ms summary App. A).
VII (b)(c)

One-step halves — the automatics

$$\text{Ag}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Ag(s)} \qquad \text{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Metal ↔ metal-ion halves need only charge balancing: $\text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cu(s)}$ (deposition), $\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)} + \text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$.
  • Halide oxidation: $2\text{Br}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Br}_{2}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$ — CP recorded the observation at such an electrode as “a reddish brown colour gas will be given out” (CP 2016 Q8).
  • Write these without thinking — they are the bricks for cell and electrolysis equations everywhere else in this studio.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Copper will not lose electrons to give Cu²⁺ at the negative electrode / Copper cannot undergo reduction at the negative electrode” (ms bank) — direction is dictated by the electrode’s role, not by wishful thinking.
VII (c)

Combining halves — the overall ionic equation

$$5 \times (\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{e}^{-}) \; + \; 1 \times (\text{MnO}_{4}^{-} + 8\text{H}^{+} + 5\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_{2}\text{O})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Scale each half so electrons lost = electrons gained, add, and cancel the electrons: $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-} + 5\text{Fe}^{2+} + 8\text{H}^{+} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{Fe}^{3+} + 4\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$.
  • Re-audit atoms and charge in the final equation (here: left +7 = right +7 ✓).
  • Electrons never appear in the overall equation, and free electrons never swim in solution — they cross at the electrode or on collision.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The mole ratios you cancel with are the ratios questions later compute from — CP 2015 Q16 was passed or failed on the 6:1 ratio hiding in this arithmetic.
VII (c)

ON-change balancing — the second syllabus method

$$\text{Cl}_{2}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{I}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^{-}\text{(aq)} + \text{I}_{2}\text{(aq)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Syllabus option: “balance redox equations by using half equations or changes in oxidation numbers” — pick either; both are examined.
  • Steps: assign ON → compute change per atom → multiply by atoms transferred → cross-reduce the two totals into coefficients → finish by inspection.
  • For $\text{Cl}_{2}$ + iodide: Cl 0 → −I gives −2 per $\text{Cl}_{2}$ molecule; I −I → 0 gives +2 per $\text{I}_{2}$ — equal totals give 1 : 2 : 2 : 1.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Count electrons per molecule: permanganate takes 5, dichromate takes 6 per formula unit — forgetting “× atoms” is the method mark killer.
  • The ON method needs the atoms-balanced skeleton first; if the stem gives the full equation, balance around the redox pair by inspection.
VII (d)

Alkaline halves — the fuel cell medium

$$\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \qquad \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 4\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Both equations are verbatim MS bank lines (electrode D and electrode E of a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell question).
  • In alkaline medium, balance H with $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ and produce/consume $\text{OH}^{-}$ — never write $\text{H}^{+}$ as a product in alkali.
  • Combine (×2 the hydrogen half) to cancel 4 electrons: $2\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$ — the overall the exam also accepts alone.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • State symbols carry marks here: $\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)}$, $\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$, $\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$ (ms bank mark rule). Hydrogen is fed to the negative electrode (it is the fuel, oxidised); oxygen to the positive.
VII (b)–(d)

Which equation is asked? Half · ionic · complete

$$\text{half: } \text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^{-} \qquad \text{overall ionic: } \text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Read the verb-noun pair: “half equation” wants electrons; “overall equation / ionic equation” wants them cancelled; “chemical equation” accepts the complete form.
  • “Some candidates gave a complete equation instead of an ionic equation” (CP 2012 Q7); the flip side — half written when the ionic equation was demanded — was logged in 2014 (CP 2014 Q9).
  • Spectators (K⁺, Na⁺, $\text{NO}_{3}^{-}$, $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ when not reacting) stay out of ionic equations.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Underline the required form on the paper before writing. “Wrongly suggested Zn as the oxidising agent and wrongly wrote Zn(s) → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻” — the equation was right; the label was wrong (CP 2016 Q8).

The Half-Equation Workshop — four stations per equation

Feed any skeleton through the stations in order; the workshop never jams because each station removes exactly one kind of imbalance.

Station A · Redox atoms

Balance the element whose oxidation number changes (and any other non-H/O element). Cr₂O₇²⁻ ⇒ 2 Cr³⁺ before anything else.

Station B · Oxygen — add H₂O

Seven O on the left ⇒ seven $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ on the right. Oxygen is always carried by water — never by “O” or “$\text{O}_{2}$” terms.

Station C · Hydrogen — add H⁺ (acid) or OH⁻ (alkali)

Acidic: 14 $\text{H}^{+}$ balance the dichromate’s water. Alkaline medium (fuel cell): reach for $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ / $\text{OH}^{-}$ pairs instead.

Station D · Charge — add e⁻

Sum charges each side; drop $\text{e}^{-}$ on the more positive side until equal. That electron count must equal the ON change — if not, an earlier station slipped.

Multi-blank Quiz — Half-Equations & Balancing

1. In a reduction half-equation the electrons are written on the left side; in an oxidation half-equation, on the right side; in the overall ionic equation electrons are cancelled.
2. The acidic balancing order is: redox atoms → balance oxygen with H₂O → balance hydrogen with H⁺ → balance charge with electrons.
3. $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}\text{(aq)}$ + 8 H⁺(aq) + 5 e⁻ → Mn²⁺(aq) + 4 H₂O(l); the colour change is purple → colourless.
4. $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$ needs 6 electrons per formula unit, and reacts with Fe²⁺ in the mole ratio 1 : 6 — the arithmetic CP flagged (CP 2015 Q16). The product ion Cr³⁺ is green in solution.
5. Sulfite’s oxidation half is $\text{SO}_{3}^{2-}$ + H₂O → $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ — water sits on the left in an oxidation.
6. The hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell (alkaline) negative electrode: $\text{H}_{2}$ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻ (ms bank); positive electrode: $\text{O}_{2}$ + 2 H₂O + 4 e⁻ → 4 OH⁻; overall: $2\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} \rightarrow$ 2H₂O(l).
7. Candidates “gave a complete equation instead of an ionic equation” (CP 2012 Q7), and others wrote only the permanganate half equation when the full ionic equation was asked (CP 2014 Q9).
8. The mark sits on a “correct half-equation with state symbols” (ms bank); “charges must be shown on all ions” (ms summary); spectator ions like K⁺ and NO₃⁻ must stay out.
9. Balancing by oxidation number changes means the increase and the decrease must be multiplied to equal totals; for $\text{Cl}_{2} + \text{I}^{-}$ that gives coefficients 1 : 2.
10. Never change subscripts to balance — only coefficients; the syllabus allows either the half-equation method or the oxidation-number method for redox equations.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Balancing & Half-Equations

Half equation vs ionic equation

A half equation has electrons and only one side of the redox; the ionic equation is the complete reaction with spectators removed. Candidates “just wrote down the half equation for the reduction of permanganate ion, but did not give the ionic equation for the reaction” (CP 2014 Q9).

e⁻ on the wrong side

Gain ⇒ electrons reactant-side (reduction, $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cu}$); loss ⇒ product-side (oxidation, $\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^{-}$). Flip the side and the charge audit fails instantly — trust the audit.

Coefficients vs subscripts

Balancing edits coefficients only. Turning $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ into “H₂O₂” or MnO₄⁻ into MnO₂ to even the oxygen count changes the substance and voids the equation.

H⁺ in alkali

The acidic recipe (add $\text{H}^{+}$) must not leak into the alkaline fuel-cell halves — there hydrogen rides on $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$/$\text{OH}^{-}$: $\text{H}_{2} + 2\text{OH}^{-} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O} + 2\text{e}^{-}$ (ms bank).

Electrons “in the equation” vs electrons “in the solution”

Electrons belong in half equations as book-keeping and in wires as real travellers — never in the solution, and never in the overall equation. A combined equation still showing $\text{e}^{-}$ means the cancel step was skipped.

Spectators that sneak in

$\text{K}^{+}$, $\text{Na}^{+}$, $\text{NO}_{3}^{-}$, $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ (unreacted) must stay out; CP recorded candidates “erroneously included I⁻(aq)” in a half equation where iodide was not the reacting couple (CP 2016 Q8).

Engine C · Strands (a)(b)(d)

Chemical Cells & Fuel Cells Inspector

From a lemon-and-coins battery to the fuel cell in a breathalyser: one redox reaction, split so its electrons must take the scenic route through your circuit.

Pick a cell topic

VII (b)

What a chemical cell is

$$\text{chemical energy} \longrightarrow \text{electrical energy} \quad (\text{spontaneous redox})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition: “In a chemical cell, chemical energy is converted to electrical energy” (syllabus overview) — a spontaneous redox reaction whose electron transfer is forced through an external circuit.
  • Ingredients: two electrodes of different materials, an electrolyte, an external conductor; “the flow of electrons in an external circuit indicates the occurrence of reduction and oxidation (redox) at the electrodes”.
  • Keep the direction straight: a chemical cell generates electricity; electrolysis consumes it.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Electrolysis generates electricity” is the reversed-conversion blunder this studio lists under NOT ACCEPT — cells and electrolytic cells are opposite machines.
  • A cell does not “produce electrons”: electrons are released at the negative electrode by oxidation and carried away through the wire.
VII (b)

Two metal electrodes + one electrolyte

$$\text{Zn: } \text{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \qquad \text{Cu: } 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{H}_{2}\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Zinc and copper strips in dilute $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$: the more reactive metal (zinc) is the negative electrode — oxidised and dissolving; hydrogen bubbles form at the copper (positive) electrode.
  • Describe and demonstrate how to build such a cell and measure its voltage with a voltmeter (in parallel across the electrodes; series = 2 cells add up).
  • Half equations at each electrode + overall: here $ \text{Zn(s)} + 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{(g)}$ — the syllabus asks you to write both halves and the whole.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Oxidation happens at the anode in every electrochemical device; in a cell the anode is the negative terminal. “Anode = +” is the electrolysis convention, not the cell one — this studio drills both in Engine D.
  • Electrode mass decreases at the dissolving (negative) electrode; state changes for both electrodes plus the solution when asked for observations.
VII (b)

Problems of the simple single-electrolyte cell

$$\text{Zn directly meets } \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{/(H}^{+}\text{)} \Rightarrow \text{electron transfer where no wire is} $$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Syllabus demand: “explain the problems associated with a simple chemical cell consisting of two metal electrodes and an electrolyte”.
  • Problem 1: the electrolyte reacts directly with the more reactive electrode (copper(II) ions plate onto the zinc / acid fizzes on it), so part of the redox bypasses the wire — the cell polarises / runs down quickly and its voltage falls.
  • Problem 2: the cell keeps reacting when the switch is off — no way to store it “flat-free”; energy leaves as heat instead of current.
  • The fix is the design of card 4: separate the half reactions into two half cells joined by a salt bridge.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Name the mechanism (“the more reactive metal reacts directly with the electrolyte”), not a vague “the chemicals run out”. “The voltage is low” without a cause earns no explanation mark.
VII (b)

Metal–metal ion half cells & the salt bridge

$$\text{Zn|Zn}^{2+} \parallel \text{salt bridge} \parallel \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{|Cu} \qquad \text{e}^{-}: \text{wire only};\quad \text{ions}: \text{bridge + solutions}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Build a cell of two metal–metal ion half cells connected by a salt bridge or porous device; each beaker holds its metal in its own ion solution (e.g. Zn in $\text{ZnSO}_{4}$(aq), Cu in $\text{CuSO}_{4}$(aq)).
  • Functions of the salt bridge (say all): it completes the circuit by allowing ions to migrate, and it keeps the two solutions electrically neutral while preventing them mixing directly.
  • Overall equation and half equations are unchanged in form: $\text{Zn(s)} + \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}$, with Cu²⁺ reduced at the copper electrode and Zn oxidised at the zinc.
  • Remove the bridge → the circuit breaks → current stops immediately; that is the classic diagnostic question.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The famous CP slip: describing the lemon as a salt bridge “which allows electrons to pass through” — wrong particle; electrons use the wire (CP 2012 Q3). This studio prints that exact phrasing under NOT ACCEPT.
VII (b)

Predicting flow & electrode changes

$$\text{e}^{-}\text{ flow: anode (–) } \longrightarrow \text{ wire } \longrightarrow \text{ cathode (+)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Predict the electron flow in the external circuit and the chemical changes in the simple chemical cells” — decide the anode first (the stronger reducing agent / higher metal in the ECS), everything else follows.
  • Checklist per electrode: oxidation ⇒ mass falls (dissolving metal); reduction ⇒ mass rises (metal deposit) or gas evolves; anode dissolving makes its solution’s metal-ion concentration rise, cathode plating makes the other fall (unless the stem says otherwise).
  • Ionic current inside: cations drift towards the cathode (positive), anions towards the anode (negative) — opposite signs, unlike electrolysis.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Electrons flow from Cu to Zn through the solution” fails twice (wrong direction, wrong medium). Write: “from the zinc electrode (negative) to the copper electrode (positive) through the external circuit”.
  • When a cell is recharged, direction reverses — see card 9’s CP rule (CP 2016 Q15).
VII (b)

Why different pairs give different voltages

$$\text{bigger ECS separation} \Rightarrow \text{bigger voltage};\quad \text{size/mass: no effect on voltage}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Syllabus: “explain the differences in voltages produced in chemical cells when different metal couples are used as electrodes” — answer with the electrochemical series distance between the two metals.
  • Voltage depends on the nature of the electrode pair (and electrolyte identity); larger electrode area, more electrolyte and stronger current duration come from capacity, not voltage.
  • Series and parallel of cells: series adds voltages; parallel adds available charge (capacity/time) — a common MC pair.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Candidates tried to rank alkali strengths by “comparing the voltages generated by the chemical cells” and misread meters (“using a voltmeter to measure the current”) (CP 2015 Q5) — know what each instrument measures before any cell-based comparison question.
VII (d)

Chemical cells with inert electrodes

$$\text{Pt | Fe}^{2+},\text{Fe}^{3+} \parallel \text{Pt | MnO}_{4}^{-},\text{Mn}^{2+},\text{H}^{+} \qquad 5\text{Fe}^{2+}:\text{1 MnO}_{4}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Describe and construct chemical cells with inert electrodes… predict the chemical changes at each half cell… write a half equation for each half cell and the overall ionic equation” — when neither couple contains a metal, platinum or graphite supplies the electron ferry.
  • The inert electrode does not react; the redox happens between dissolved ions: $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ gives electrons (oxidised, negative electrode) and $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ takes them (reduced, positive electrode).
  • 2015’s platinum-electrode MC ran the same logic with dichromate: “the mole ratio of ‘Fe²⁺(aq) to Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq)’ in the overall equation is ‘6:1’” (CP 2015 Q16).
  • Iodine/iodide, $\text{H}_{2}$ on Pt and $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$ all live in this family — the electrode is scenery, the ions are the actors.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not write Pt into half equations — no $\text{Pt}^{2+}$ product ever appears; the bank’s inert-cell equations contain only the solution species.
VII (d)

The hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

$$2\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Understand the principles of hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell” + write both electrode equations: negative electrode (oxidation of the fuel): $\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$; positive electrode (reduction of oxygen): $\text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 4\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$ — both verbatim from the MS bank.
  • Gases diffuse into inert electrodes through the alkaline electrolyte; $\text{OH}^{-}$ is recycled: consumed at one electrode, regenerated at the other.
  • STSE (syllabus): breath-alysers (intoximeter) use fuel-cell technology to detect alcohol; fuel cells power space missions and some vehicles — “not widely for commercial or domestic purposes”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Hydrogen side is the negative electrode; writing $\text{O}_{2}$’s equation there flips two marks at once. Include states — the bank marks half equations “with state symbols”.
VII (d)

Fuel cells — pros & cons (say both)

$$\text{pros: continuous, clean} \qquad \text{cons: H}_{2}\text{ supply, storage, cost}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “State the pros and cons of a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell” — pros: power lasts as long as gas is supplied (no “running flat”), high efficiency, quiet, and “only water is emitted … do not emit pollutant gases such as CO₂, CO and NOₓ” (CP 2013).
  • Cons: hydrogen is flammable/light to store awkwardly; making $\text{H}_{2}$ costs money and energy — “if hydrogen is produced from electrolysis of water, the process needs electricity. However, the generation of electricity can also pose harm to our environment” (CP 2013).
  • Exam register: judge the whole cycle — a clean tailpipe is not a clean fuel-chain unless the hydrogen itself is green.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Statements of fact alone died here: “answers merely stating the fact would receive no marks because the question required comments from environmental aspects” (CP 2013) — always append the environmental judgement.
VII (a)(d)

Primary vs secondary — what recharging does

$$\text{discharge: } \text{chemical} \rightarrow \text{electrical} \qquad \text{recharge: electrical} \rightarrow \text{chemical, reaction reversed}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Primary cell: cannot be recharged (zinc-carbon, alkaline manganese, silver oxide); secondary cell / accumulator: rechargeable (lithium ion, NiMH, lead-acid).
  • Recharging rule from the CP verbatim: “reduction would occur at the negative electrode when the cell is being recharged. Moreover, the reaction during recharge is in the opposite direction of the one during discharge” (CP 2016 Q15).
  • Worked shape (Ni–Cd, 2016’s stem): from the discharge equation $2\text{NiO(OH)(s)} + \text{Cd(s)} + 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{Ni(OH)}_{2}\text{(s)} + \text{Cd(OH)}_{2}\text{(s)}$, the negative-electrode recharge half is $\text{Cd(OH)}_{2}\text{(s)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cd(s)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$ — reverse the discharge half at that electrode.
  • Fuel cells are neither: they are continuously fed, not recharged.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • During recharge the cell is being electrolysed — so the external supply’s positive forces oxidation at the electrode that was the cell’s positive during discharge. Track “negative electrode”, not “anode”, when the CP wordings talk terminals.
VII (a)

Commercial cells — characteristics ONLY (depth-capped)

$$\text{voltage} \cdot \text{capacity} \cdot \text{rechargeability} \cdot \text{size} \cdot \text{price} \Rightarrow \text{justify the use}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Describe the characteristics of the six named cells and justify uses without describing structures or working principles — that is the official cap (NTF-2018 II-B VII (a)).
  • Zinc-carbon ≈1.5 V primary, cheap, low capacity (clocks, remotes); alkaline manganese ≈1.5 V primary, higher capacity & longer shelf life (high-drain devices); silver oxide ≈1.5 V primary, tiny button cell, steady voltage (watches, calculators).
  • Lithium ion ≈3.6–3.7 V rechargeable, light and high capacity (phones, laptops); NiMH ≈1.2 V rechargeable, robust high capacity (hybrid cars, AA replacements); lead-acid ≈2 V per cell (12 V pack) rechargeable, delivers very large current, heavy (car battery).
  • Environmental side: “understand the environmental impact of using dry cells” — disposable cells carry heavy metals to landfill; rechargeables and recycling cut that burden.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Use-justification answers pair a characteristic with a requirement: “lithium ion cells are light and rechargeable, so suitable for mobile phones”. Naming the electrolyte or paste composition is off-syllabus and can smuggle in wrong facts — don’t.

Complicated cells — the “given information” protocol

$$\text{given: } 2\text{MnO}_{2}\text{(s)} + 2\text{NH}_{4}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Mn}_{2}\text{O}_{5}\text{(s)} + 2\text{NH}_{3}\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Official ruling (NTF-2018 II-B VII (d)): “With sufficient information given, students should be able to apply the concepts of electrochemistry to solve problems involving more complicated chemical cells.”

Your tasks: read which electrode consumes $\text{e}^{-}$ (reduction ⇒ positive electrode), combine with the supplied oxidation half ($\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^{-}$) and cancel electrons.

Cell Anatomy — answer four questions, never five

Any chemical cell — lemon, accumulator or fuel cell — falls to the same interrogation in the same order.

Q1 · Who is oxidised?

The stronger reducing agent — metal higher in the given series, or the species whose ON rises. That electrode is the anode = negative terminal, and it releases electrons into the wire.

Q2 · Where do the electrons go?

Through the external circuit only, anode → cathode. Inside the cell they never travel; anything claiming otherwise is a NOT ACCEPT line.

Q3 · Who carries the current through the liquid?

Ions: cations to the positive cathode, anions to the negative anode; the salt bridge / porous device shuttles ions and keeps both beakers neutral. Remove it → circuit open → zero current.

Q4 · How much voltage, and why?

Fixed by the identity of the electrode pair (ECS separation) — explain voltage differences from the series. Bigger plates add capacity (longer current), not voltage. Series connection sums the cell voltages.

Multi-blank Quiz — Chemical Cells & Fuel Cells

1. In a chemical cell, chemical energy is converted to electrical energy by a spontaneous redox reaction, with electron flow in the external circuit.
2. In a Zn–Cu cell in dilute sulphuric acid, the zinc electrode is negative because it is oxidised; hydrogen gas bubbles form at the copper electrode where $\text{H}^{+}$ is reduced.
3. The salt bridge completes the circuit by letting ions migrate and keeps the solutions electrically neutral; electrons pass only through the wire.
4. The simple one-beaker cell “runs down” because the electrolyte reacts directly with the more reactive electrode, so electrons are transferred not through the wire, and energy escapes as heat.
5. Two cells with different metal pairs give different voltages because the separation in the electrochemical series differs; using larger electrodes changes capacity (not voltage) (pillar-standard cell principle — no 2015 CP passage states it).
6. In a cell with inert platinum electrodes, $\text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$ is oxidised to $\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{(aq)}$ at the negative electrode while $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ is reduced at the positive one; the electrode itself does not react (CP 2015 Q16 family).
7. Alkaline fuel-cell negatives: $\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2$ OH⁻(aq) → $2$ H₂O(l) $+ 2\text{e}^{-}$; positives: $\text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} +$ 4e⁻ → $4\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)}$ — both verbatim (ms bank).
8. A fuel cell’s emission is only water, with no pollutant gases such as CO₂, CO and NOₓ (CP 2013), but hydrogen made “from electrolysis of water” needs electricity whose generation “can also pose harm to our environment” — that comment is where the marks are (CP 2013).
9. When a secondary cell is being recharged the reaction runs in the opposite direction and reduction occurs at the negative electrode (CP 2016 Q15).
10. Matching voltages to cells: zinc-carbon ≈1.5 V, primary; lithium ion ≈3.6–3.7 V, rechargeable; lead-acid ≈2 V per cell, secondary — and describing their internals is not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VII (a)).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Chemical Cells

Anode sign: cell vs electrolytic cell

Oxidation is at the anode always — but the cell’s anode is negative (it pushes electrons out) while the electrolytic cell’s anode is positive (the supply sucks them out). Memorise the definition, derive the sign.

“Electrons flow through the salt bridge”

The CP’s exact wrong answer for the lemon cell (CP 2012 Q3). Bridge = ion highway; wire = electron highway; the two never change lanes.

Recharge: same vs opposite direction

Recharge is the reverse of discharge at every electrode — at the negative electrode that means reduction ($\text{Cd(OH)}_{2} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cd} + 2\text{OH}^{-}$) (CP 2016 Q15). Copying the discharge equation unchanged is the trap.

Fuel cell vs battery

A battery stores its reactants and dies; a fuel cell is fed continuously and only “dies” with the gas supply — it is not “recharged”, and its “product” must be quoted as water plus the no-pollutants clause (CP 2013).

Voltage vs capacity

Bigger electrodes / more electrolyte = more stored charge (capacity: how long it lasts), never a larger voltage; voltage belongs to the electrode pair. Zinc-carbon vs alkaline manganese: same nominal 1.5 V, different capacity — that is why cameras take alkaline.

Inert electrode participates vs conducts

Pt/graphite merely conduct and host electron exchange; equations for inert-electrode cells contain no electrode atoms. Candidates who “included I⁻(aq)” wrongly learned this the hard way (CP 2016 Q8).

Describing internals vs applying given data

Exam questions hand complicated cells with their own equations (NTF-2018 II-B VII (d)); answering “the zinc-carbon cell contains ammonium chloride paste because…” is off-cap and often wrong. Read, apply, move on.

VII (e)

Electrolysis & the cell kit

$$\text{electrical energy} \longrightarrow \text{chemical energy} \qquad (\text{forced, non-spontaneous decomposition})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Electrolysis: decomposition of an electrolyte by direct electricity — “electrolysis as the decomposition of substances by electricity” (syllabus wording).
  • An electrolyte is accepted by any of: a compound conducting when molten or dissolved in water; a substance with mobile ions in those states; a substance decomposed by electricity (ms summary §7.1).
  • “Describe the materials needed to construct an electrolytic cell”: container, two electrodes (Pt/graphite or an active metal), the electrolyte (molten or aqueous), a direct-current supply and connecting wires.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Solid ionic solids do not conduct: “ions must be free to move” (ms summary App. A). And say direct current — a.c. swaps the electrodes’ roles every half cycle.
  • NOT ACCEPT: “conducts by electrons passing through the solution” — carriers in the electrolyte are ions (ms summary §7.1).
VII (e)

Naming electrodes & the two rivers

$$\text{anode} = \text{+ terminal} = \text{oxidation} \qquad \text{cathode} = \text{− terminal} = \text{reduction}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • In an electrolytic cell the anode joins the supply’s positive terminal; the cathode the negative — opposite signs to a chemical cell, same oxidation/reduction definitions.
  • “Anode and cathodic reactions” are examinable by name — every product question is decided by which river the species swims in: cations → cathode, anions → anode.
  • Electrons: pumped out of the anode into the supply and pushed back at the cathode; through the liquid, charge rides on ions migrating — never on electrons.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Oxidation occurs at the cathode” fails the definition regardless of cell type — anode is defined as where oxidation occurs. CP logged exactly this confusion: candidates “were confused about the anodic and cathodic changes … gave the wrong half equations” (CP 2014 Q8).
VII (e)

Migration of ions — the coloured-bands demo

$$\text{Cu}^{2+}\text{: blue band} \rightarrow \text{cathode} \qquad \text{MnO}_{4}^{-}\text{: purple band} \rightarrow \text{anode}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Syllabus activity: “investigating the migration of ions of aqueous solutions, e.g. copper(II) dichromate and potassium permanganate, towards oppositely charged electrodes”.
  • Method: a drop of the coloured solution on wet filter paper (or a gel) between two electrode tips on a microscope slide, d.c. on — blue $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ drifts to the cathode, orange $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{7}^{2-}$ / purple $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ drift to the anode.
  • The 2016 exam version used $\text{FeSO}_{4}$ + $\text{K}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$: a pale green band moves to the cathode while “the $\text{K}^{+}$(aq) and $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$(aq) that migrate to the middle of the filter paper do not show any colour” (CP 2016 Q2).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Colour facts are marks: $\text{Fe}^{2+}\text{(aq)}$ is pale green — “dark green, dirty green, or bluish green” were rejected (CP 2016 Q2). After reversing the battery, the colourless ions reaching mid-paper stay invisible (CP 2016 Q2).
VII (e)

The ion inventory — four species before any prediction

$$\text{NaCl(aq)} \Rightarrow \text{Na}^{+},\ \text{H}^{+}\ /\ \text{Cl}^{-},\ \text{OH}^{-} \quad (\text{H}^{+},\ \text{OH}^{-}\ \text{from water})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Aqueous electrolysis always offers two competing cations (metal ion vs $\text{H}^{+}$) and two competing anions (the salt’s anion vs $\text{OH}^{-}$) — list all four, then compare.
  • Water’s own ions are easy to forget and fatal to omit: “a few of them omitted $\text{H}^{+}$ and $\text{OH}^{-}$ in their answers” (CP 2015 Q7).
  • Molten salts have no water: only the salt’s ions exist — which is why molten electrolyses give the reactive metals.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Examiner-accepted sentence patterns name the ion and the electrode together: “$\text{H}^{+}$(aq) ions are discharged at the cathode”; “$\text{Cl}^{-}$(aq) ions are discharged at the anode to give $\text{Cl}_{2}$(g)” (ms bank).
VII (e)

Factor 1 · position in the electrochemical series

$$\text{“cation lower in ECS discharged preferentially”} \Rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+} \succ \text{H}^{+} \succ \text{Zn}^{2+} \dots \text{K}^{+}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Cathode choice: compare the metal ion with $\text{H}^{+}$ — metals below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Hg) come out as metal; metals above hydrogen (K … Al, plus Zn, Fe, Pb) keep their ions in solution and hydrogen gas forms instead (ms summary §7.3 logic).
  • Anode choice among anions: $\text{I}^{-} \succ \text{Br}^{-} \succ \text{Cl}^{-} \succ \text{OH}^{-} \succ$ oxo-anions ($\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$, $\text{NO}_{3}^{-}$ never discharged in aqueous solution — water’s $\text{OH}^{-}$ gives $\text{O}_{2}$).
  • Reasoning wording that earns the mark: “hydrogen ions in the solution are easier to be reduced than magnesium ions” (CP 2014 Q8) — or “…is a stronger oxidising agent”, never “better”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Wrongly stated that magnesium ion would be reduced at electrode X” — that is the year’s headline miss (CP 2014 Q8); and the plating item demanded “$\text{Cu}^{2+}$ lower than $\text{H}^{+}$ in ECS / stronger oxidising agent → $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ discharged” (ms summary §7.3; CP 2015 Q7).
VII (e)

Factor 2 · concentration of ions

$$\text{concentrated } \text{Cl}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_{2}; \qquad \text{dilute } \text{Cl}^{-} \rightarrow \text{OH}^{-} \rightarrow \text{O}_{2}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • A high concentration can outrank the series: concentrated chloride gives chlorine, dilute chloride gives oxygen — the syllabus’s own “preferential discharge of ions in relation to … concentration of ions”.
  • The iodide precedent from the bank: “I⁻ ions are preferentially discharged to give I₂ which dissolves in KI(aq) to give brown I₃⁻ ions” — explain preference “based on concentration effect” (ms bank; CP 2013).
  • Concentration drifts mid-experiment: brine becomes dilute near the anode as Cl⁻ drains away — extension questions probe exactly this.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Say concentrated sodium chloride solution / brine — “electrolysis of sea water or [plain] sodium chloride solution” lost marks in the chlor-alkali item (CP 2013).
VII (e)

Factor 3 · nature of the electrodes

$$\text{copper anode: } \text{Cu(s)} \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \quad \text{instead of } \text{O}_{2}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • An active electrode dissolves in preference to aqueous anion discharge: with a copper anode, copper atoms are oxidised and no oxygen appears — the working principle of plating and purification.
  • A mercury cathode outranks the hydrogen rule for sodium: “Sodium amalgam produced at cathode reacts with water forming NaOH(aq)” — the flowing-mercury-cell route (ms bank).
  • Inert electrodes (Pt, graphite) never enter the equation — their only job is conduction.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Check the electrode material before quoting products; “state the observation at electrode X” answers flip entirely between graphite and copper anodes (CP 2016 Q8 context).
VII (e)

Molten electrolytes — no competition

$$\text{Na}^{+}\text{(l)} + \text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Na(l)} \qquad 2\text{Cl}^{-}\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Melted ionic compounds contain only their own ions — the cation must discharge at the cathode (even $\text{K}^{+}$, $\text{Na}^{+}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$): this is why reactive metals come from molten-salt electrolysis (Topic III/XIII link).
  • The bank writes molten states with (l): $\text{Sr}^{2+}\text{(l)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Sr(l)}$ with $2\text{Cl}^{-}\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$ (ms bank; ms summary App. C.2).
  • No preferential-discharge argument is needed — say so if the question asks you to “explain the difference”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Aqueous or molten?” is the first word to read in every electrolysis stem — the products differ completely for NaCl. State symbols (l) vs (aq) carry the equation marks.
VII (e)

Named case i · dilute sulphuric acid

$$\text{cathode: } 2\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} \qquad \text{anode: } 4\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)} + 4\text{e}^{-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Electrolysis of dilute $\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$ with inert electrodes is electrolysis of water ($\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ and $\text{H}^{+}$ survive): $2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + \text{O}_{2}\text{(g)}$.
  • Observations: bubbles at both electrodes; volume ratio H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1; acid left behind becomes more concentrated (pH falls), since water is removed.
  • Gas tests: hydrogen — lit splint, pops; oxygen — glowing splint relights.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Give ratios as volumes of gases collected, and name the test reagent (“lighted splint”, “glowing splint”) — vague “burns”/“reignites” without the splint loses the test mark.
VII (e)

Named case ii · NaCl(aq) at two concentrations

$$\text{brine: } 2\text{NaCl(aq)} + 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + \text{Cl}_{2}\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Dilute NaCl(aq): cathode $\text{H}^{+}$ discharged ⇒ $\text{H}_{2}$; anode $\text{OH}^{-}$ discharged ⇒ $\text{O}_{2}$ — “The amount of OH⁻ ions increases at electrode B as H⁺ is being consumed. So universal indicator turns blue under alkaline conditions.” (ms bank)
  • Concentrated (brine): cathode still $\text{H}_{2}$; anode now $\text{Cl}_{2}$ (pale green, bleaches litmus — concentration beats the series) and NaOH accumulates — the chlor-alkali industry.
  • Cell types to recognise from given data: flowing mercury / diaphragm / membrane cells (ms bank wording); mercury’s drawback “Mercury is poisonous”; membrane cell’s advantage “no toxic mercury; less energy required” (ms summary §7.6; CP 2015).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The 2013 report’s error list is the answer scheme: name the electrolyte as brine / concentrated sodium chloride solution, mention the type of electrolytic cell, and say which electrode gives Cl₂ and which gives H₂ (CP 2013).
⚠️ Safety line

Chlorine is toxic (testing: damp blue litmus bleaches — in a fume hood); NaOH solution is corrosive; mercury cells are avoided for environmental reasons, not convenience.

VII (e)

Named case iii · CuSO₄(aq) & copper purification

$$\text{inert: } \text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{Cu} \;/\; 4\text{OH}^{-} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O} + \text{O}_{2} + 4\text{e}^{-} \qquad \text{Cu anode dissolves}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Inert electrodes: cathode — reddish-brown copper deposits ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$ below $\text{H}^{+}$ ⇒ “$\text{Cu}^{2+}$ discharged preferentially”); anode — oxygen; the blue fades and the solution acidifies ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$ and $\text{OH}^{-}$ both drain away, $\text{H}^{+}$ and $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ remain).
  • Purification of copper: impure copper = anode, pure copper = cathode, $\text{CuSO}_{4}$(aq) electrolyte: anode “Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (Cu anode dissolves)”, cathode deposits “brown solid; electrode mass increases”, “solution colour: no observable change (Cu²⁺ concentration maintained)”, precious-metal anode sludge (ms summary §7.4).
  • Observation discipline: report electrode and solution changes — candidates “wrongly stated the expected changes at the electrodes … without referring to the observable changes that would occur in the solution” (CP 2015 Q7).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • With copper electrodes the blue colour does not fade (dissolution replaces deposition); with inert electrodes it does. Which electrode material the stem used is the whole answer.
VII (e)

Electroplating & the electron arithmetic

$$Q = I \times t \qquad n(\text{e}^{-}) = Q / F \qquad m = \frac{n(\text{e}^{-})}{z} \times M$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Electroplating setup: object = cathode, plating metal = anode, electrolyte = solution of a salt of the plating metal; clean the object first — “oily dirt hinders conduction of electricity / hinders plating of copper” (ms summary §7.4; CP 2015 Q7).
  • During plating with metal electrodes: anode loses mass, cathode gains, electrolyte concentration (and colour) stay constant.
  • The stoichiometry examiners actually mark: “it takes 2 moles of electrons to reduce 1 mole of Cu²⁺” (CP 2015 Q7); $m = n(\text{e}^{-}) \times M / z$ with $F = 96\,500\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$ given in the question — “the unit must be correct” (ms bank).
  • Environment: the electroplating industry’s effluent (heavy-metal ions, acids) must be treated — “assess the environmental impact” is syllabus wording; “Mercury is poisonous” (ms bank).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Report mass change of the object as an increase and the anode’s as a decrease in the same breath; give final answers to the significant figures the data justify (see Bench 2).

The Preferential-Discharge Decision Tree

Run the electrolyte through these four gates and the products, half equations and observations write themselves.

Gate 1 · Molten or aqueous?

Molten: only salt ions exist — cation → metal, anion → non-metal; no contest. Aqueous: add $\text{H}^{+}$ and $\text{OH}^{-}$ from water to the shortlist and go to Gate 2–3.

Gate 2 · Cathode contest (reduction)

Lower in ECS wins. Metal ion below hydrogen (Cu²⁺, Ag⁺) → metal deposited. Above hydrogen (K⁺ … Al³⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺) → $\text{H}^{+}$ is discharged as $\text{H}_{2}$; the near-cathode solution turns alkaline.

Gate 3 · Anode contest (oxidation)

$I^{-}$/$Br^{-}$ → iodine/bromine; $Cl^{-}$ concentrated → chlorine, dilute → oxygen; $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$/$\text{NO}_{3}^{-}$ never discharge — $\text{OH}^{-}$ gives $\text{O}_{2}$.

Gate 4 · Does the electrode overrule?

Copper (or silver) anode → the anode dissolves instead of any gas. Mercury cathode → sodium amalgam instead of $\text{H}_{2}$ (ms bank). Otherwise electrodes are inert and silent.

Multi-blank Quiz — Electrolysis

1. Electrolysis is the decomposition of a substance by electricity (direct current); an electrolytic cell converts electrical energy into chemical energy.
2. In electrolysis the anode is the positive electrode where oxidation occurs; cations migrate to the cathode and anions to the anode.
3. On wet filter paper, the purple $\text{MnO}_{4}^{-}$ band moves to the anode and the blue $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ band to the cathode; $\text{K}^{+}$ and $\text{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ migrate too but are colourless (CP 2016 Q2).
4. Electrolysing aqueous sodium chloride means juggling four ion species: Na⁺, Cl⁻, plus water’s H⁺ and OH⁻ — omitting them cost marks (CP 2015 Q7).
5. At the cathode the ion lower in the electrochemical series discharges: $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ (below hydrogen) is discharged/deposited as Cu, but $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ loses to H⁺ ⇒ hydrogen gas (CP 2014 Q8).
6. The anion preference order is $\text{I}^{-} > \text{Br}^{-} > \text{Cl}^{-} >$ OH⁻; sulfate and nitrate are never discharged in aqueous solution, so O₂ appears instead.
7. Concentration flips the chloride verdict: concentrated Cl⁻ gives chlorine, dilute gives oxygen — and the syllabus’s third factor is the nature of the electrodes.
8. Electrolysing molten $\text{SrCl}_{2}$: cathode $\text{Sr}^{2+}\text{(l)} + 2\text{e}^{-} \rightarrow$ Sr(l); anode Cl₂(g) — the state symbols are (l), not (aq) (ms summary App. C.2).
9. Dilute sulphuric acid with inert electrodes: gases $\text{H}_{2}$ and O₂ in volume ratio 2 : 1; test hydrogen with a lighted splint (pop) and the acid left behind becomes more concentrated.
10. Chlor-alkali feeds the industry with brine (concentrated sodium chloride solution) (CP 2013): chlorine at the anode, hydrogen at the cathode, plus NaOH(aq) in solution; the mercury cell fell from favour because “Mercury is poisonous” (ms bank; CP 2015).
11. Electroplating nickel: the object is the cathode, the nickel bar the anode, the electrolyte contains Ni²⁺ ions; degrease first because “oily dirt hinders conduction / plating” (ms summary §7.4), and reducing 1 mol of $\text{Ni}^{2+}$ (like $\text{Cu}^{2+}$) needs 2 mol of electrons (CP 2015 Q7).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Electrolysis

Discharged vs deposited vs oxidised

Ions are discharged (leave solution via electron transfer); metals being plated are deposited; the sacrificial protection metal is oxidised/corrodes. “Magnesium is preferentially discharged” in a corrosion answer scored nothing (CP 2012 Q9).

Cell anode (−) vs electrolytic anode (+)

Both are where oxidation occurs — only the sign flips because one device pushes electrons out and the other sucks them in. Say which device you are in before naming any sign.

“Sodium is discharged” from brine?

On ordinary electrodes, $\text{H}^{+}$ wins the cathode contest (H₂ gas) — sodium only exits as amalgam on a flowing mercury cathode “and reacts with water forming NaOH(aq)” (ms bank). Molten NaCl is the only common route to sodium metal.

CuSO₄: fading vs stable blue

Inert electrodes: blue fades + acidifies. Copper electrodes: “no observable change (Cu²⁺ concentration maintained)” (ms summary §7.4). The electrode material decides which observation story you tell (CP 2015 Q7(f) context).

Iodine: brown solid? purple gas?

In the electrolysis cell it is “I₂ which dissolves in KI(aq) to give brown I₃⁻ ions” — a brown solution; “a brown solid or a brown gas” were recorded wrong answers (ms bank; CP 2013).

Electrode mass vs solution colour as “observation”

Answers stated the reddish-brown deposit but “without referring to the observable changes that would occur in the solution” lost the mark — observations must cover both phases (CP 2015 Q7).

One mole of electrons buys one mole of metal?

Only for 1⁺ ions. $\text{Cu}^{2+}$: 2 mol e⁻ per mol Cu; $\text{Al}^{3+}$: 3. Read the charge, then halve or third: “mass = n(e⁻) × M / 2” (ms summary §7.4; CP 2015 Q7(g)).

2 · Oxidation-number rules — the taught list, in order▾

R1 Every atom in a free element: 0 (Na, Cl₂, O₂, diamond). R2 Monatomic ion: equal to its charge (Fe³⁺ → +III; S²⁻ → −II).

R3 Hydrogen in compounds: +I — except metal hydrides (NaH: H = −I). R4 Oxygen in compounds: −II — except peroxides (H₂O₂: O = −I).

R5 Fixed patterns: Group I +I, Group II +II, Al +III, fluorine always −I; other halogens −I unless combined with oxygen or a more electronegative halogen.

R6 Sum rule: for a neutral compound Σ(ON × atoms) = 0; for an ion = the ionic charge. Solve for the unknown with signs shown.

R7 Read the change: rising ⇒ oxidised (the species is the reducing agent); falling ⇒ reduced (the species is the oxidising agent). Worked bank examples: “the oxidation number of N in HONH₂(aq) is −1”; oxide “−1 + 2 = +1” ⇒ N₂O; “oxidation number of Mn … +4 to +3” ⇒ MnO₂ is the oxidising agent; “Cu changes from +1 in Cu₂O to +2 in CuSO₄” — variable numbers exist.

3 · The half-equation method & the oxidation-number method — steps▾

Half-equation method (acidic): ① balance the redox atom; ② balance O by adding H₂O; ③ balance H by adding H⁺; ④ balance charge by adding e⁻; ⑤ scale the two halves to equal electrons and add — the overall has no free electrons; ⑥ re-check atoms, charge, and state symbols.

Alkaline / neutral (fuel-cell) medium: reach for OH⁻ and H₂O instead of H⁺ — e.g. negative: $\text{H}_{2}\text{(g)} + 2\text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_{2}\text{O(l)} + 2\text{e}^{-}$.

Oxidation-number method: ① write ON under each atom; ② compute increase and decrease (per atom, times atoms transferred); ③ cross-multiply so totals cancel; ④ place those coefficients, finish by inspection; ⑤ verify O, H and charge balance everywhere.

Form check: “half equation” keeps e⁻; “overall / ionic equation” does not; spectators (K⁺, Na⁺, NO₃⁻, unreacted SO₄²⁻) stay out of ionic equations; “with state symbols” is a mark line, not a suggestion.

7 · NOT ACCEPT — phrases that score zero (HKEAA wording bank + CP-recorded errors)▾
Written answerAward instead
“Electrolysis generates electricity” / “a cell decomposes a substance electrically”Electrolysis uses electricity to decompose; a chemical cell generates electricity — the conversions are opposite.
“Electrons flow / pass through the solution” (or through the salt bridge — “the lemon … allows electrons to pass through”)Ions carry charge in the liquid; electrons only in the electrodes and wire (CP 2012 Q3; CP 2015 Q7; CP 2016 Q2).
“K₂SO₄(aq) provides mobile electrons”It provides mobile ions (K⁺, SO₄²⁻, plus H⁺, OH⁻) (CP 2016 Q2).
“The cell / electrode produces electrons”Oxidation at the negative electrode releases electrons from zinc atoms — the cell separates an existing redox, it does not manufacture electrons.
“Magnesium is preferentially discharged” (sacrificial protection)Magnesium is oxidised / corrodes preferentially — discharge is for ions leaving an electrolysed solution (CP 2012 Q9).
“H⁺ is oxidised to hydrogen” / “copper is reduced at the negative electrode” (during discharge)H⁺ is discharged and reduced to H₂; at a cell’s negative electrode copper (as anode material) is oxidised, and the MS rejection wording covers the reverse claim (ms bank).
“Zn is the oxidising agent” (from Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻)Rising number ⇒ zinc (oxidised) is the reducing agent (CP 2016 Q8).
“Better oxidising agent”Say stronger oxidising/reducing agent (ms summary App. A).
“Sodium is deposited when electrolysis aqueous sodium chloride” (ordinary electrodes)H⁺ discharges in preference (H₂ gas); sodium only appears as amalgam on a mercury cathode, or from the molten salt (ms bank; CP 2014 Q8 logic).
“Electrolysis of sodium chloride solution” for the industrial processName brine / concentrated sodium chloride solution, the cell type, and each gas’s electrode (CP 2013).
“Brown solid / brown gas” for the iodine productI₂ dissolves in KI(aq) to give brown I₃⁻ ions (ms bank; CP 2013).
Structural claims about commercial cells (“the zinc-carbon cell contains a … paste that works by …”)Not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VII (a)) — quote characteristics; apply given equations for working questions (NTF-2018 II-B VII (d)).
“H₂(g) → 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻” at the anode of an alkaline fuel cellMatch the medium: in concentrated KOH(aq) the anode half is H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ — candidates “wrongly suggested that H2(g) is oxidised to H+(aq) ions in the alkaline medium at anode” (CP 2025 Q9).
A salt bridge drawn into an electrolysis set-up diagramTwo electrodes in one electrolyte, driven by a d.c. supply whose +/− terminals you label — “Incorrectly included a salt bridge in the diagram for the set-up” was marked wrong (CP 2023 Q8); bridges separate the half-cells of a chemical cell.
“The concentration of chloride ions is high” (stop there)Complete the comparison: Cl⁻(aq) is far more concentrated than OH⁻(aq), so chloride is preferentially discharged — answers “without a comparative sense” were logged (CP 2022 Paper 2 context).

☐ Three registers of oxidation/reduction: O·H atoms, electrons, oxidation numbers — answer the one the stem names.
☐ OIL RIG; the species oxidised is the reducing agent.
☐ Free element 0; monatomic ion = charge; H +I (−I in hydrides); O −II (−I in peroxides); Σ = species charge.
☐ Show the ON working with signs — “−1 + 2 = +1” bank-style.
☐ Acidified KMnO₄ / K₂Cr₂O₇: “acidified” is part of the name; purple→colourless, orange→green.
☐ Conc. HNO₃ / conc. H₂SO₄ are oxidising acids — products NO₂ / SO₂, never H₂.
☐ Balancing order: atoms → O (H₂O) → H (H⁺) → charge (e⁻); acidic recipe never inside alkali.
☐ Overall ionic equations contain no free electrons and no spectators (K⁺, NO₃⁻ out).
☐ Half vs overall vs complete: give the exact form asked; state symbols carry marks.
☐ Cell: chemical→electrical; more reactive metal = negative electrode = oxidised; e⁻ flow −→+ outside.
☐ Salt bridge = ion path + charge balance; removing it = circuit open = current stops.
☐ Voltage explained by ECS separation of the pair; size of electrodes ≠ voltage.
☐ Inert Pt/graphite never appears in the equation — ions do the chemistry.
☐ Fuel cell: H₂ to OH⁻ half at negative, O₂ half at positive, only water out — no “recharging” language.
☐ Recharge: reverse reaction; reduction at the negative electrode (Ni–Cd, 2016 Q15).
☐ Six commercial cells: characteristics only (≈ voltages: 1.5 / 1.5 / 1.5 / 3.6–3.7 / 1.2 / 2 per cell). Internals = NOT expected.
☐ Electrolysis first move: list all ions present (metal, H⁺, non-metal, OH⁻) — water’s ions included.

9 · Famous Misconceptions — flip your answer before an examiner does▾

Wrong ideas collected from HKDSE Candidates’ Performance records 2012–2025, the mark-scheme keyword bank, and the RSC / Oak classroom-research framing of electrochemistry misconceptions (universal ideas, other-board labels stripped).

The wrong ideaExam-true correctionSource
“Electricity travels through the electrolyte as electrons.”In the liquid, current = ions migrating (cations → cathode, anions → anode); electrons move only in the metals/wires.CP 2012 Q3; CP 2015 Q7; CP 2016 Q2; RSC framing
“The battery/cell pushes electrons which get used up in the solution.”A closed loop consumes no electrons: they enter the cathode half-reaction and leave the anode half-reaction in equal amounts; the cell converts chemical to electrical energy.Oak/RSC circuit misconception; ms summary §7.1
“Anode = positive, always.”Anode = where oxidation happens (definition). Sign flips by device: cell anode −, electrolytic anode +.syllabus (b)/(e); CP 2014 Q8 confusion
“Oxygen is −II everywhere and hydrogen +I everywhere.”Defaults with taught exceptions: H₂O₂ gives O = −I; NaH gives H = −I. Let the sum rule catch violations.rule set (c); ms bank ON workings
“A substance whose oxidation number rises is the oxidising agent.”Rise ⇒ oxidised ⇒ reducing agent. CP’s named wrong answer: “Zn as the oxidising agent”.CP 2016 Q8; ms bank
“More reactive metal ⇒ its ion is the stronger oxidising agent.”The stronger reducing agent (high metal) has the weakest ion as oxidising agent; oxidising power of ions grows downwards the series.CP 2012 Q3 reversed order; ms summary §7.3
“Electrolysis produces the electricity in a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.”Reversed: the fuel cell generates electricity from the spontaneous H₂/O₂ redox; producing hydrogen by electrolysis costs electricity (whose generation “can also pose harm”).CP 2013; syllabus overview
“Recharging just puts charge back the same way it left.”Recharge reverses the cell reaction; at the negative electrode reduction now occurs — pick the reversed half equation, not the discharge one.CP 2016 Q15 (Ni–Cd)
“Copper gets ‘discharged onto’ the object” / “magnesium is discharged”.Ions are discharged; a metal is deposited; a corroding sacrificial metal is oxidised. Use the register for the particle.CP 2012 Q9; ms bank phrasings
“One mole of electrons plates one mole of any metal.”It plates one mole of 1⁺ ions only: Cu²⁺ and Pb²⁺ halve it, Al³⁺ thirds it — read the charge before the arithmetic.CP 2015 Q7(g); ms summary §7.4
“The KOH(aq) in a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell is a catalyst.”KOH(aq) is the electrolyte — the ion highway; the catalytic role belongs to the porous platinum electrodes (about a quarter of candidates got that in 2025).CP 2025 Q9
“An electrolysis set-up needs a salt bridge between the electrodes.”An electrolytic cell dips both electrodes into one electrolyte with a d.c. supply; salt bridges belong to the two half-cells of a chemical cell.CP 2023 Q8; CP 2024 Q3
