Topic IX Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(c))

Topic IX (a) Following reaction progress

Select and justify techniques to follow the progress of a reaction

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Four assessed technique families: titrimetric analysis; measuring changes in volume / pressure of gases; mass of a mixture; colour intensity of a mixture — plus turbidity in interpreting results.
  • Select and justify for a given reaction: what observable actually changes? (gas evolved? gas consumed? coloured ion? precipitate?)
  • Distinguish continuous monitoring (syringe, balance, sensor) from quenching + sampling (titration of withdrawn aliquots).
  • Interpret the resulting progress graphs: volume–time, mass–time, absorbance-style colour trends.
  • Apparatus-level accuracy only — calculation and instrumentation details of these techniques are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B IX (a)); colorimetry calibration curves belong to Analytical Chemistry (Topic XV).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Suggesting colorimetry is credited only with the supporting observation (“the solution is colourless at the start and becomes coloured”) — recorded failure (CP 2014). “Calorimetry” written for a colour-change detector = zero (CP 2016). Mass-loss fails when the gas is soluble or dense-retained (NH₃, HCl, Cl₂) or when no gas escapes at all. Micro-scale is about waste and safety, not speed (CP 2015).

Topic IX (a) Instantaneous & average rate

Determine instantaneous and average rate from a suitable graph; initial rate at t = 0

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Average rate $= \dfrac{\text{change in quantity}}{\text{time taken}}$ over the stated interval — read the endpoints off the graph, divide, and attach the compound unit.
  • Instantaneous rate at time t = slope of the tangent to the curve at t (“determine the slope of the tangent of curve at t = 10 s” — MS bank).
  • Initial rate = instantaneous rate at t = 0 (tangent drawn through the origin point of the curve) (Part IX (a) wording).
  • Rate is largest at the start and falls as reactants are used up — [reactant] drops → fewer effective collisions per unit time.
  • Express rate with respect to a named species: e.g. for $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$, O₂ formed at half the rate H₂O₂ is consumed.
  • Units track the measured quantity: cm³ s⁻¹ · g s⁻¹ · mol s⁻¹ · mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Explaining “initial rate” is a standing loss — three quarters could not (CP 2014). “Rate = total volume” (a quantity, not a rate) and tangent-as-chord to a later point both score zero. A wrong unit voided an otherwise correct answer (CP 2012 Q11). For “initial rate of decomposition of H₂O₂”, halving/doubling per the equation is where full credit lives (CP 2014).

Topic IX (b) Factors affecting rate

Concentration, temperature, surface area, catalyst — explained qualitatively by collision theory

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Collision theory skeleton: reactions occur when colliding particles carry energy ≥ activation energy Eₐ — “effective collisions per unit time” is the currency of every answer.
  • Concentration ↑ → more particles per unit volume → higher collision frequency → more effective collisions per unit time (Part IX (b) wording).
  • Temperature ↑ → particles move faster (collision frequency ↑) and average kinetic energy ↑, so a larger portion of collisions have E ≥ Eₐ — state both halves.
  • Surface area ↑ (powder vs lump) → more exposed particle area → more chance of collisions at the solid–liquid interface.
  • Catalyst → alternative pathway with lower Eₐ → at the same temperature, more particles have sufficient energy → effective collisions per unit time ↑; catalyst is unchanged chemically at the end.
  • Design experiments on each factor: one variable at a time, all others controlled; interpret results (e.g. volume–time graphs from first-hand investigations).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Do not draw or name the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curve here — that explanation belongs to Industrial Chemistry (NTF-2018 II-B IX (b)); say “a larger portion / fraction of molecules have energy ≥ Eₐ”, nothing about curves. “Particles expand / get bigger / are excited” when heated: rejected wording. Keeping total volume constant across trials is the fair-test mark candidates keep losing (CP 2016 Q11); volume ≠ concentration (CP 2016).

Topic IX (b) Catalysts in industry & life

Appreciate the importance of catalysts in chemical industries and biological systems

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Industrially, catalysts let processes run faster and at lower temperatures — saving fuel and cost; practically all large-scale chemical plants use them.
  • In living systems, reactions are catalysed by enzymes — biological catalysts at body temperature.
  • Mechanism sentence stays the same everywhere: alternative pathway of lower activation energy; a catalyst is not consumed overall.
  • A catalyst speeds the forward and backward reactions to the same extent — it never increases the yield of a reaction at equilibrium (bridge to Topics X / XIII).
  • Be aware of importance only — specific industrial processes and biological systems are NOT to be described here (NTF-2018 II-B IX (b)).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“The catalyst increases the yield” is the single most-rejected clause in catalysis answers. CP logged “a positive catalyst is related to an alternative pathway with lower activation energy” as the explanation candidates could not supply (CP 2012), and the wrong catalyst named where a specific one was required (CP 2012; CP 2013). Named processes (finely divided iron in ammonia manufacture, catalyst poisoning) are Topic XIII — mention the principle, not the plant.

Topic IX (c) Molar volume of gases

Molar volume of gases at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.) — complete the stoichiometric toolkit

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • At r.t.p. the molar volume of any gas is 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹; at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules (Avogadro’s principle).
  • Deduce the 24 dm³ value from a given data set (Part IX (c) wording): mass → moles → volume → V ÷ n.
  • Use $n = \dfrac{V}{24.0\ \text{dm}^3}$ (V in dm³) and $\dfrac{V(\text{cm}^3)}{24\,000}$ interchangeably — the ×1000 step is the mark.
  • Stoichiometric calculations involving gases: mass ↔ moles ↔ volume at r.t.p. (magnesium + acid, carbonates + acid, airbag-style gas generation).
  • Molar volume applies to gases only, and it is a conversion factor (dm³ mol⁻¹), not an amount.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

22.4 dm³ is s.t.p. (0 °C) — not the DSE r.t.p. value; using it loses the final answer. CP logged the quantity-unit slip “0.00649 mol dm⁻³” for a volume answer (CP 2015). Airbag-style chains die at the limiting reagent — “many failed to recognise that Na(s) is the limiting reagent” (CP 2013). The 24.0 figure is given data, deduced once and then used — questions rarely ask you to state it from memory alone.

Methods Inspector — following the progress of a reaction

“Select and justify the following techniques…” — six inspector cards, one decision map, and every wording trap the reports record. Instrumentation internals are out of scope (NTF-2018 II-B IX (a)).

Pick a technique

IX (a) ii

Volume of gas collected

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\Delta V(\text{gas})}{\Delta t} \qquad [\text{cm}^3\ \text{s}^{-1}]$$

Apparatus: conical flask + delivery tube → gas syringe (or gas out over water into an inverted measuring cylinder / eudiometer). Read the volume at fixed time intervals; plot V–t.

When appropriate: a reaction giving a gas that is not very soluble in the reaction mixture — Mg + dilute HCl (H₂), H₂O₂ with MnO₂ (O₂), carbonates + acid (CO₂).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Start timing immediately after inserting the stopper — gas made before then is lost and the curve begins wrong.
  • Over water, the gas must be insoluble; a gas syringe avoids the solubility problem.
  • The V–t curve rises then flattens when the reaction finishes — final volume is constant.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Syringe friction / plunger sticking and stopper leaks are the standard “why is the volume low?” answers — name the actual defect.
  • Do not collect NH₃ or HCl over water: they dissolve before you measure them. CP logged wrong collection reasoning for hydrogen (CP 2015).
📏 Depth note: reading the syringe is the skill — calculation and instrumentation details of the apparatus are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B IX (a)).
IX (a) ii

Pressure of gas in a sealed vessel

$$n(\text{gas})\uparrow,\; V,T\ \text{fixed} \;\Rightarrow\; P\uparrow$$

Apparatus: sealed conical flask + pressure sensor and data-logger (or manometer); record P against time continuously.

When appropriate: gas produced (moles of gas increase) in a closed system where volume is fixed — handy when the gas volume is too small for a syringe, or the reaction is fast.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • P–t graph has the same shape as a V–t graph: rises, then levels off when reaction stops.
  • Data-logging gives “more accurate and efficient” monitoring (Guide teaching activities) — many readings, no human lag.
  • If the gas dissolves as it forms, P rises less than expected — same solubility caveat as over-water collection.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Explain results, do not describe sensor engineering — sensor internals are capped out (NTF IX (a)).
  • A reaction that consumes gas shows a falling P–t curve; read the direction before answering.
IX (a) ii

Mass of reaction mixture (mass loss)

$$\begin{gathered}m(t) = m_{\text{start}} - m_{\text{gas escaped}}\\[2pt] \text{mass lost per second} = \text{rate}\end{gathered}$$

Apparatus: conical flask on a top-pan balance, mouth covered with cotton wool / loose plug (lets gas out, keeps splashes in); record mass at fixed times; plot m–t.

When appropriate: gas escapes to the atmosphere and is not very soluble — marble chips + dilute HCl (CO₂), metal carbonates + acids.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Curve falls steeply then levels off; time to constant mass = reaction finished.
  • Cotton wool, not a tight stopper: a stopper seals the gas in and the mass never changes.
  • Rate comparisons can use “time to lose a fixed mass” — inverse reading like the cross method.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • NOT suitable when the gas is very soluble (NH₃, HCl) or heavy and slow to escape (Cl₂): much of it never leaves the flask, so the loss under-reads — state the reason, not just “inaccurate”.
  • No gas evolved? Mass loss is useless (e.g. NaOH + HCl) — pick another technique.
📏 Depth note: balance precision/error calculations for this method are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B IX (a)).
IX (a) i–ii

Colour intensity of a mixture

$$\begin{gathered}\text{MnO}_4^{-}: \text{purple}\\ \to\ \text{Mn}^{2+}: \text{colourless}\end{gathered}$$

Apparatus: at this level a colour comparator / eye against a white tile (qualitative), or a colorimeter reading absorbance / colour intensity against time for a cleaner curve.

When appropriate: a coloured reactant consumed (acidified KMnO₄ decolourised by C₂O₄²⁻) or a coloured product formed (yellow–brown iodine–starch colours) with the rest of the mixture colourless.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • State both ends of the colour journey: “colourless at the start, becomes brown” — CP marks dropped suggestions with no starting colour (CP 2014).
  • Absorbance / colour intensity ∝ concentration of the coloured species → rising or falling curve.
  • Colorimetry detects small colour changes more accurately than the eye (CP 2016 wording).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Calorimetry” is the recorded wrong instrument name (CP 2016) — a colorimeter reads colour, a calorimeter reads heat.
  • Intense natural colour (red wine, dark syrup) masks the change — the bank records this as a valid reason the method fails.
📏 Depth note: calibration curves and the quantitative machinery of colorimetry are Topic XV (Analytical) (NTF-2018 II-B IX (a)) — here: name it, justify it, interpret its trend.
IX (b) able-line

Turbidity of a mixture (precipitate formed)

$$\begin{gathered}\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-}(\text{aq}) + 2\text{H}^{+}(\text{aq}) \to\\ \text{S}(\text{s}) + \text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})\end{gathered}$$
Full equation: Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + SO₂(aq) + S(s) — the sulphur suspension is the measured change.

Apparatus: flask on a paper with a black cross; time from mixing until the cross disappears as sulphur clouds the solution; rate comparison via 1 / t.

When appropriate: reactions forming an insoluble solid that clouds the mixture — the thiosulphate–acid clock is the classic.

🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Turbidity of a mixture” appears on the interpret results able-line — expect graph/table questions built on it.
  • Same mass of cloud (obscuring the same light path) at the endpoint → 1/t is a fair rate measure provided conditions (volume, depth, eye position, light) are fixed.
  • SO₂ stays largely in solution — no gas-volume route here; turbidity is the practical choice.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Endpoint is subjective — improve reliability by the same observer, same mark, repeat and average.
  • Never claim the cross “disappears because the solid sinks” — it disappears because the sulphur suspension scatters the light.
IX (a) i

Titrimetric analysis — withdraw, quench, titrate

$$\text{aliquot at } t \;\xrightarrow{\text{quench}}\; \text{titrate}$$

Apparatus: pipette for timed aliquots; quench (pour into ice-cold water / excess of a reagent that stops further reaction); standard solution + indicator for the titration.

When appropriate: no gas, no colour, no precipitate — e.g. base hydrolysis of an ester: titrate the NaOH left at intervals (Guide teaching activity).

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Sampling, not continuous: each titration freezes one instant — contrast with syringe / balance / sensor methods.
  • A titrimetric method is practical only when the reaction has a detectable change, goes significantly complete, and the titration itself is fast enough (mark-scheme wording).
  • Plot concentration (or moles left) vs time; rates come off the graph as usual.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Explain why quenching: without it, the aliquot keeps reacting during the titration and every point is wrong.
  • Volumetric calculation details belong to Topic XV — in IX, credit lives on the design of the method (NTF IX (a)).

The Decide-Method Map — match the observable, justify in one line

Exam questions ask “suggest and justify a suitable method”. Run the reaction down these four gates; the first YES is your answer.

Gate 1 · Gas produced (insoluble)?

→ Measure volume of gas (gas syringe / over water) or pressure in a sealed vessel. Justify: “a gas is evolved and is not very soluble”. If the gas is soluble (NH₃, HCl): reject mass-loss/over-water — say why.

Gate 2 · Gas escapes to air?

→ Follow mass loss on a balance. Justify: “the gas escapes and is not very soluble, so the mixture’s mass falls”. Reject when the gas is retained or soluble — under-reading is the examiner keyword.

Gate 3 · Colour or cloudiness changes?

→ Colour intensity (coloured reactant/product; colorimeter = more accurate than the eye) or turbidity (insoluble product clouds the mix — disappearing cross, rate ∝ 1/t).

Gate 4 · None of the above?

→ Titrimetric analysis: withdraw timed aliquots, quench, titrate. Justify with the three requirements: detectable change, significantly complete, titration fast enough. (Guide: base hydrolysis of esters.)

Multi-blank Quiz — following reaction progress

1. For marble chips + dilute hydrochloric acid the mixture can be followed by mass loss on a balance or by collecting the CO₂ over water / in a gas syringe.

11. Micro-scale technique reduces reagent use and hazard — it does not increase the rate (CP 2015 wrong answer).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Methods

Mass loss vs mass of a sealed vessel

The balance method works only because the gas leaves. Stopper the flask and mass is constant forever — “cover the flask tightly to be accurate” inverts the method.

Any gas vs a soluble gas

CO₂/O₂/H₂ suit volume and mass methods; NH₃ and HCl are so soluble that most never escapes — the loss under-reads and you must choose titrimetric analysis instead. Naming the solubility is the mark.

Colorimeter vs calorimeter

Colour in → colorimetry. Heat in → calorimetry (Topic VIII). CP 2016 logged “calorimetry” as the wrong-instrument answer to a colour-detection question.

Continuous monitoring vs quenching

Syringe / balance / sensor / colorimeter trace the reaction as it runs. Titrimetric analysis freezes samples at moments — calling a titration “continuous” is a recorded wording loss.

Turbidity endpoint vs objective instrument

“Cross disappears” depends on the observer — reliability comes from repeats and fixed conditions, not from claiming the endpoint is precise. Do not defend the method by pretending it is instrumental.

Method name vs justification

“Use a gas syringe” alone is half the answer. The mark pair is technique + observable reason (“a gas is produced and is not very soluble”). CP marks method suggestions down for missing the supporting observation.

Micro-scale = faster? No.

Micro-scale cuts reagent use, waste and hazard. Rate depends on concentration, temperature, surface area, catalyst — not on pipette size (CP 2015).

Engine B · Strand (a)

Rates Inspector — average, instantaneous & initial rate

Every number in strand (a) lives on a graph. Read the quantity, draw the right line, attach the right unit — this engine drills all three.

Pick a rate idea

IX (a)

Average rate = Δquantity ÷ Δtime

$$\bar{r} = \frac{\Delta(\text{quantity})}{\Delta t} = \frac{q_2 - q_1}{t_2 - t_1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Quantity” is whatever the graph tracks: gas volume, mass lost, moles, or concentration.
  • MS worked example: “Initial rate = 60/4 = 15 cm³ min⁻¹” — read two points, take the difference, divide by the time difference.
  • Average rate hides the fact that the true rate is falling — quote it only over the interval the question names.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Interval error: “rate over the first 40 s” needs the change between 0 and 40 s, not to the end of the reaction. CP’s most common graph slip.
  • Always carry the unit (cm³ min⁻¹ / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) — “a wrong unit in the answer” is a logged mark-loser (CP 2012 Q11).
IX (a)

Instantaneous rate — the tangent

$$r(t) = \frac{dq}{dt}\ \text{at}\ t\ =\ \text{tangent slope}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • MS wording: “determine the slope of the tangent of the curve at t = 10 s” — draw the tangent touching the curve at t, never through two data points.
  • Pick two widely-spaced points on the tangent line to read its gradient (rise/run), not two points on the curve.
  • Instantaneous rate is a slope; average rate is a chord — the two must not be confused on the same answer.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Report the triangle you used (rise and run with units) — method marks are on the working, not only the number.
  • A tangent drawn across the whole curve is a chord: it gives the average, not the instantaneous, rate.
IX (a)

Initial rate = instantaneous rate at t = 0

$$r_0 = \left.\frac{dq}{dt}\right|_{t=0}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition (able-line): “recognise that initial rate equals the instantaneous rate at time t = 0”.
  • It is the steepest slope on the curve (for reactant-favoured readings) because concentration is highest at the start.
  • Initial-rate method (rate ∝ 1/time-to-a-fixed-change) sidesteps later slowing — used to compare experiments.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Explain the term initial rate” failed for three-quarters of candidates (CP 2014) — say rate at the very start of the reaction (t = 0), measured from the tangent there.
  • When rate is asked “with respect to BrO₃⁻(aq)”, tie the quantity to that ion — not to the whole mixture (CP 2014).
IX (a)

Why the rate falls as the reaction runs

$$\begin{gathered}[\text{reactant}]\downarrow \Rightarrow \text{fewer effective}\\ \text{collisions per unit time} \Rightarrow \text{rate}\downarrow\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The reactants are used up → concentration decreases → fewer successful collisions each second → the curve flattens.
  • The graph is steepest at the start and horizontal at the end (reaction complete / limiting reagent spent).
  • This is the same collision-theory engine you use in Engine C — concentration is the factor at work.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Correct causal direction: concentration drop → rate drop. “Rate drops so concentration drops” is backwards.
  • Never claim the rate rises over time or “more catalyst forms” — the curve cannot get steeper for a simple reactant-only reaction.
IX (a)

Rate of one species vs another (stoichiometry)

$$\begin{gathered}2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2\\[2pt] r(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\ \text{used}) = 2\times\, r(\text{O}_2\ \text{made})\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Species rates are in the ratio of their balancing coefficients: for $a\text{A}+b\text{B}\to\dots$, $\dfrac{r_\text{A}}{a}=\dfrac{r_\text{B}}{b}$.
  • O₂ formed at half the rate H₂O₂ is consumed here; if O₂ = 0.030 mol s⁻¹, H₂O₂ disappears at 0.060 mol s⁻¹.
  • State whether a species is being formed (+) or used (−); the magnitudes still follow the coefficients.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP logged candidates unable to give “the initial rate of reaction with respect to BrO₃⁻(aq)” (CP 2014) — name the species and apply its coefficient.
  • Do not multiply by the wrong coefficient: the species with the bigger coefficient changes faster, not slower.

Reading any progress graph

$$\begin{gathered}\text{product curve: }\nearrow\text{ then flat}\\ \text{reactant curve: }\searrow\text{ then flat}\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals

  • Final (maximum) volume/mass = where the curve goes flat → the point of reaction completion; read total gas volume from the plateau.
  • Deduce a limiting-reagent finish: the curve levels because a reactant is used up, not because time ran out.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A mass–time curve falls; a gas-volume–time curve rises. Compare gradients of the same shape, not mirror images.
  • Excess reagent: the rate still falls with time (its own concentration drops) — “it is in excess” does not mean “rate is constant”.

The Rate Ladder — from two points to a tangent

Four rungs, each one a question type. Climb them in order and no rate graph surprises you.

Rung 1 · Average rate

Two points, subtract, divide. Over the named interval only. Unit = quantity-unit ÷ time-unit. (q₂−q₁)/(t₂−t₁).

Rung 2 · Instantaneous rate

Tangent at the point → gradient triangle on the line → slope. This is dq/dt read graphically, not a chord.

Rung 3 · Initial rate

Instantaneous rate at t = 0 — steepest point. Often compared across experiments; the species name and coefficient set the number.

Rung 4 · Species conversion

Divide/multiply by coefficients to move from “rate of O₂ made” to “rate of H₂O₂ used”. Same reaction, different species.

Multi-blank Quiz — rates from graphs

1. Average rate = change in quantity divided by change in time, taken over the interval the question names.
2. Instantaneous rate at time t is the slope of the tangent to the curve at t; the triangle points must lie on the tangent line, not the curve.
3. The initial rate equals the instantaneous rate at time t = 0, where the curve is steepest because reactant concentration is highest.
4. As the reaction proceeds the reactants are used up, concentration falls, so effective collisions per unit time decrease and the rate falls.
5. For $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$, if O₂ forms at 0.030 mol s⁻¹, H₂O₂ is consumed at 0.060 mol s⁻¹ — the coefficient 2 vs 1.
6. Rate read off a volume–time graph carries units cm³ s⁻¹; off a concentration–time graph, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹; a wrong unit voids the answer (CP 2012 Q11).
7. The curve goes flat when the reaction is complete; the plateau value is the total (maximum) quantity of product.
8. To compare two experiments by initial rate, measure the tangent at t = 0, or the reciprocal 1 / time for a fixed change — the fixed change must be the same quantity in every trial.
9. “Rate over the first 40 s” uses points at 0 s and 40 s — not the whole reaction; that interval choice is a frequent CP slip.
10. A reactant curve decreases to a flat line; a product curve increases to a flat line — same sign of completion (horizontal = finished), opposite direction.
11. Rate “with respect to a named species” needs that species’ quantity and its stoichiometric coefficient: the species with the bigger coefficient changes faster (CP 2014).
12. The causal chain to state: concentration falls → rate falls (never the reverse); the reaction ends when the limiting reactant is used up, so the curve cannot keep rising.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Rates

Tangent vs chord

Tangent touches the curve at one point → instantaneous rate. A chord through two points → average rate over that span. MS “slope of the tangent at t = 10 s” demands the touching line.

Rate falling vs rate rising

For a plain single-direction reaction the rate only falls (reactants thin out). A “rate increases then decreases” curve belongs to autocatalysis — not this topic — so never draw it here.

mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ vs mol s⁻¹ vs cm³ s⁻¹

The unit must match the axis: concentration → mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹; amount → mol s⁻¹; gas volume → cm³ s⁻¹. Swapping dm⁻³ in where the axis was volume is the CP-logged unit slip.

Average over wrong interval

“First 40 s” = between 0 and 40 s. Reading to the plateau (whole reaction) gives a smaller number — the average over the wrong span, marked wrong even with correct arithmetic.

Rate of A used vs rate of B formed

In $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\to\text{O}_2$, H₂O₂ disappears twice as fast as O₂ appears. Confusing which species is faster flips the answer; the bigger coefficient changes faster.

Initial rate vs “first reading”

The initial rate is the gradient at t = 0, not the volume/mass at your first measurement. Quoting a raw first data point as “the rate” is a category error (quantity ≠ rate).

Plateau = finished, not “paused”

The flat top means the limiting reactant is gone and net change has stopped. Writing “the reaction stopped because it ran out of time” or “temperature fell” invents causes the graph does not show.

Factors Inspector — collision theory in exam wording

Four factors, one mechanism sentence each, qualitative only. The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve stays in Topic XIII (NTF-2018 II-B IX (b)) — here you win with the frequency / energy / pathway discrimination.

Pick a factor

IX (b)

Collision theory — the qualitative skeleton

$$\begin{gathered}\text{collision} + \text{energy} \ge E_a\\[2pt] = \text{effective collision}\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Chemical reactions happen when reactant particles collide; only collisions with energy at least the activation energy Eₐ are effective.
  • The measured rate is the number of effective collisions per unit time — every factor explanation must end there.
  • Activation energy: the minimum energy particles must have before a collision can lead to reaction — “minimum energy for a reaction to start” is the CP-credited phrasing (CP 2014).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • One mechanism sentence per factor — say what changes in the collision picture, never just “the reaction speeds up”.
  • Words to avoid: particles “stick”, “get excited”, “expand”. Particles themselves do not change size or energy content except through temperature.
IX (b)

Concentration — crowding the arena

$$\begin{gathered}[\text{X}]\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{more particles}\\ \text{per unit volume} \Rightarrow \text{frequency}\uparrow\\ \Rightarrow \text{rate}\uparrow\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The mark-bearing phrase: “more reactant particles in the same volume → more frequent collisions → more effective collisions per unit time”.
  • MS pair: “Initial rate increases as the concentration of H⁺ increases” + “frequency of effective collisions increases” — copy this register.
  • For gases, higher pressure ≡ higher concentration (same crowding argument in a smaller volume).
  • Dilution is the inverse: fewer particles per unit volume → slower.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “More particles” alone is NOT ACCEPT — per unit volume. 100 cm³ of 2 M has twice the crowding of 100 cm³ of 1 M, but 200 cm³ of 1 M has the same crowding as 100 cm³ of 1 M: rate follows concentration, not amount (CP 2016).
  • 2.0 M H₂SO₄ beats 2.0 M HCl because [H⁺] is higher — the same-acid-concentration trap (CP 2012 Q11).
IX (b)

Temperature — the two-half answer

$$\begin{gathered}T\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{more frequent}\\ \text{collisions; avg KE}\uparrow\\ \Rightarrow \text{more with } E\ge E_a\\ \Rightarrow \text{rate}\uparrow\uparrow\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Bank phrases to own: “An increase in temperature will increase the average kinetic energy of the molecules” and “increases the collision frequency and results in greater effective collisions”.
  • Energy half is the powerful one: at the higher temperature a larger portion of molecules carry kinetic energy ≥ Eₐ, so proportionally far more collisions succeed.
  • Without drawing any distribution curve, you may state “the fraction with E ≥ Eₐ increases” — curve mechanics belong to XIII (NTF IX (b)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A frequency-only answer was recorded as a partial at best (CP 2013: “just stated collision frequency… without making reference to the graph”) — give both halves.
  • Particles do not expand, grow or store heat — “the particles get bigger with heating” is a zero-score phrase.
IX (b)

Surface area — powder vs lump

$$\begin{gathered}\text{powder} \Rightarrow \text{more surface area}\\ \Rightarrow \text{collisions/s}\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{rate}\uparrow\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Only particles at the surface can collide with the other reactant — grinding a lump exposes far more surface to the acid.
  • Bank wording: “Fine powder can greatly increase the reaction rate by providing a large surface area”.
  • Graph signature: powder reaches the same final volume faster — steeper initial slope, identical plateau (same moles of gas).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The failure CP recorded is omitting the surface-area clause entirely (CP 2013) — name it as the reason, not “smaller pieces” alone.
  • Do not say “the particles become smaller so they react faster”: the mass and the particles are unchanged — it is the exposed area per gram that changed.
IX (b)

Catalyst — the pathway answer

$$\begin{gathered}\text{catalyst} \Rightarrow \text{alternative pathway}\\ \text{with lower } E_a \Rightarrow \text{rate}\uparrow\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Full MS chain: “the reaction would proceed in an alternative pathway with lower activation energy” → “more particles would possess sufficient kinetic energy to react” → “the number of effective collisions per unit time becomes greater”.
  • A catalyst is not used up overall; a tiny mass suffices because it is regenerated.
  • Catalyst ≠ extra reactant: adding “more concentrated” catalyst solution works by the pathway, not by supplying more particles to collide (CP 2013 wrong answer).
  • At equilibrium a catalyst speeds forward and backward reactions to the same extent — it reaches the endpoint sooner, never makes more product.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Increases the collision frequency” is the WRONG mechanism for a catalyst — that is the concentration/temperature register (CP 2013, CP 2012).
  • “Lowers the activation energy of the reaction” was recorded wrong vs “provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy” (CP 2013) — verbs matter.
  • “Increases the yield” = zero. A catalyst never changes the final amount of product.
IX (b) aware

Catalysis in industry & living systems — awareness only

$$\begin{gathered}\text{milder } T,\ \text{faster} \Rightarrow \text{fuel saved}\\ \text{enzymes} = \text{bio catalysts}\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Industrially: catalysts make processes feasible at milder temperatures and faster — the same chemistry in less time, with less energy bill; “practically all chemical reactions in large-scale plants involve catalysts” (Guide overview).
  • In living systems: the reactions occurring in living systems are catalysed by enzymes — name them as biological catalysts and stop there.
  • Deeper catalysis — named plants, conditions, catalyst compositions, poisoning — is Topic XIII (NTF-2018 II-B IX (b)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Questions ask the importance: “faster rate → more product per hour” and “lower temperature → less fuel” are the marks; brand names of processes are not needed and not credited here.
  • Enzyme detail (lock-and-key, denaturation curves) belongs elsewhere — one sentence: enzymes are biological catalysts that work at body temperature.
IX (b) design

Designing the experiments — one variable, everything else fixed

$$\begin{gathered}\text{compare rates: time to a}\\ \text{fixed change, or tangent at }t=0\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Concentration trials: vary one solution’s concentration but keep the total volume constant (make up with water) — CP: “less than half could state this” (CP 2016 Q11).
  • Surface-area trials: same mass of the same solid, lump vs powder; everything else identical.
  • Temperature trials: same volumes and concentrations, water-bath both flasks, measure the gas/mass change.
  • Rate comparisons must be defined the same way in every trial (same volume read, or same mass lost).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Repeat and average — reliability language earns method marks that “use more accurate apparatus” never will.
  • Interpreting two curves: same plateau = same final amount; steeper initial slope = faster rate; a lower plateau with a steeper start is impossible for the same moles of limiting reagent — watch inconsistent student graphs.

Factor Classifier — which half of the collision engine does it touch?

Every explanation is one of three sentences. Name the sentence first, then finish with “more effective collisions per unit time”.

Sentence 1 · More frequent collisions

Used by concentration (and gas pressure) and surface area (exposed face). Crowd more particles into the same space, or open more surface, and collisions thicken. Frequency only — energy unchanged.

Sentence 2 · More energetic collisions

Used by temperature alone. Faster particles (higher average KE) → a larger portion of collisions clear Eₐ. Temperature gets both halves: frequency AND energy.

Sentence 3 · Easier to succeed

Used by a catalyst alone: an alternative pathway of lower Eₐ — at the same temperature more collisions now qualify. Never “more collisions”; the pathway is the story.

Shared ending (always add)

“…so the number of effective collisions per unit time increases → the rate of reaction increases.” Without this final clause the chain is incomplete and marks are capped.

Multi-blank Quiz — factors affecting rate

1. For a reaction to occur, reactant particles must collide with energy at least equal to the activation energy (Eₐ); successful collisions are called effective collisions.
2. Raising the concentration increases rate because there are more reactant particles per unit volume, so the collision frequency — and hence effective collisions per unit time — increases.
3. 2.0 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄ reacts with magnesium faster than 2.0 mol dm⁻³ HCl because its [H⁺(aq)] is higher — sulphuric acid is dibasic — the increase in initial rate is due to higher H⁺ concentration, not “stronger acid” (CP 2012 Q11).
4. Heating increases rate by two effects: particles move faster (more frequent collisions) and their average kinetic energy rises, so a larger portion of collisions have E ≥ Eₐ.
5. Grinding a lump to powder increases the total surface area exposed; collisions can then happen at more surface sites per second — the particles themselves do not change size.
6. A catalyst works by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy; at the same temperature, more particles then possess sufficient (enough) energy to react.
7. “The catalyst increases the collision frequency” is wrong register — frequency language belongs to concentration / temperature / surface area; the catalyst’s verb is pathway.
8. With a catalyst the final volume of gas is unchanged — the catalyst is not consumed and never increases the yield.
9. Industry values catalysts because reactions run faster and at lower temperatures, saving fuel / energy costs; describing a specific named process is not expected (NTF-2018 II-B IX (b)).
10. Reactions in living systems are catalysed by enzymes — biological catalysts working at body temperature (awareness level only).
11. In concentration trials the total volume must be kept the same in every trial (top up with water) so that concentration is the only variable — CP: less than half stated it (CP 2016 Q11).
12. Rate of neutralisation “compared by how fast the beaker warms up” conflates heat with speed; a valid comparison measures the time for a fixed change under fixed conditions — comparing rates of neutralisation to prove the strength of an alkali was a logged wrong method (CP 2015).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Factors

Catalyst register vs temperature register

Catalyst = pathway / lower Eₐ. Temperature = kinetic energy / more frequent + more energetic collisions. Mixing them (“a catalyst increases collision frequency”, “heating lowers Eₐ”) is the CP-logged pair of zeroes (CP 2012; CP 2013).

“Lowers Eₐ” vs “provides a pathway of lower Eₐ”

Eₐ is a property of the reaction route, not a setting the catalyst turns down — the accepted verb is provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy (CP 2013).

“Rate ↑ because more product” vs “rate ↓ because reactants used up”

Product does not drive a simple reaction faster. The rate falls because reactant concentration falls — the curve must be drawn steepest at t = 0.

Particles grow / get excited? No.

Heating changes particle speed and energy, never their size or kind. “Particles expand”, “particles become excited”, “bonds break inside the particles when heated” — all rejected classroom phrasings.

More particles vs more particles per unit volume

Doubling the volume of the same solution adds particles but keeps concentration — rate does not speed up. CP: “wrongly stated that the rate was affected by the volume of NaOH(aq) added” (CP 2016).

Catalyst amount vs catalyst concentration

A small amount of the right catalyst suffices; “more catalyst solution → more collisions” borrows the concentration register. Homogeneous/heterogeneous catalysis detail is Topic XIII — not expected here (NTF-2018 II-B IX (b)).

Fair test vs more accurate

Improving a comparison means controlling the variables (same total volume, same mass, same temperature, same measurement point), not “use a better balance”. Reliability language: repeat and average.

Molar Volume Inspector — the gas gate of stoichiometry

One constant does all the work: 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at r.t.p. — deduce it once from data, then use it until it is boring.

Pick a conversion idea

IX (c)

Molar volume of a gas at r.t.p. = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹

$$\begin{gathered}V_m = \dfrac{V}{n}\\[2pt] = 24.0\ \text{dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}\\ \text{(r.t.p.)}\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • One mole of ANY gas occupies the same volume at the same temperature and pressure — at r.t.p. that volume is 24.0 dm³ (Guide: “the constant molar volume of gases at room temperature and pressure viz. 24 dm³”).
  • Equal volumes of different gases (same T, P) contain equal numbers of molecules — the reason the value is universal while molar masses differ.
  • r.t.p. here is room temperature and pressure — not 0 °C; 22.4 dm³ mol⁻¹ belongs to s.t.p. and is not the DSE value.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Treat the unit as arithmetic: mol × (dm³ mol⁻¹) = dm³. If your unit cancels wrongly, your quantity is wrong.
  • The value only applies to gases — “24 dm³ of liquid water” is meaningless at this level.
IX (c)

Volume ↔ moles — the two-way door

$$\begin{gathered}n = \dfrac{V}{24.0}\ (\text{V in dm}^3)\\[4pt] n = \dfrac{V}{24\,000}\ (\text{V in cm}^3)\\[4pt] V = n \times 24.0\ \text{dm}^3\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Decide the volume unit first: dm³ → divide by 24.0; cm³ → divide by 24 000 (or convert ×10⁻³ then ÷ 24.0).
  • 0.25 mol of gas at r.t.p. → V = 0.25 × 24.0 = 6.0 dm³ = 6 000 cm³.
  • 360 cm³ of CO₂ → n = 0.360 ÷ 24.0 = 0.015 mol.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2015 logged the answer units themselves drifting: “6.49 dm³” vs “0.00649 mol dm⁻³” for the same quantity — name the quantity (volume, unit dm³) before writing digits.
  • Never divide a cm³ reading by 24.0 — that silently cuts the answer 1000-fold.
IX (c)

Deducing 24 dm³ from a given data set

$$\begin{gathered}0.0500\ \text{mol occupies } 1.20\ \text{dm}^3\\[2pt] V_m = \dfrac{1.20}{0.0500} = 24.0\\[2pt] [\,24.0\ \text{dm}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}\,]\end{gathered}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Able-line: “deduce the molar volume of gases at r.t.p. as 24 dm³ using a given data set” — find moles (from mass ÷ M, or from the equation), divide the measured volume by them.
  • The deduction is a one-step division shown with its unit — hide the arithmetic and lose the method mark.
  • Use your deduced value in the rest of the question even if it differs slightly from 24.0 — follow-through credit runs on consistency.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Moles come from the pure gas actually collected: if some gas dissolved or leaked, the deduced Vₘ is off — that is experiment commentary, not a re-do of the arithmetic.
  • Practical determination (“perform experiments to determine the molar volume of a gas”) is on the activities list — expect design-of-method questions, not instrument internals (NTF IX (a)).

Mass ↔ gas volume — the two-gate route

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mass never converts to volume directly — moles are always the bridge: 0.48 g Mg → 0.48 ÷ 24.3 = 0.0198 mol Mg → same mol H₂ → V = 0.0198 × 24.0 = 0.474 dm³ ≈ 474 cm³.
  • Work backwards the same way when the graph gives you a plateau volume: V → n(gas) → n(solid) → mass or Mr.
  • The bridge idea is what the Guide wants the molar volume “for a complete stoichiometric treatment of chemical equations”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Use the mole ratio from the equation at the bridge — 1 : 1 for Mg : H₂, 1 : 1 for CaCO₃ : CO₂, 2 : 1 for Al : H₂ — not by heart, by balancing.
  • State symbols and the 24.0 assumption: the volume is at r.t.p.; if the question says “measured at r.t.p.”, the conversion is a green light.

Volume ratios = mole ratios (gases at the same T & P)

🎯 Learning Goals

  • 40 cm³ H₂ need only 20 cm³ O₂ to burn completely; leftover gas = excess reagent.
  • Product water is a liquid at r.t.p. — it contributes no gas volume to the final mix (condensation-style questions).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Only apply volume-ratio thinking when every species counted is a gas at the same T, P; aqueous or liquid species never take part in the ratio.
  • “Reacting volumes” questions are answered in cm³ — you need not touch 24.0 when the ratio itself does the work.

Limiting reagent decides the gas yield

🎯 Learning Goals

The Gas Mole Highway — four gates into n, one exit to V

Every IX(c) calculation is a route: enter through one gate, cross the bridge, leave through the exit. Moles are always the bridge.

Gate 1 · From mass of a substance

$n = m / M$ with M from the formula (CaCO₃ = 100.1, Mg = 24.3). This gate opens on “what mass produces this volume?” questions.

Gate 2 · From volume of a gas at r.t.p.

$n = V / 24.0$ (dm³) or $V / 24\,000$ (cm³). The signature move of this topic.

Gate 3 · From a solution

$n = c \times V$ (V in dm³!). 25.0 cm³ of 2.0 M = 0.050 mol — the ×10⁻³ conversion before the multiplication.

Gate 4 · Across the equation bridge

Mole ratio from the balanced equation moves you from one species to another (1 Mg ↔ 1 H₂; 1 CO₃²⁻ ↔ 1 CO₂; 2 Al ↔ 3 H₂).

The exit ramp — and the exam chain

Exit: $V = n \times 24.0\ \text{dm}^3$ — only once n(gas) is final and the limiting reagent has been checked. Typical Paper 1B chain: graph plateau → Gate 2 (moles of gas) → Gate 4 (moles of solid) → Gate 1 reversed (mass or Mr) — or the mass-to-volume direction used to deduce 24.0 first (Gate 1 → Gate 2 reversed). One more gate is a rate one: divide the volume change by the time it took (Engine B).

10. One mole of ammonia and one mole of carbon dioxide occupy the same volume at the same T and P, but their masses differ — 17 g of NH₃ vs 44 g of CO₂.
11. Molar volume is a conversion factor (dm³ mol⁻¹) — it is neither an amount nor a concentration; “mol dm⁻³” belongs to c, plain “dm³” to V.
12. The Guide adds molar volume to this topic “for a complete stoichiometric treatment of chemical equations” — expect it inside larger questions, and when asked to deduce it, divide the volume by the moles and quote the unit dm³ mol⁻¹ from the given data set.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Molar Volume

24.0 (r.t.p.) vs 22.4 (s.t.p.)

DSE gases live at room temperature and pressure: 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹. Importing 22.4 from 0 °C answers deflates every volume by ~7 % — and the marker knows.

dm³ vs cm³

1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Dividing a cm³ volume by 24.0 (instead of 24 000) makes the moles 1000× too big — always rewrite the volume unit before touching Vₘ.

dm³ mol⁻¹ vs mol dm⁻³ vs dm³

Vₘ = dm³ per mol (conversion factor); c = mol per dm³ (concentration); V = dm³ (a volume). CP 2015 caught the trio being swapped: the unit of the asked quantity fixes the unit of the answer.

Same volume vs same mass (1 mol)

1 mol of any gas: same volume at same T & P, different mass (molar mass differs). “1 mol of gas X and 1 mol of gas Y have the same mass” reverses the two columns.

Gas-only rule

The molar volume applies to gases. Liquids and solids keep their own densities — never write V = n × 24.0 for water, metals or solutions as such.

Excess vs yield

Pouring in more excess reagent after the limiting one is spent produces zero extra gas; a graph that keeps rising with “more acid” is wrong — plateau at the limiting-reagent volume (CP 2013).

1 · Definitions to state word-perfect (Engines A–D)▾
TermExam wording
Rate of reactionThe change in quantity of a reactant or product per unit time.

Biological catalystAn enzyme — a catalyst produced by living systems that speeds up the reactions occurring in them (awareness level, IX (b)).
