Giant Covalent Substances — Diamond, Graphite & Quartz

Topic II (h): diamond, graphite and quartz are all giant covalent substances — every atom is joined to its neighbours by strong covalent bonds, so there are no molecules at all. Build each structure and find out why they behave so differently. (i) compares them with giant ionic, simple molecular and giant metallic substances.

Bond counting & the energy bill

Tool 1 — count the bonds, then explain the property

Tool 2 — the same comparison done with bond enthalpies

Use mean bond enthalpies to compare the energy needed to break one mole of bonds in each structure. C–C = 348 kJ mol⁻¹. Keep the source of a C–C value consistent: literature quotes about 348 kJ mol⁻¹ for diamond and about 356 kJ mol⁻¹ for the in-plane bond in graphite, but they are not different bonds — the quoted value depends on how the measurement is defined. Never build an answer on the difference between two quoted numbers. This tool is here so you can see the arithmetic behind "a lot of energy is needed" — mean bond enthalpies are Unit 2 material, so treat the numbers as enrichment.

Step 1 — worked example (learn the SHAPE of the answer)

Every structure-property question in Topic II follows this pattern: name the structure, say what must be broken, then link to the property. Learn the shape here first.

Explain why diamond has a very high melting point and does not conduct electricity. (3)

1 — name the structure type Diamond is a GIANT COVALENT substance: each carbon atom is joined to four others by strong covalent bonds, and the bonding extends throughout the whole structure. There are no molecules. 2 — say which bonds must be broken, and how much energy that needs Melting requires breaking a very large number of strong covalent bonds, so a very large amount of energy is needed → the melting point is extremely high (about 3550 °C). 3 — explain the conductivity SEPARATELY, from the electrons Each carbon uses ALL FOUR outer-shell electrons in bonding, so there are no delocalised electrons and no mobile charge carriers → diamond does not conduct electricity in any state. Two properties, two separate chains of reasoning. Using the melting-point argument to explain conductivity is the most common way to lose a mark here.
