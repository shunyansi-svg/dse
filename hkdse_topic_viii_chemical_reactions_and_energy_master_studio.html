Syllabus Master Overview

Topic VIII Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(c))

Every Students should learn / be able to strand of Chemical Reactions and Energy, with HKEAA watch-outs from mark-scheme language and Candidate Performance reports.

Topic VIII (a) Energy conserved · ΔH = heat at constant p

Energy changes in reactions: conservation of energy, and enthalpy change as heat change at constant pressure

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Every chemical reaction involves an energy change, usually as heat; total energy is conserved — energy released by the reacting system is taken up by the surroundings (and vice versa); it is never created or destroyed.
  • The enthalpy change, ΔH, of a reaction is the heat change measured at constant pressure — be ready to state the condition verbatim (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
  • Explain reactions “in terms of the concept of conservation of energy”: temperature rise of the surroundings ⇒ energy left the system; temperature fall ⇒ energy entered it.
  • Units and sign: ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹, reported with its + or − sign (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B); “deriving the relation” between heat change and enthalpy change is not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VIII (a)) — recognise and use the relation only.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The condition word is pressure, not temperature: “enthalpy change is the heat change at constant pressure” (CP 2016 Paper 1B context). Do not say energy is “used up” or “lost” by a reaction — the register is transferred / released / absorbed, energy being conserved. A mark-scheme line worth imitating: “q = 27.5 × 4.3 × (25.8 − 20.2) = 662.2 J” — substitution, then answer, then unit.

Topic VIII (a) Exo/endo · bonds · diagrams

Exothermic and endothermic reactions in terms of bond breaking/forming, explained diagrammatically via enthalpy change

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Bond breaking is endothermic (energy is absorbed to pull atoms apart) and bond forming is exothermic (energy is released) — both always happen; the balance decides the sign of ΔH (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0, products lower in enthalpy than reactants, surroundings get hotter. Endothermic: ΔH > 0, products higher, surroundings get colder.
  • Drawing skill asked by the syllabus is the enthalpy level diagram: reactant and product levels labelled, ΔH arrow between them, states on each line — not a reaction profile with activation energy (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
  • Everyday anchors (syllabus STSE): hand-warmers and self-heating cans exothermic; cold-packs endothermic; fuels chosen for efficient thermal-energy release.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The recorded inversions are lethal: “energy is released in bond breaking” and “bond forming is larger” without saying which process releases / absorbs — half-answers get capped (CP 2015 Paper 1B context). Heat is “given out”, but the mark is earned by “ΔH is negative” — two registers, one sign check. “Explain diagrammatically” names the diagram: draw exactly what the verb asks (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).

Topic VIII (b) ΔHr · ΔHf° · ΔHc° · ΔHneut°

Standard enthalpy changes: explain and use reaction, formation, combustion and neutralisation terms under standard conditions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • ΔHf° — enthalpy change when one mole of a compound forms from its elements under standard conditions (ΔHf° of an element = 0). ΔHc° — complete combustion of one mole of substance in excess oxygen. ΔHneut° — per one mole of water formed by acid + alkali. Each “under standard conditions”.
  • Standard conditions: about 101.3 kPa (1 atm) and 25 °C (298.15 K), elements in their standard states, solutions at 1 mol dm⁻³ — if a question supplies its own conventions, use the question's.
  • Pair every value with a balanced equation carrying state symbols: “the chemical equations given should include the correct state symbols” is MS wording (CP 2014 Q6; CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
  • Which term is it? Decide from the sentence: from its elements → formation; burnt in oxygen → combustion; H⁺ + OH⁻ → water → neutralisation; else plain reaction enthalpy.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Definitions are marked element by element: examiners recorded combustion answers losing the definition for omitting any of “enthalpy change involved / one mole of substance / complete combustion / under standard conditions” (CP 2014 Q6). “Per mole” is load-bearing everywhere — decomposition ΔH had to be “per mole of KHCO₃” (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B). Combustion water is H₂O(l); swap in (g) and the equation no longer matches the ΔHc° value.

Topic VIII (b) Simple calorimetry

Carry out experimental determination of enthalpy changes by the simple calorimetric method — and calculate ΔH from the results

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Two syllabus practicals: acid–base neutralisation (polystyrene cup + lid, stir, max temperature) and combustion of an alcohol (spirit burner under a copper can, draught shield, weigh burner before/after).
  • Calculation chain: $q = mc\Delta T$ (assume c = water's 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ unless the question gives its own; a temperature difference in °C equals one in K) → J ÷ 1000 → $\Delta H = \pm q \div n$ per mole of the right substance.
  • Listed limitations the MS accepts verbatim: “Not performing the experiment in standard conditions”, “Heat transfer with the surroundings”, “The heat capacity of the container was neglected”.
  • Error direction: heat loss shrinks |ΔT|, so the measured ΔH is less negative (or less positive) than literature — always name the direction of the bias, and justify any exclusion of a reagent route (insoluble CaSO₄ coats CaCO₃ (CP 2016 Paper 1B context)).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Mass traps on record: a “wrong mass of the reacting mixture” in the calculation (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B) — use the mass of what is heated (water/solution), never the fuel's mass. Bomb-calorimeter principle and operation are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VIII (a)) — simple cup calorimetry is what gets examined. “The unit must be correct” is repeated MS emphasis: J→kJ before dividing, kJ mol⁻¹ at the end.

Topic VIII (c) Hess's law & cycles

Hess's law: construct enthalpy change cycles and find the changes that cannot be determined directly by experiment

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Statement: the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whether it takes place in one step or several steps, provided the initial and final conditions are the same — a consequence of energy conservation.
  • Use it because some ΔH values are not reachable directly: forming MgCO₃ from Mg + C + O₂ is “very difficult” (MS wording) and burning carbon gives a CO/CO₂ mixture, so ΔHf°[CO(g)] needs an indirect route (CP 2013 Q.15; CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
  • Book-keeping rules: reverse an equation ⇒ flip the sign; multiply by n ⇒ multiply ΔH by n; add signed step values line by line — “step marks for each line” (CP 2015 Paper 1B context coefficient-‘2’ slip).
  • Two table routes worth having: $\Delta H_\text{r} = \sum \Delta H_\text{f}(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_\text{f}(\text{reactants})$ and the combustion version $\Delta H_\text{r} = \sum \Delta H_\text{c}(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta H_\text{c}(\text{products})$.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Many candidates were not able to construct an enthalpy change cycle based on Hess's law” (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B) — draw the target substances first, then fit the given steps around them. If your absolute values are wrong but the arithmetic is consistent, the MS “deducts 1 mark only”: show every line. Sign, value and unit carry separate marks — a perfect cycle with a missing sign is a lost mark (CP 2014 Q6).

VIII (a)

Energy is conserved — the system/surroundings ledger

$$E_\text{system} + E_\text{surroundings} = \text{constant}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Chemical reactions are always accompanied by energy changes, most often appearing as heat; the energy may take other forms (light, electricity) but the total is never created or destroyed.
  • Treat every reaction as a transfer between the reacting system and the surroundings: heat released by the system warms the surroundings by the same amount, and vice versa.
  • Explain observations with the ledger: temperature of the mixture rises ⇒ energy flowed out of the chemicals; falls ⇒ energy flowed in.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never write that energy is “used up”, “lost” or “created” by a reaction — the verbs that score are released / absorbed / transferred / conserved.
  • “In terms of the concept of conservation of energy” is the syllabus phrasing — quote it back inside the answer sentence (CP 2012 general comments flag weak energy-calculation explanations generally).
VIII (a)

Enthalpy change, ΔH, = heat change at constant pressure

$$\Delta H = q_p \quad (\text{heat change at constant pressure})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • At constant pressure (open beaker, polystyrene cup, atmospheric conditions) the heat change of a reaction is its enthalpy change — recognise and use this; the derivation is not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VIII (a)).
  • ΔH carries the reaction's identity: it belongs to the equation as written — states and coefficients included.
  • Units: kJ mol⁻¹ (per mole as specified by the equation or definition in play).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Only a very small number of candidates were able to state 'constant pressure' as the required condition” (CP 2016 Paper 1B context) — two words, one guaranteed mark.
  • Do not write “constant temperature” or “in a sealed container”: the pressure condition is the examinable one.
VIII (a)

Exothermic reactions — heat released, ΔH negative

$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{(g)} + 5\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 3\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \quad \Delta H < 0$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition: a reaction that releases heat to the surroundings; the surroundings' temperature rises; enthalpy of products is lower than that of reactants; $\Delta H$ is negative.
  • Combustion, neutralisation, metal + acid, thermite-type and slow oxidation (rusting in a hand-warmer) are the familiar exothermic families.
  • Word it in pairs: “heat is given out and ΔH < 0” — one sentence, both registers.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Sign errors on enthalpy terms were explicitly recorded in a Hess calculation (CP 2014 Q6 “incorrect +/− signs”): exothermic ⇒ the minus belongs to ΔH, never to the quoted “heat released = 57 kJ”-style magnitude.
  • “The reaction gives out heat, so ΔH is positive” is the classic self-contradiction; examiners award nothing for it (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B — omitted '+' on the endothermic side of the same trap).
VIII (a)

Endothermic reactions — heat absorbed, ΔH positive

$$2\text{NaHCO}_3\text{(s)} \to \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} \quad \Delta H > 0$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition: a reaction that absorbs heat from the surroundings; the surroundings cool; products sit higher in enthalpy; $\Delta H$ is positive — and you must write the “+” when a sign is expected (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).
  • Recognise the tell: thermal decomposition (baking-powder style carbonates), photosynthesis-type gas building, dissolving a cold-pack salt — the mixture gets cold yet the reaction still proceeds.
  • Endothermic ≠ “won't happen”: it needs a continuous energy input to keep going at the rate observed.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Many candidates were not able to recognise that reaction (1) is endothermic. They also omitted the '+' sign” — recognition plus sign, one mark each (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).
  • A temperature fall belongs to the surroundings (the water/solution you measure); the system has gained enthalpy — keep the two objects of the sentence straight.
VIII (a)

Enthalpy level diagrams — the drawing this strand asks for

$$\text{exothermic: } \Delta H = H_\text{products} - H_\text{reactants} < 0 \qquad \text{endo: } \Delta H > 0$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Two horizontal levels labelled reactants and products (with state symbols), vertical axis enthalpy / energy, H, one arrowhead ΔH joining the two levels, its magnitude proportional to the data given.
  • Exothermic: products below reactants, ΔH arrow pointing down, written with “−”. Endothermic: products above, arrow pointing up, “+”.
  • The syllabus verb is “explain diagrammatically … in terms of enthalpy change” — the level diagram is the tool (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Recorded misfire: “wrongly drew a reaction profile or an energy cycle instead of an enthalpy level diagram” — draw the one named, or draw the level diagram as well (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
  • No activation-energy hump belongs on a level diagram; adding one wastes ink and can contradict the ΔH arrow's endpoints.
VIII (a)

Bond breaking is endothermic

$$\text{H–H(g)} \xrightarrow{\text{energy absorbed}} \text{H(g)} + \text{H(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • To separate bonded atoms you must overcome the attractive force between them — that takes energy in: bond breaking is always endothermic, no exceptions.
  • Stronger bonds need more energy to break (compare H–H vs Cl–Cl reasoning) — the phrase is “energy required to break”, not “energy stored in the bond”.
  • Say which bonds and in which species: “the H–H bonds and O=O bonds in the reactant molecules are broken” (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The inverted model is on record: “energy is released in bond breaking” (CP 2015 Paper 1B context) — it caps the whole explanation at zero, because the follow-on comparison then contradicts itself.
  • Do not import the phrase into physical changes: when water boils it is the intermolecular forces that weaken apart, not the O–H bonds (boundary with Topic VI).
VIII (a)

Bond forming is exothermic

$$2\text{H(g)} + \text{O(g)} \to \text{H–O–H(g)} \;:\; \text{energy released}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Atoms reaching a bonded arrangement fall to lower potential energy; the difference is given out as heat — bond making is always exothermic.
  • More (and stronger) bonds made than broken ⇒ net release ⇒ exothermic; the converse ⇒ endothermic.
  • State the products' bonds specifically: “two new O–H bonds form in each water molecule” — naming the bond beats gesturing at the formula (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Examiners capped answers that said only “bond forming is larger than bond breaking” without saying which releases and which absorbs energy — supply the verbs with each process (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
  • Write bond types with correct multiplicities: O=O and C=O double bonds, not “O–O” and “C–O” — a wrong bond list loses the mark even when the energy logic is right (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
VIII (a)

The net balance decides ΔH — both processes always occur

$$\Delta H \sim E_\text{absorbed(breaking)} - E_\text{released(forming)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Every reaction breaks reactant bonds and forms product bonds — exo/endo is the net result, never “breaking only” or “forming only”.
  • Exothermic: energy released forming new bonds is greater than energy absorbed breaking old ones; endothermic: it is less.
  • Here the comparison stays qualitative: tabulated mean bond enthalpy arithmetic is beyond this topic's columns (see the out-of-scope box).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Model answer shape (MS register): “energy is absorbed to break bonds in H₂ and O₂; more energy is released when the O–H bonds in H₂O form; the reaction is exothermic, ΔH < 0” — four clauses, each examinable (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
  • Giving formulae instead of bonds (“CO₂ and H₂O form”) answers a different question — the mark asks which bonds form (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).

Energy in daily life — warmers, packs, fuels and the atmosphere

$$\text{Fe(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{iron oxide: exothermic (hand-warmer)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Named applications from the Guide: hand-warmers and physiotherapy heat-packs (slow iron oxidation, exothermic), cold-packs (dissolving the salt absorbs heat, endothermic), self-heating coffee/lunch boxes (e.g. CaO + H₂O), burning fuels for efficient thermal energy release.

The Energy Ladder — break up, fall down, net result

Strand (a) is one model read three ways. Climb the first rung, ride the second, and the ΔH sign writes itself.

Rung 1 · Break bonds — climb (endothermic)

Atoms must be pulled apart against their mutual attraction: energy is absorbed. Up the ladder, always: $\text{A–B} \to \text{A} + \text{B}$ costs heat.

Rung 2 · Make bonds — fall (exothermic)

New arrangements let atoms drop to lower potential energy; the difference is released as heat. Down the ladder, always.

Rung 3 · Net = released more than absorbed

Exothermic: surroundings warm, products sit lower on the level diagram, $\Delta H < 0$ — write the “−”.

Rung 3′ · Net = absorbed more than released

Endothermic: surroundings cool, products sit higher, $\Delta H > 0$ — write the “+” (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).

The two level diagrams, side by side

Levels labelled with state symbols; the ΔH arrow spans reactant level → product level. No humps, no catalyst — that is a reaction profile (Topic IX/XIII language) (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).

Multi-blank Quiz — Energy Changes in Reactions

1. In any chemical reaction energy is conserved, not created: heat released by the reacting system is taken up by the surroundings.
2. The enthalpy change, ΔH, of a reaction is the heat change measured at constant pressure — a condition only a few candidates stated (CP 2016 Paper 1B context); deriving that relation is not expected.
3. An exothermic reaction releases heat: the surroundings get hotter, the products lie lower in enthalpy than the reactants, and ΔH carries the − (negative) sign.
4. When the mixture in a cold-pack gets colder, the process is endothermic: energy enters the system from the surroundings, products lie higher, and ΔH must be written with a “+” sign — examiners recorded candidates omitting it (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).
5. Bond breaking is endothermic (energy absorbed to overcome attraction); bond forming is exothermic (energy released) — the two recorded inversions both fail on these verbs (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
6. A reaction is exothermic overall when the energy released in bond forming is greater than the energy absorbed in bond breaking — name both processes or the mark is capped.
7. In the hydrogen–oxygen reaction, saying only “H₂O forms” is not enough: you must say the H–H and O=O bonds break and the O–H bonds form — “CO₂ and H₂O” written as bond types “O–O and C–O” lost marks too (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
8. On an enthalpy level diagram the ΔH arrow runs from the reactants level to the products level, pointing downward for exothermic; drawing a reaction profile instead of the level diagram asked for is a recorded misfire (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
9. Hand-warmers exploit the exothermic slow oxidation of iron; a self-heating can uses quicklime plus water; burning fuels releases CO₂, linking the topic to global warming in the Guide's STSE notes.
10. Natural gas is preferred to coal for electricity because it burns more cleanly — less SO₂ (and less CO₂ per kJ), no ash — reasons the exam wanted spelled out, since fewer than half managed it (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Energy Changes

Bond breaking ABSORBS vs “releases stored energy”

Breaking is always endothermic — you fight the attraction. “Energy is released when bonds break” was a recorded inversion that guts the whole explanation (CP 2015 Paper 1B context). Reserves of chemical energy refer to the net of break-vs-make, never to a bond's “stored” energy being freed by snapping it.

“Heat given out” vs “ΔH is negative”

One fact, two registers. Heat given out by the system = heat received by the surroundings (they get hotter); the system's ΔH carries “−”. Say both halves, and keep the sign on ΔH, not on “heat”.

Enthalpy level diagram vs reaction profile vs energy cycle

Level diagram = two labelled levels + ΔH arrow (this strand). Profile = curve with activation hump (rate language, Topic IX/XIII). Cycle = Hess box-diagram (strand c). Candidates drew the wrong one when the stem named it clearly (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).

ΔH sign vs diagram direction

Products below reactants ⇔ ΔH “−” ⇔ exothermic — the three must agree. A diagram with products lower but ΔH “+” self-destructs; sign slips on enthalpy terms were recorded in calculations too (CP 2014 Q6).

Surroundings warm up vs “the reaction gets hot”

Temperature is a property of the water/solution you measure; the reaction either supplies or demands heat. “The reaction's temperature rose” is sloppy register; “the temperature of the surroundings rose, so the reaction is exothermic” is the mark-laden sentence.

Endothermic vs “needs a burner”

Needing heat to start says nothing about ΔH (methane needs a spark yet is fiercely exothermic). Classify by the sustained heat flow: continuous absorption ⇒ endothermic, ΔH > 0 with the “+” written (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).

Breaking bonds in reactions vs breaking bonds on boiling

Boiling water overcomes intermolecular forces; the O–H covalent bonds survive (that is Topic VI's boundary — recorded in 2012 CP on boiling oxygen). Chemical change is the one that rearranges bonds and carries a ΔH.

VIII (b)

Enthalpy change of reaction, ΔH

$$\text{N}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{NH}_3\text{(g)} \quad \Delta H = -92\;\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The heat change accompanying a stated reaction at constant pressure, as the equation is written: here ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹ means per 2 mol NH₃ formed (i.e. per mole of reaction).
  • ΔH is quoted with the equation — double the coefficients and the number doubles; the two must travel together.
  • Absolute enthalpy H is unmeasurable: every definition speaks of a change.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Definitions start with “the enthalpy change when …” — writing “heat energy” or “energy” alone fails the first element the MS checks (CP 2014 Q6).
  • “per mole of KHC03”-style slips show the object of the mole must be named — of the substance, of water formed, of the reaction as written (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).
VIII (b)

Standard conditions — the “°” in ΔH°

$$101.3\;\text{kPa} \;(1\;\text{atm}),\quad 25\,{}^{\circ}\text{C} \;(298.15\;\text{K}),\quad 1\;\text{mol dm}^{-3}\;(\text{solutions})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Standard conditions: pressure about 101.3 kPa (1 atm), temperature 25 °C (298.15 K) — normally assumed — reactants and products in their standard states (pure physical form at that pressure), solutions at 1 mol dm⁻³.
  • Elements take their usual form: Na(s), Br₂(l), Hg(l), O₂(g), C(graphite) — the reference state for formation values.
  • “Standard enthalpy change” = enthalpy change under standard conditions — the phrase must appear in the definition (CP 2014 Q6).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not confuse standard conditions (definition) with constant pressure (why heat change counts as ΔH at all): both phrases are examinable, both were missed — the first (CP 2014 Q6), the second (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
  • If the question states its own conditions, honour them; “at room temperature and pressure” alone does not satisfy “standard conditions” in a definition.
VIII (b)

Standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔHf°

$$\text{Mg(s)} + \text{C(s,graphite)} + \tfrac{3}{2}\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{MgCO}_3\text{(s)} \quad \Delta H_\text{f}^{\circ}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition: the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements, under standard conditions.
  • Every symbol on the left must be an element in its standard state; coefficients may be fractional ($\tfrac{3}{2}\text{O}_2$) so that exactly one mole of product forms.
  • $\Delta H_\text{f}^{\circ} = 0$ for an element in its standard state — the reference level of the whole table.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Some ΔHf° cannot be measured directly — carbon burning gives a mixture, so ΔHf°[CO(g)] needs an indirect route, and that sentence was the MC discriminator (CP 2013 Q.15).
  • “Formed from its elements” — if the equation's left side contains a compound, the value is not ΔHf° however else it looks.
VIII (b)

Standard enthalpy change of combustion, ΔHc°

$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH(l)} + 3\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \quad \Delta H_\text{c}^{\circ} \approx -1367\;\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition, four checked elements: the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance undergoes complete combustion in excess oxygen, under standard conditions (CP 2014 Q6).
  • “Complete combustion” ⇒ $\text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ — the water is liquid, not steam; CO formation is incomplete.
  • ΔHc° values are always negative (exothermic); typical data value for ethanol −1367 kJ mol⁻¹ — always use the number printed in the question.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Examiners' record: answers “failed to give a complete answer including ‘enthalpy change involved’, ‘one mole of substance’, ‘complete combustion’ and ‘under standard conditions’” — learn the definition as a four-item checklist (CP 2014 Q6).
  • The accompanying equation lost marks for missing state symbols (CP 2014 Q6); per mole means per mole of the fuel, never of oxygen.
VIII (b)

Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation, ΔHneut°

$$\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \quad \Delta H_\text{neut}^{\circ} \approx -57\;\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Definition: the enthalpy change when one mole of water is formed from the reaction between an acid and a base (alkali), under standard conditions.
  • Strong acid + strong alkali give almost the same value (about −57 to −58 kJ mol⁻¹ in DSE data) because the same net ionic reaction occurs: $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$.
  • Weak acids (ethanoic) absorb extra energy to ionise ⇒ less exothermic than the strong–strong value.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • ΔHneut° is per mole of water, so sulphuric acid's two H⁺ matter: 1 mol H₂SO₄ neutralised gives 2 mol water and twice the heat — divide by n(H₂O), not n(acid).
  • “Explain why all strong acid–strong alkali pairs give similar values” ⇒ name the identical net ionic equation; “why ethanoic is smaller” ⇒ ionisation consumes energy.
VIII (b)

“Per mole of …” — the load-bearing words of every definition

🎯 Learning Goals
  • Each definition anchors its number to a different “one mole”: product (formation), fuel (combustion), water (neutralisation), reaction as written (plain ΔH).
  • A calorimetry result must be divided by the moles of that object — the recorded slip: decomposition ΔH not expressed “per mole of KHCO₃” (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).
  • If the equation doubles the substance, the ΔH doubles — scaling discipline before any arithmetic.
💡 Examiner Tips

State symbols are part of the mark, not decoration

Which term is this? The naming decider

🎯 Learning Goals

  • Same skill in reverse: given a term, write the defining equation with states — the exam's two-mark handshake.

Definition Anatomy — four slots, every standard term

Fill the same four slots and any enthalpy definition writes itself. Each empty slot is a mark the examiners recorded losing (CP 2014 Q6).

Slot 1 · The quantity

“The enthalpy change …” — never “energy” or “heat” alone; heat is what you measure, ΔH is what you report at constant pressure.

Slot 2 · The process + its one mole

Slot 3 · The conditions

Slot 4 · The equation

Balanced, with state symbols, exactly one mole of the anchoring substance — the value is meaningless off its own equation (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).

Multi-blank Quiz — Definitions that carry full marks

1. The standard enthalpy change of formation is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements, under standard conditions.
2. Standard conditions are commonly taken as about 101.3 kPa (1 atm) and 25 °C (298.15 K), with elements in their standard states and solutions at 1 mol dm⁻³.
3. The combustion definition needs all four elements checked by the MS: “enthalpy change”, “one mole of substance”, “complete combustion” and “under standard conditions” (CP 2014 Q6).
4. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon gives $\text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$ and H₂O(l) — liquid water, not steam; burning with limited air yields CO — incomplete, which is why ΔHf°[CO(g)] can't be measured by burning carbon directly (CP 2013 Q.15).
5. ΔHf° of an element in its standard state is zero — the reference level — while its ΔHc° (e.g. C → CO₂) is decidedly not zero.
6. Neutralisation is reported per mole of water formed; the net ionic equation is $\text{H}^{+}\text{(aq)} + \text{OH}^{-}\text{(aq)} \to$ H₂O(l), and strong–strong values cluster near −57 kJ mol⁻¹.
7. Ethanoic acid's heat of neutralisation is less exothermic than HCl's because energy is absorbed as the weak acid ionises.
8. Write every energetics equation balanced, with state symbols — examiners called their omission “the most common mistake” (CP 2016 Paper 1B context), and $\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ versus $\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$ differ by about 44 kJ mol⁻¹ of vaporisation heat.
9. “Enthalpy change when one mole of methane burns in oxygen” misses standard conditions; the plain term enthalpy change of combustion is still correct, but standard enthalpy change of combustion (ΔHc°) is not.
10. ΔH is reported in kJ mol⁻¹ — the MS repeats that “the unit must be correct” — and a sign is a separate mark from the value: 1 mark sign + 1 mark value + 1 mark unit (CP 2014 Q6).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Enthalpy Definitions

Formation vs combustion vs neutralisation — “per mole of what?”

Compound formed / substance burnt / water formed. Swapping the object swaps the term and the arithmetic; the anchoring object is the definition's spine (CP 2014 Q6).

Enthalpy change vs enthalpy

We measure changes (heat at constant pressure), never absolute H. A definition beginning “the heat content of …” cannot be marked right — start every one with “the enthalpy change when …”.

Complete vs incomplete combustion

ΔHc° is defined for complete combustion to CO₂ and H₂O(l). Soot, CO or steam products make the process a different (smaller-magnitude) reaction — and burning C to a CO/CO₂ mixture is exactly why CO's ΔHf° needs Hess (CP 2013 Q.15).

“Standard conditions” vs “constant pressure”

Standard conditions = 101.3 kPa, 25 °C, 1 mol dm⁻³ (definition slot 3). Constant pressure = why heat change equals ΔH at all (strand a statement). Both phrases must be available separately (CP 2014 Q6; CP 2016 Paper 1B context).

ΔHneut° per mole of acid vs per mole of water

H₂SO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) makes two moles of water: the heat measured is twice ΔHneut°. Divide by n(H₂O formed) — never reflexively by n(acid).

“All neutralisations are −57 kJ mol⁻¹” vs the weak-acid truth

Only strong acid + strong alkali match ≈ −57 to −58. Weak acid or weak base spend part of the heat on ionisation — smaller magnitude, and the explanation is the mark.

Value without equation vs value on its equation

“ΔHc°[ethanol] = −1367 kJ mol⁻¹” is only meaningful beside its balanced equation with states; double the equation, double the number. State-symbol omissions were twice recorded as the commonest error (CP 2014 Q6; CP 2016 Paper 1B context).

The idea: trap the heat in a known mass, measure ΔT

🎯 Learning Goals

Measuring ΔT properly — the protocol that protects the maximum

$$\Delta T = T_\text{max} - T_\text{initial}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Record the initial temperature of both reactants (they should match); mix quickly; fit the lid; stir gently with the thermometer; read the highest temperature reached, at the moment mixing is complete — not a later cooling value.
  • For two solutions, record both temperatures first and take the mean as Tinitial if they differ.
  • Repeat and average concordant runs; a single throwaway trial is not a result (Guide: accept results within tolerance limits).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Suggest the possible error” items (about 70% could, (CP 2016 Paper 1B context)) want a named, physical loss — “heat lost to surroundings / absorbed by the cup” — not “human error” or “wrong thermometer”.
  • Answers must use the maximum temperature for exothermic runs: reading the thermometer after the mixture has cooled shrinks ΔT and the whole |ΔH| with it.

Neutralisation experiment — polystyrene cup, measured volumes, one ΔT

💡 Examiner Tips

Combustion of an alcohol — spirit burner under a copper can

Which mass? Which ΔT? — the substitution the mark is on

Error sources and the direction they push the answer

The Calorimetry Chain — four gates, every number question

Every simple-calorimetry calculation walks the same four gates in order. Skip a gate, lose its mark.

Gate 1 · Read the heat bath

Choose the mass that actually changes temperature: water alone (combustion) or total mixture (solution work). ΔT = |final − initial|; the difference is the same in K and °C.

Gate 2 · q = mcΔT

One substitution line, answer in J. Use the question's c; otherwise water's 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Label the data you assume.

Gate 3 · Moles of the anchor

n = m ÷ M of what the definition says “one mole of”: fuel burnt, compound made, water formed, stated solid. For solutions n = cV.

Gate 4 · Sign, divide, unit

J → kJ (× 10⁻³); ΔH = ±q ÷ n; “−” if the bath warmed, “+” if it cooled; write kJ mol⁻¹. Three marks sit in this gate alone (MS bank).

Multi-blank Quiz — Simple Calorimetry

7. In the alcohol-combustion practical the fuel's amount comes from weighing the burner before and after; soot on the can means incomplete combustion, pushing |ΔHc°| too low.
8. The decomposition of potassium hydrogen carbonate got cold: candidates had to recognise it as endothermic, keep the “+” sign, and express the value per mole of KHCO₃ (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Calorimetry Numbers

q in joules vs ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹

q = mcΔT with c in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ yields J. The ×10⁻³ before (or inside) the division is a visible step — “8400 J = 8.4 kJ” on its own line — or “the unit must be correct” punishes you (MS bank).

°C ↔ K for ΔT vs for a reading

A difference of 20 °C is 20 K — no conversion. An absolute reading needs +273.15. Never add 273 to a ΔT; that “correction” invents a 273-kJ ghost in the chain.

Mass of fuel vs mass of water

The fuel's mass belongs to n = Δm ÷ M, never to q = mcΔT; the water's mass belongs to q only. Swapping them is the recorded “wrong mass of the reacting mixture” family (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).

Tmax vs “the temperature at the end”

After mixing, the reading peaks and falls. The exothermic ΔT uses the maximum; quoting a cooled-down final temperature quietly under-reports |ΔH| — the bias then looks “random” when it is systematic.

“Heat loss makes ΔH too small” — small in which sense?

Precise wording: less negative (−49 vs −57) for exothermic, less positive for endothermic; “smaller” alone can mean magnitude (right) or position on the number line (wrong). Prefer “smaller in magnitude”.

ΔH sign from ΔT vs ΔH sign from the data table

Experiment: the bath warming means the reaction is exothermic ⇒ ΔH “−” (forced by observation). Table work: take the printed values' signs as they are. Mixing the two conventions produced the recorded +/− slips (CP 2014 Q6; CP 2015 Paper 1B context).

Assume everything dissolves vs check the limiting reagent

In excess-solid runs the acid (or water's solubility, or the coating of CaSO₄ (CP 2016 Paper 1B context)) sets n — verify which species truly finishes reacting before dividing by any moles.

VIII (c)

Hess's law — the statement worth memorising word-perfect

$$\Delta H_\text{direct} = \Delta H_\text{route\,1} + \Delta H_\text{route\,2} + \cdots$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Statement: the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same, whether the reaction takes place in one step or in several steps, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
  • It is conservation of energy in book-keeping form: no path can create or destroy enthalpy if it starts and ends at the same substances in the same states.
  • Because ΔH does not depend on route, an unmeasurable step can be recovered from measurable ones.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “State Hess's law” = two clauses: same total ΔH + one or several steps (add “same initial and final conditions” for safety).
  • The law is about enthalpy changes, not “heat” or “energy” generally — keep the examinable noun.
VIII (c)

Why indirect methods exist — the two classic excuses

$$2\text{C(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{CO(g)}\;? \qquad \text{Mg(s)} + \text{C(s)} + \tfrac{3}{2}\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{MgCO}_3\text{(s)}\;?$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Messy product: burning carbon gives a mixture of CO and CO₂, never pure CO — so ΔHf°[CO(g)] cannot be measured directly (CP 2013 Q.15).
  • Reaction that will not cooperate: MS wording — “It is very difficult for Mg(s), C(s) and O₂(g) to react directly to form MgCO₃(s)” — a direct calorimetry run on three solids cannot control which moles reacted.
  • A third class: reactions that are too slow, or accompanied by side changes — all legitimate “indirect method needed” explanations (CP 2016 Paper 1B context: only about one third explained it).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Answer “why can't ΔHf°[X] be determined directly?” by naming the practical obstacle (mixture / incompleteness / uncontrolled extent), not by saying “Hess's law is easier”.
  • For CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ the obstacle is different and specific: insoluble CaSO₄ coats the solid and stops the reaction (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
VIII (c)

Book-keeping rules — flip the sign, scale the number

$$\text{reverse} \Rightarrow \Delta H \to -\Delta H \qquad \times\, n \Rightarrow \Delta H \to n\,\Delta H$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Only two operations are legal, and ΔH obeys the equation: reverse the equation ⇒ same number, opposite sign; multiply the equation ⇒ multiply ΔH by the same factor.
  • Never “cancel the minus signs inside a ΔH term” — the sign is data (ΔHf° values arrive negative; subtracting them is signed arithmetic).
  • Substances may be eliminated only when an equation supplies them where another consumes them — with equal moles after scaling.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The recorded slip: “missed the coefficient ‘2’ in the term for ΔHf°[H₂O(l)]” when 2 mol water appeared on the target side (CP 2015 Paper 1B context) — if the equation doubles, the number doubles.
  • Wrong +/− on enthalpy terms cost marks in a Hess calculation exactly as in a definition one (CP 2014 Q6); carry signs through every line, and rewrite subtractions as additions of the opposite.
VIII (c)

Building a two-step cycle — the MS's own MgCO₃ worked example

$$\Delta H_\text{f}^{\circ}[\text{MgCO}_3\text{(s)}]:\quad y - 50 = -467 - 394 - 286 \;\Rightarrow\; y = -1097\;\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Given steps (MS bank): MgCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g), ΔH₁ = −50; Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g), ΔH₂ = −467; C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₃ = −394; H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l), ΔH₄ = −286.
  • Both routes from Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) + C(s) + 3/2 O₂(g) end at MgSO₄(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l): direct leg y + step 1-reversed equals the three-step leg — hence y − 50 = −467 − 394 − 286.
  • Method: write targets as dots, connect given arrows and the unknown, then read one equality from start line to finish line.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Not able to construct an enthalpy change cycle” (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B) — draw the cycle before the algebra and label every arrow with its ΔH and states; the picture earns the method marks.
  • Per-mole care: −1097 kJ mol⁻¹ is per mole of MgCO₃(s) — the anchor of the formation definition (card: Engine B).
VIII (c)

From ΔHf° tables — products minus reactants

$$\Delta H_\text{r}^{\circ} = \sum \Delta H_\text{f}^{\circ}(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_\text{f}^{\circ}(\text{reactants})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Formation data imagine each substance built from elements; the elements cancel, leaving products-minus-reactants — multiply each tabulated value by its stoichiometric coefficient.
  • Elements contribute zero — an explicit 0, not a skipped cell.
  • Worked shape (from MS bank arithmetic): $\Delta H = \tfrac{1}{2}(-1146 - 394 - 286 - (-959 \times 2)) = +46\;\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ — brackets, signs, then the ½ outside.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The classic coefficient slip is 2 mol of $\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ taken once (CP 2015 Paper 1B context) — annotate the equation above each value you substitute.
  • “Products minus reactants” direction is for formation data; swapping the order flips the sign of the whole answer (CP 2014 Q6).
VIII (c)

From ΔHc° tables — reactants minus products

$$\Delta H_\text{r}^{\circ} = \sum \Delta H_\text{c}^{\circ}(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta H_\text{c}^{\circ}(\text{products})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Combustion data burn everything to common ash (CO₂, H₂O); the route that equals the target is “burn the reactants, un-burn the products” — hence reactants minus products, the mirror of the formation rule.
  • Substances that cannot burn (CO₂, H₂O, N₂…) contribute zero as fuels — write the zeros.
  • MS bank worked chain: ΔHc°[CO(g)] = −80 ÷ (7.89/28) = −284 kJ mol⁻¹ (per-mole conversion first!), then ΔHf°[CO(g)] = (−394) − (−284) = −110 kJ mol⁻¹.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Remember which mnemonic fits which table — formation: products − reactants; combustion: reactants − products. A wrong direction with right numbers is a fully-signed wrong answer.
  • Subtracting negatives: “(−394) − (−284)” becomes −394 + 284 = −110 — keep every bracketed sign on the page (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
VIII (c)

Cycle vs level diagram — two honest drawings of one law

$$\text{cycle: boxes + route arrows} \qquad \text{levels: stacked } H \text{ lines} + \Delta H \text{ arrow}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • An enthalpy change cycle lists substances as nodes and given ΔH values as labelled arrows — arrows must point the way the equation runs; a reversed arrow is a reversed sign.
  • An enthalpy level diagram can also carry a multi-step route (three levels, two ΔH arrows spanning them) — useful when a question asks you to read an unknown level gap off the picture.
  • Consistency test: whichever drawing you chose, your algebra must reproduce it — sum of arrows round any closed loop is zero.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • When the stem says “enthalpy level diagram”, drawing a reaction profile or a cycle instead scores nothing for the drawing mark (CP 2013, Paper 1B context) — draw the requested form, add the other beside it if unsure.
  • Label every arrow with sign + unit and every node with state symbols — those labels are marks, not decoration (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
VIII (c)

How Hess marks are awarded — spend your working like the MS

$$1\;\text{sign} + 1\;\text{value} + 1\;\text{unit}; \quad \text{step marks per line}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • MS bank conventions: “Step marks for each line of Hess's law”; “If absolute values wrong but consistent, deduct 1 mark only”; “1 mark for correct sign, 1 mark for value, 1 mark for unit”; “the unit must be correct”.
  • Practical consequence: an earlier arithmetic slip faithfully carried is nearly free; a blank answer sheet is not.
  • Write each scaled equation line (with its flipped sign) before summing — those lines are the step marks.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Endothermic answers keep their “+” (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B); units arrive as kJ mol⁻¹ per the anchor substance — restate it in words.
  • The general comments since 2012 name enthalpy calculations as a whole-paper weakness (CP 2012 general comments; CP 2013 general comments) — clean line-by-line working is the single best remedy.

Multi-blank Quiz — Hess's Law & Cycles

1. Hess's law: the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whether it takes place in one step or in several steps, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
2. ΔHf°[CO(g)] cannot be measured directly because burning carbon produces a mixture of CO and CO₂ — the MC that tested this had key 2C(s) + O₂(g) → 2CO(g) (CP 2013 Q.15).
3. The MS justification for an indirect MgCO₃ determination: “It is very difficult for Mg(s), C(s) and O₂(g) to react directly to form MgCO₃(s)” — the cycle supplies y = −1097 kJ mol⁻¹.
4. When an equation is reversed, ΔH keeps its magnitude but changes sign; when an equation is multiplied by 2, ΔH is multiplied by 2 — the missing “2” on ΔHf°[H₂O(l)] is on the CP record (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
5. Formation tables use ΔHr = ΣΔHf°(products) − ΣΔHf°(reactants); combustion tables use the reverse order — reactants minus products.
6. From the MS chain: ΔHc°[CO(g)] = −80 ÷ (7.89/28) = −284 kJ mol⁻¹, then ΔHf°[CO(g)] = (−394) − (−284) = −110 kJ mol⁻¹ — subtracting a negative means adding its opposite.
7. The mark scheme pays “step marks for each line” of a Hess calculation and, if absolute values are wrong but consistent, “deduct 1 mark only” — so write the lines and never skip the algebra.
8. Asked for an enthalpy level diagram, some candidates arrived with a reaction profile or an energy cycle instead — the drawing mark is form-specific (CP 2013, Paper 1B context).
9. On a cycle diagram each arrow's direction must match the equation that supplies its ΔH, every arrow label carries its sign, and every node carries its state symbols — omissions were “the most common mistake” (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).
10. ΔHf° of elements in standard states is zero — write the zeros when a target equation has O₂ or C on a side; they keep sums honest, e.g. y − 50 = −467 − 394 − 286 (MS bank).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Hess Book-Keeping

Reversing the equation vs “dropping the minus”

A reversed combustion ΔHc° = −284 becomes +284 — flip the sign, keep the number. Candidates were recorded losing the whole sum by mangling +/− on enthalpy terms (CP 2014 Q6; CP 2015 Paper 1B context).

Scaling the equation vs scaling only the picture

If the cycle needs 2 mol H₂O, the term is 2 × ΔHf°[H₂O(l)] — in the algebra, not just in the drawing; the missing coefficient was recorded verbatim (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).

Products − reactants vs reactants − products

Formation data: products first. Combustion data: reactants first. Mixing the two orders returns the right magnitude with the wrong sign — the sign, value, unit triple then all fails (MS bank).

Using a given ΔH twice vs cancelling a shared species

One supplied equation contributes at most one signed term to the sum. Intermediate species (H₂SO₄, MgSO₄ in the MS's MgCO₃ cycle) must appear on both routes to cancel — if a substance cancels, its ΔH has already done its job.

“Missing data” vs ΔHf° = 0 for elements

O₂(g), C(graphite), Hg(l)… have formation enthalpy exactly zero — substitute 0, don't hunt the table or leave a hole that skews the sum.

ΔH of the cycle vs ΔH of the reaction asked

The sum gives ΔH for the target equation as printed. If the question then asks “per mole of X”, divide by X's coefficient — 2 NaHCO₃ decomposing means ΔH ÷ 2 per mole of KHCO₃-style slip check (CP 2012 Q8, Paper 1B).

Adding signed numbers vs adding magnitudes

“−467 − 394 − 286” is −1147, and “(−394) − (−284)” is −110 — every MS line keeps its brackets (MS bank). Magnitude arithmetic is how consistent-error deductions (and lost sign marks) happen (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).

4 · Simple-calorimetry experiment checklist (Engine C)▾
☐ Polystyrene cup in a beaker (neutralisation) or copper can over a spirit burner (combustion) — insulate from below.
☐ Lid on; add reactants quickly, in one go; replace lid immediately.
☐ Stir gently (thermometer/glass rod) for a uniform temperature.
☐ Record initial temperatures, then the maximum (exothermic) — ΔT = Tmax − Tinitial.
☐ Mass = total solution (1 cm³ ≈ 1 g) or water in the can — never the fuel's mass inside q.
☐ c from the question; else 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (water). Label it “Data:”.
☐ Burner + fuel weighed before and after → n(fuel) = Δm ÷ M.
☐ Repeat; average concordant runs; accept results within tolerance (Guide).
☐ List limitations MS-style: not standard conditions · heat transfer with surroundings · container heat capacity neglected.
☐ Finish: ΔH = ±(q ÷ 1000) ÷ n of the anchor substance, unit kJ mol⁻¹, sign from the ΔT direction.

“Energy is released when bonds break”Recorded inversion: breaking absorbs energy; forming releases it — state both verbs (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
“Bond forming is larger than bond breaking” (nothing else)Half-answer capped: add “…so more energy is released than absorbed; the reaction is exothermic” (CP 2015 Paper 1B context).
“The reaction gets hot / has a high temperature”The surroundings get hot; the reaction releases heat. Keep system and surroundings as separate nouns.
“ΔH = heat given out” (no condition)Missing constant pressure — the condition “only a very small number stated” (CP 2016 Paper 1B context).

The wrong ideaExam-true correctionSource
“Breaking bonds releases energy — that’s what fuels store.”Breaking absorbs; forming releases; the reaction's ΔH is the net of the two.CP 2015 Paper 1B context
“Bonds in CO₂ are O–O and C–O single bonds.”Name bonds with multiplicity: C=O double bonds — and say which bonds break/form, not just the formulae.CP 2015 Paper 1B context
“If heat is given out, ΔH = +something.”Heat out ⇒ ΔH negative; heat in ⇒ ΔH positive with the ‘+’ written.CP 2014 Q6; CP 2012 Q8
“Convert the 20 °C rise to 293 K before using it.”A difference of 20 °C is 20 K — q = mcΔT uses the difference directly; converting it is a 273-joule ghost.MS bank q-chain; CP 2012 Q8 (calculation slips)
“Use the fuel's mass in q = mcΔT.”q heats the water/solution; the fuel's mass only gives n = Δm ÷ M later.CP 2012 Q8
“Heat loss makes the value wrong but unpredictable.”It biases predictably: |ΔT| smaller ⇒ measured ΔH less negative than the data-book value — name the direction.CP 2016 Paper 1B context; MS bank error wording
“One more mole of equation — same ΔH number.”ΔH is tied to the equation as written: double the equation, double the kJ; per-mole anchors (KHCO₃!) fix the number reported.CP 2012 Q8; CP 2015 Paper 1B context (×2 term)
“ΔHf° of CO can't be measured because carbon won't burn.”Carbon does burn — but to a mixture of CO and CO₂, so the pure-CO formation step needs an indirect route.CP 2013 Q.15
“Any energy diagram earns the drawing mark.”The stem names the form: enthalpy level diagram here (profiles/cycles answer a different demand).CP 2013 (Paper 1B)
