Topic I overview The world of chemicals

“We are living in a world of chemicals” — classify, separate, test

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Know the terms “element”, “compound” and “mixture”, “physical change” and “chemical change”, “physical property” and “chemical property”, “solvent”, “solute” and “saturated solution” — the overview names all nine verbatim (Part I wording).
  • Use word equations to represent chemical changes — this topic asks for word equations, not balancing.
  • Suggest appropriate methods for the separation of mixtures — evaporation, distillation, crystallisation, filtration — and undertake tests for chemical species.
  • Recognise that chemistry “includes the investigation of possible methods to isolate useful materials in our environment and to analyse them” — the seed of the whole course, harvested again in Topic XV (Part I wording; → Topic XV).
  • Value safe handling and disposal of chemicals and show concern for the limited reserve of natural resources (values & attitudes, verbatim).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

The skills this block demands are exactly where the reports record method confusion: “obtaining an insoluble substance from a mixture” vs “obtaining a soluble compound from a solution” (CP 2014 Q12), and dissolving a solid before testing it (CP 2016 Q9). Examiners also grade hazard precision — “toxic” for bromine, not “harmful”/“irritant” (CP 2016 Q8), and no ‘corrosive’ label for very dilute acids (CP 2015 Q1).

Topic I (a) The atmosphere

Composition of air · separation of oxygen and nitrogen from liquid air · test for oxygen

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State the composition of air by volume: ≈78% nitrogen, ≈21% oxygen, ≈1% other gases (argon ~0.9%, carbon dioxide ~0.04%, variable water vapour) (Part I wording: “composition of air”).
  • Describe the processes involved in fractional distillation of liquid air, and understand the concepts and procedures involved — dust and CO₂/water removed, air liquefied, components separated by boiling point (N₂ boils off first) (Part I wording).
  • Demonstrate how to carry out a test for oxygen: a glowing splint relights in the gas (Part I wording).
  • Know the uses of the separated gases (medicinal oxygen, inert nitrogen, noble-gas lamps) — “applications of the products obtained from fractional distillation of liquid air” (suggested activities).
  • Treat air as a mixture: physically separable, not chemically combined.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Fractional distillation separates only what air already contains: candidates who “wrongly stated that hydrogen can be obtained from air by fractional distillation” lost the paper’s logic mark (CP 2013), and just below half could not “suggest nitrogen can be obtained from fractional distillation of liquefied air” (CP 2016 Paper 2 context). Gas-to-use matching is graded: “Many candidates wrongly wrote that neon is used in light bulbs or potato chips packages” (CP 2012 Q1).

Topic I (b) The ocean

Sea water: composition, salt and pure water, tests, electrolysis products

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Composition of sea water and “describe various kinds of minerals in the sea” — water plus dissolved salts (mostly sodium chloride), including compounds from which elements such as bromine are obtained (Part I wording).
  • Extract common salt and isolate pure water from sea water: evaporation for the salt, simple distillation for the water — and “evaluate the appropriateness of using evaporation, distillation, crystallisation and filtration for different physical separation situations” (Part I wording).
  • Tests to show sodium and chloride in common salt, and the test for the presence of water in a sample — flame test and silver nitrate with the bank’s procedure wording (ms bank §11).
  • Electrolysis of sea water and uses of the products — chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide with their society benefits (the Guide’s own STSE line) (Part I wording).
  • STSE anchors from the file: desalination “is an alternative means of providing fresh water to the Hong Kong people rather than importing water from the Guangdong province.”
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Dissolve the solids separately in water” first — the report records candidates who skipped it and reached for a flame test that cannot work on non-characteristic flames (CP 2016 Q9). When the industrial context is meant, “electrolysis of sea water” is the wrong phrase: brine is the electrolyte and the cell type plus electrode products are separately marked (CP 2013 Q2). Sea-water bromine work asks for “toxic”, not “harmful/irritant” (CP 2016 Q8).

Topic I (c) Rocks and minerals

Rocks → minerals → metals; calcium carbonate in three forms and its reactions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Rocks as a source of minerals; “isolation of useful materials from minerals as exemplified by the extraction of metals from their ores” — the methods named by the column are the physical method, heating alone and heating with carbon (Part I wording).
  • Limestone, chalk and marble as different forms of calcium carbonate — one substance, three origins (Part I wording).
  • Erosion processes as exemplified by the action of heat, water and acids on calcium carbonate, including the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate and test for carbon dioxide (limewater turns milky) (Part I wording).
  • Tests to show calcium and carbonate in limestone/chalk/marble, and use word equations to describe chemical changes (Part I wording).
  • Conservation frame (values): “to show concern over the limited reserve of natural resources”.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Method-to-example matching carries the marks — “physical method” is a genuine extraction answer, yet most candidates over-reacted and reached for acids or carbon heating when a property-based separation was wanted (CP 2015, Paper 2 context). On the heating-to-limewater set-up, only about a third gave the correct sucking-back procedure (remove the delivery tube before stopping heating) (CP 2016 Q7).

Engine A · Strand (a)

The Atmosphere Inspector

What air is, how it is split apart, and how you prove you have the oxygen. Every depth line below is capped by the Part I wording — Topic I has no Part II Section B rows.

Pick a topic

I (a)

Composition of air

$$\text{N}_2 \approx 78\%\,,\qquad \text{O}_2 \approx 21\%\ \text{by volume}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The strand names “composition of air” (Part I wording) — learn the major components and their approximate percentages by volume: nitrogen ≈78%, oxygen ≈21%, argon ≈0.9%, carbon dioxide ≈0.04%, plus variable water vapour.
  • Nitrogen and oxygen dominate; argon is the main “other”; carbon dioxide is a trace that human activity lifts.
  • Percentages are by volume, and “≈” matters — air’s composition is not a fixed recipe.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “21% by mass” is rejected wording for oxygen — the convention is by volume; the bank reminds you “the unit must be correct” even in percentage answers.
  • Do not add exotic trace gases to a “composition of air” answer — name N₂, O₂, Ar (+ CO₂, water vapour) and stop there.
I (a)

Making liquid air — the preparation steps

$$\text{air} \xrightarrow{\text{clean + dry}} \xrightarrow[\text{cool}]{\text{compress}} \text{liquid air}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Describe the processes: dust is filtered out; carbon dioxide and water vapour are removed before liquefaction; the air is then compressed and cooled until it liquefies (around −200 °C).
  • Why the cleaning step: frozen CO₂ and ice would block the fractionating column — state this reason when asked to comment on the procedure.
  • All steps are physical changes: no new substance, no chemical equation required (word-equation level is Topic I’s register).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The order of steps is marked: clean → dry → liquefy → fractionate. “Evaporate the air to separate it” scores nothing — you must liquefy first, then allow the liquid to warm.
  • “Remove the oxygen before liquefying” is a self-contradiction — the whole point is to keep it for the column.
I (a)

The fractionating column — nitrogen boils first

$$\text{b.p.:}\quad \text{N}_2\,(-196\,{}^{\circ}\text{C})\;<\;\text{O}_2\,(-183\,{}^{\circ}\text{C})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Strand core: “separation of oxygen and nitrogen from liquid air by fractional distillation”, assessed as “describe the processes involved … and understand the concepts and procedures involved” (Part I wording).
  • Liquid air is warmed in the column: the component with the lower boiling point vaporises first — nitrogen boils off first and is collected as a gas near the top.
  • Oxygen, with the higher boiling point, remains liquid longer and is collected lower down; argon sits between the two and is taken off separately.
  • Same idea you already know from Topic I skills — differences in physical properties make physical separation possible.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Write “nitrogen has a lower boiling point, so it evaporates/boils off first” — reversing first-out and first-to-condense is the standing slip; the golden-rule note for this topic is literally “N2 boils first”.
  • Why the molecules behave that way is Topic II/VI bonding science — out of scope here; at Topic I the boiling-point difference is the complete answer.
🛡️ Safety
  • Handling liquid air: extremely cold — cryogenic burns; oxygen-enriched surroundings make fires spread rapidly. Industrial detail → Topic XIII, not examinable here.
I (a)

Uses of the separated gases

$$\text{O}_2,\ \text{N}_2,\ \text{Ar/Ne} \rightarrow \text{medicine},\ \text{packaging},\ \text{lamps}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The suggested activities name “applications of the products obtained from fractional distillation of liquid air” — learn one use plus its property reason per gas.
  • Oxygen: medical respiration and oxy-fuel welding (supports combustion). Nitrogen: unreactive → protects food in packets and stops oxidation. Argon: unreactive → fills filament lamps. Neon: glows with a characteristic coloured light → advertising signs. The Guide’s STSE line: “Oxygen extracted from air can be used for medicinal purposes.”
  • Liquid nitrogen as a coolant (deep freezing) rounds out the set.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The report is blunt about matching: “Many candidates wrongly wrote that neon is used in light bulbs or potato chips packages” — bulbs are argon’s use, packets are nitrogen’s (CP 2012 Q1).
  • Vague answers lose marks: “for making advertising boards” was flagged; say neon signs glow with characteristic coloured light at low power. Justification (“because it is unreactive”) is the second half of the mark.
I (a)

Test for oxygen

$$\text{glowing splint} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \longrightarrow \text{splint relights}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Demonstrate how to carry out a test for oxygen” (Part I wording): place a glowing wooden splint into the tube of gas.
  • Observation: the glowing splint relights. Conclusion: the gas is oxygen.
  • Bank discipline applies: “Correct chemical reagent = 1 mark / Correct observations with comparison = 1 mark” (ms bank §11) — procedure, observation and conclusion are separate marks.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “A burning splint” is hydrogen-test wording — for oxygen the splint must be glowing, and “relights” (not “burns brighter”) is the mark word.
  • Don’t decorate the answer: “the gas is colourless and supports combustion” is a property, not the test result the question asked for — “Award zero marks for answers which are contradictory” (bank conventions).
I (a)

Air is a mixture, not a compound

$$\text{not chemically combined, physically separable} \Rightarrow \text{air}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Tie the column to the overview vocabulary: element / compound / mixture “Students should know the terms …” (Part I wording).
  • Evidence air is a mixture: composition varies (water vapour, CO₂); the gases keep their individual properties (oxygen in air still relights a splint); they are separable by a physical method (fractional distillation of the liquid).
  • A compound forms in fixed proportions with properties different from its elements and needs a chemical change to decompose — none of that describes air.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “The nitrogen and oxygen in air are chemically combined” = zero for the classification mark. The phrase that earns it: “not chemically combined; can be separated by physical processes”.
  • Fractional distillation of liquid air is a physical change — do not offer a word equation for it; save word equations for genuine chemical changes (Engine C).

From air to cylinders — the four-stage pipeline

The strand verb is “describe the processes involved … and understand the concepts and procedures involved” (Part I wording). Walk the stages in order; every Paper question on liquid air is one of these four gates.

Stage 1 · Clean

Air is drawn in and filtered to remove dust and soot. Skipped here = “incorrectly considered…” answers later; cleaning is part of the procedure mark.

Stage 2 · Dry

Carbon dioxide and water vapour are removed. Reason to state: they would freeze and block the equipment. (How they are absorbed is detail beyond the columns — the removal itself is what is marked.)

Stage 3 · Liquefy

Compress and cool the cleaned air until it becomes a liquid (≈ −200 °C). Physical change — the molecules are the same as before.

Stage 4 · Fractionate

Warm the liquid air up the fractionating column: nitrogen (b.p. −196 °C) boils off first and exits as gas; oxygen (b.p. −183 °C) stays liquid longer and is collected; argon comes off between them.

Multi-blank Quiz — The Atmosphere

1. Air contains approximately 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen by volume, with about 1% of other gases, mostly argon.
2. Before liquefaction the air is filtered to remove dust and has its carbon dioxide and water vapour removed, because they would freeze and block the column.
3. In the fractionating column the components separate because they have different boiling points: nitrogen boils off first at about −196 °C, while oxygen (b.p. ≈ −183 °C) remains liquid longer.
4. Test for oxygen: place a glowing wooden splint in the gas; if it relights, the gas is oxygen.
5. Fractional distillation of liquid air is a physical change — air is a mixture, so its components keep their own properties and no chemical reaction takes place.
6. Uses: oxygen for medicinal respiration; nitrogen flushed through food packets because it is unreactive; argon fills lamps and neon makes coloured advertising signs.
7. Hydrogen cannot be obtained from the fractional distillation of air, because distillation separates only substances already present in air (CP 2013).
8. A 400 cm³ sample of dry air holds about 312 cm³ of nitrogen and 84 cm³ of oxygen — multiply the total by 78/100 and 21/100 (Solver Bench 1).
9. A test answer scores procedure, observation and conclusion separately: the bank marks “Correct chemical reagent = 1 mark” and “Correct observations” (ms bank §11).
10. Water vapour in the air is variable, not fixed — one of the pieces of evidence that air is not a compound; compounds have fixed proportions of elements.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — The Atmosphere

Boils first vs condenses first

On warming liquid air, the gas with the lower boiling point leaves the liquid first: nitrogen (−196 °C) before oxygen (−183 °C). “Oxygen boils first” inverts the column and forfeits the comparison mark.

78% by volume vs 78% by mass

Air percentages are quoted by volume. Oxygen is ~21% of the volume but ~23% of the mass — the exam convention is volume, and the bank demands the quantity be stated correctly.

Neon vs argon vs nitrogen uses

Neon → coloured glow in signs; argon → inert filling in lamps; nitrogen → unreactive blanket in food packets. CP 2012 Q1 recorded candidates giving the bulb and the packet answers for neon — the gas-to-use mismatch is this strand’s classic slip.

Glowing splint vs burning splint

Oxygen’s test uses a glowing splint that relights. A lighted/burning splint belongs to the hydrogen “squeaky pop” test — not on the Topic I list (it appears in later lab contexts; → Topic XV full scheme).

Remove CO₂ + water vs remove oxygen

Before liquefying, you remove dust, carbon dioxide and water vapour — not oxygen, which is one of the wanted products. Removing the product you are about to fractionate is self-contradictory (zero-mark territory: “Award zero marks for answers which are contradictory”, bank conventions).

Mixture vs compound (air)

Air’s composition varies, its gases keep their properties and it is physically separable — three signs of a mixture. Compounds form in fixed ratios, need chemical change to split, and their properties differ from their elements.

Engine B · Strand (b)

The Ocean Inspector

Sea water is the syllabus’s first working sample: one liquid, four school-level operations — read it, split it, test it, electrify it. Depth is capped by the Part I wording — no Section B rows exist for this topic.

Pick a topic

I (b)

Composition of sea water

$$\text{sea water} = \text{water} + \text{dissolved salts} \Rightarrow \text{a mixture}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The column asks “composition of sea water” and “describe various kinds of minerals in the sea” (Part I wording): water with many dissolved salts, mostly sodium chloride.
  • Sea water is a mixture — the dissolved substances keep their properties (it tastes salty) and can be separated by physical methods.
  • The ocean supplies usable materials: salt, water after desalination, and elements such as bromine obtained from sea-derived brines.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Sea water is a compound because it is uniform” is a rejected idea — a solution is a homogeneous mixture, uniform yet physically separable.
  • Quote salinity as ≈3.5% by mass (“about” is honest; the exact figure is not a memorisation target of the columns).
  • When the question ties to the sea-bromine context, the hazard word the report rewards is “toxic”, not “harmful”/“irritant” (CP 2016 Q8).
I (b)

Common salt from sea water — evaporation

$$\text{sea water} \xrightarrow{\text{evaporate}} \text{salt} + \text{water vapour}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Demonstrate how to extract common salt … from sea water” (Part I wording): evaporation — solar evaporation in shallow pans industrially; an evaporating dish with gentle heating and stirring in the laboratory.
  • Property exploited: water is volatile and escapes; sodium chloride is non-volatile and stays as solid.
  • Stopping rule in lab wording: heat until crystals just appear, then allow the residual water to evaporate / dry the solid — no charring needed for salt.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Answer the method and the reason in one sentence: “evaporation, because the water evaporates and the salt does not”.
  • CP 2014 Q12 recorded candidates swapping procedures — don’t import the filtration/washing routine here: there is nothing insoluble to catch on paper.
🛡️ Safety
  • Evaporating dishes get very hot — tongs, not fingers; eye protection against spitting.
I (b)

Pure water from sea water — simple distillation

$$\text{sea water} \xrightarrow[\text{condense}]{\text{boil}} \text{pure water}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Isolate pure water from sea water” (Part I wording) demands simple distillation: evaporate, then condense the vapour and collect the distillate — the salt stays behind in the flask.
  • The chain to state: evaporation → condensation → collection. The condenser cools the steam below its boiling point.
  • Evaluation verb (also Part I wording): explain why evaporation fails here — “you want the water, and evaporation lets it go”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Two products, two methods, one sample: salt → evaporation; pure water → distillation. Swapping them is the exact confusion CP 2014 Q12 logged.
  • “Condensation” carries a bank spelling flag — “[†]: correct spelling required” (ms bank §11, Mark Allocation): write the word correctly.
🛡️ Safety
  • Sealed-system warning: never distill to complete dryness with a closed delivery; anti-bumping granules before heating (heating-apparatus convention).
I (b)

Flame test for sodium (in common salt)

$$\text{Na}^+ \xrightarrow[\text{non-luminous flame}]{\text{clean wire}} \text{golden yellow}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Tests to show the presence of sodium … in a sample of common salt”, assessed as “demonstrate how to carry out the flame test” (Part I wording).
  • Bank procedure verbatim: “Moisten a clean nichrome / platinum wire with concentrated hydrochloric acid and dip the nichrome wire into a small amount of… crystals. Heat the tip of the nichrome wire in a non-luminous flame” (ms bank §11).
  • Result wording from the bank: “Sodium ions give an intense golden yellow flame while potassium ions give a lilac flame” (ms bank §11).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Colour vocabulary: sodium is golden yellow. Reserve “brick red” for calcium (Engine C) — the reports’ flame-trap family shows colour words are graded literally; potassium must be “lilac [purple/light purple]”, “Not Accept deep purple and violet” (ms bank §11).
  • The flame test is not a general identifier: candidates who “incorrectly considered that flame test can be used” and claimed a “bright white light” for a magnesium salt lost the marks (CP 2016 Q9) — magnesium gives no characteristic flame colour.
🛡️ Safety
  • Concentrated hydrochloric acid is corrosive — one drop on the wire holder, not a beaker of it; Bunsen flame discipline (tie hair, safety goggles).
I (b)

Test for chloride (in common salt)

$$\text{Cl}^-\text{(aq)} + \text{AgNO}_3\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)}\ \text{(white ppt)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Tests to show the presence of … chloride in a sample of common salt” (Part I wording).
  • Procedure: dissolve a little solid in water first (the CP discipline), then add aqueous silver nitrate with a little dilute nitric acid. The bank’s phrasing of the reaction: “Add excess AgNO₃(aq) to the solution to form AgCl(s)…” (ms bank §11).
  • Observation: a white precipitate forms → conclusion: chloride ions present. (The ammonia-solubility distinction between silver halides is a Topic XV deepening — badge only.)
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Acidify with nitric acid — never hydrochloric acid, which would be adding chloride to a chloride test.
  • “White precipitate” is the observation mark; “silver chloride” is the explanation — the bank convention requires that observation and explanation match (ms bank conventions). Testing a solid? Dissolve it in water first — “Most candidates failed to use water to dissolve the solids first” (CP 2016 Q9).
🛡️ Safety
  • Silver nitrate stains skin black — small volumes only; dilute nitric acid is corrosive to skin.
I (b)

Test for the presence of water in a sample

$$\text{anhydrous CuSO}_4\ \text{(white)} \xrightarrow{\text{water}} \text{blue}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Test for the presence of water in a sample” and “demonstrate how to carry out the … test for water” (Part I wording).
  • Two accepted routes: add the liquid to white anhydrous copper(II) sulphate — turns blue; or, for water trapped in a solid, warm the solid and hold dry blue cobalt chloride paper at the tube mouth — turns pink: “Heat respectively… place a piece of dry CoCl₂ paper in the mouth of the tube” (ms bank §11).
  • The reverse direction is what you see on heating a hydrated solid: “The crystals change from blue to white / pale blue” (ms bank §11) — losing water, not gaining it.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Anhydrous” = without water — it is the white powder, and “white → blue” is the test direction. Writing “blue → white” for the test reverses the chemistry (that is the heating of the hydrate).
  • The test is reversible in both forms — that is why it doubles as a water detector and a drying indicator in the bank’s line “Only MgSO₄·7H₂O can turn dry CoCl₂ paper from blue to pink” (ms bank §11).
🛡️ Safety
  • Copper(II) sulphate and cobalt chloride solutions are harmful — small amounts, no skin contact, dispose as directed; the overview value line demands “safe handling and disposal of chemicals”.

Electrolysis of sea water and uses of the products

One sea, four operations

Every ocean bullet in the columns is an operation on the same liquid. Pick the box the question names, then give procedure + property + result.

Operation 1 · Read it

Composition: water + dissolved salts (mostly NaCl) ≈ mixture. The “various kinds of minerals in the sea” go from table salt to industrial elements such as bromine.

Operation 2 · Take the salt

Multi-blank Quiz — The Ocean

1. Sea water is a mixture of water and dissolved salts, the common salt mainly being sodium chloride.
2. To obtain common salt from sea water use evaporation; to obtain pure water use simple distillation, because in the first case the wanted product is the non-volatile solid and in the second it is the condensed vapour (distillate).
3. Flame-test procedure per the bank: moisten a clean nichrome/platinum wire with concentrated hydrochloric acid, dip it into the crystals and heat it in a non-luminous flame (ms bank §11).
4. Sodium ions give an intense golden yellow flame; potassium ions give a lilac flame — and the bank will not accept “deep purple” or “violet” (ms bank §11).
5. Test for chloride: first dissolve the solid in water, then add aqueous silver nitrate with dilute nitric acid — a white precipitate shows chloride (CP 2016 Q9 discipline; ms bank §11).
6. Water turns white anhydrous copper(II) sulphate blue, and turns dry blue cobalt chloride paper pink (ms bank §11).
7. Electrolysing concentrated sodium chloride solution gives chlorine at the anode and hydrogen at the cathode, leaving sodium hydroxide in solution.
8. Uses of those products: chlorine for bleaches / killing germs, hydrogen as fuel / for margarine, sodium hydroxide for soap / paper.
9. For industrial chlorine the required words are electrolysis of brine — brine being concentrated sodium chloride solution; answering “electrolysis of sea water” was recorded wrong (CP 2013 Q2).
10. Evaporating sea water to collect the salt loses the water to the air; distilling it instead condenses the water vapour and collects the distillate — CP 2014 Q12 logged exactly this swap.
11. A 50.0 g sea-water sample leaves 1.75 g of dried residue: salt content = 1.75 ÷ 50.0 × 100 = 3.5 % by mass (Solver Bench 2).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — The Ocean

Evaporation vs distillation

Same starting liquid, opposite wanted product. Salt → evaporate, keep the residue. Pure water → distil, keep the distillate. CP 2014 Q12: swapping the procedures was the recorded failure.

Residue / filtrate vs distillate

Filtration gives residue (on paper) and filtrate (through it). Distillation gives distillate. Using any of the three words where another is meant costs the naming mark even when the setup drawn is right.

Nitric acid vs hydrochloric acid (chloride test)

Silver nitrate tests need a chloride-free acidic medium: dilute nitric acid. Acidifying with hydrochloric acid pours chloride into the very test you are running — a guaranteed contradiction (bank: zero for contradictory answers).

White → blue vs blue → white

The test for water: white anhydrous CuSO₄ goes blue. Heating the hydrate: “The crystals change from blue to white / pale blue” (bank). Reversing which sentence answers which question is the most common slip on this card.

Golden yellow (Na) vs brick red (Ca)

Sodium’s bank colour is “intense golden yellow”; brick red belongs to calcium (Engine C). Both are flame colours — neither is “bright white light”, the CP 2016 Q9 claim that scored zero.

Sea water vs brine

School strand: electrolysis of sea water. Industrial answer (CP 2013 Q2): electrolysis of brine. Same chemistry family, different wording demanded — match the electrolyte named in the question stem, and say where each product forms.

Sodium vs sodium ions (and the “get sodium metal” trap)

Common salt contains sodium ions, not sodium metal — the flame test detects the ions. Electrolysing aqueous salt solution gives NaOH solution, never sodium metal; extracting sodium itself is electrolysis of the molten salt — Topic VII/III depth, beyond this column.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Rocks & Minerals

Thermal decomposition vs burning

Decomposition: one reactant split by heat, oxygen not needed. Burning (combustion): a substance reacts with oxygen. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ has no oxygen on the left; don’t write “burning limestone” as the reaction type.

Limestone vs lime vs slaked lime

CaCO₃ = limestone/chalk/marble. CaO = calcium oxide / quicklime (heating product). Ca(OH)₂ = calcium hydroxide / slaked lime (water-on-lime product); its solution = limewater. One name swap = one zero.

Test for CO₂ vs test for carbonate

CO₂ test: gas → limewater. Carbonate test: solid + dilute HCl first, then limewater on the gas produced. The limewater is the shared step; the sample type decides which procedure you write.

Heating alone vs heating with carbon

Heating alone frees very unreactive metals (silver from silver oxide). Heating with carbon needs carbon to steal the oxygen (copper oxide → copper). “Why” is the reactivity series — that reasoning is Topic III; matching the named pairs is the Topic I mark.

Physical method vs chemical method

A magnet, panning or solubility exploit properties and leave the wanted metal intact — a physical method. Acids and carbon heating react with solids and can consume the product; CP 2015 shows most candidates chose them needlessly.

“No reaction with water” vs “reacts slowly with water”

Calcium carbonate: insoluble, no observable change with water — write it flatly. It is calcium oxide that reacts with water (exothermically). Transferring the oxide’s behaviour onto the carbonate loses both marks.

Preventing suck-back vs “cooling slowly”

The recorded right answer: lift the delivery tube out of the limewater when heating stops. The recorded wrong answers, verbatim: “the hot boiling tube should be cooled down slowly”, “the gaseous products should be taken into a fume cupboard” (CP 2016 Q7). Safety answers must change the pressure path, not the temperature.

The separation decision tree — ask two questions, get the method

“Evaluate the appropriateness of using evaporation, distillation, crystallisation and filtration for different physical separation situations” (Part I wording). The tree: ① what is the wanted stuff (insoluble solid / dissolved solid / liquid)? ② what state-pair is the mixture?

Insoluble solid + liquid

Filtration. Want the solid: residue, wash, dry. Want the liquid: it is already the filtrate.

Dissolved solid + liquid → want the solid

Evaporation (dry solid, e.g. salt from sea water) or crystallisation when crystals are asked for.

Dissolved solid + liquid → want the liquid

Simple distillation. Boil, condense, collect the distillate — pure water from sea water.

Liquids

Immiscible (oil + water) → separating funnel (bank wording: water is the lower liquid layer here). Miscible with different boiling points → (fractional) distillation — the liquid-air case.

6. Two immiscible liquids (oil + salty water) separate with a separating funnel; the water is the lower liquid layer and the oil leaves in the upper layer (ms bank §11).
7. A complete test answer gives procedure, observation and conclusion — the bank pays reagent and observation as separate marks (ms bank §11).
8. Distilling a salt solution is a physical change, but decomposing calcium carbonate with heat is a chemical change because new substances form.
9. Separation methods exploit physical properties: distillation exploits boiling-point differences, filtration exploits solubility, and a magnet exploits magnetism (CP 2015, Paper 2 context).
10. Word equation of the strand: “calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide” (Part I wording: use word equations).
11. A very dilute acid bottle does not need a corrosive label (CP 2015 Q1), but bromine is labelled toxic — not “harmful” or “irritant” (CP 2016 Q8).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Chemistry Toolkit

Dissolve vs melt vs react

Dissolve: solid + liquid, recoverable by evaporation — physical. Melt: solid + heat only. React: new substance. Salt “disappearing” in water is dissolving, not decomposing.

Saturated vs concentrated

Saturated = the maximum dissolved at that temperature, undissolved solid can sit there; concentrated = a lot dissolved, saturation unproved. Only “saturated” carries the temperature qualifier in definitions.

Evaporate to dryness vs crystallise

Powder wanted → evaporate to dryness. Crystals wanted → concentrate, cool, filter, dry. Both come from the same solution; the stem’s wanted form is the only thing that differs.

Residue vs filtrate vs distillate

Three products of three rigs: filtration gives residue + filtrate; distillation gives distillate (+ the non-volatile left behind in the flask). Naming them interchangeably is CP 2014 Q12’s confusion in its shortest form.

Observation vs conclusion

“Turns limewater milky” is an observation; “the gas is carbon dioxide” is a conclusion. Questions that say what do you observe want the first; test answers need both, in order.

Evidence vs proof (chemical change)

Bubbles, colour change and heat suggest a chemical change; identity of the products proves it — which is why the tests exist. “Fizzing = chemical change” without product identity is half the argument.

Filter vs separate the dissolved

Filter paper stops particles, not ions: a dissolved solute travels straight through in the filtrate. Salt+sand: dissolve → filter (sand) → evaporate/crystallise the filtrate (salt). One rig never does the other rig’s job.
