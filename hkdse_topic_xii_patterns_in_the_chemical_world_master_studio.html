Topic XII (a) Li → Ar physical trends

Periodic variation in physical properties of the elements from Li to Ar — nature of bonding, melting point, electrical conductivity

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Describe the nature of bonding and structures of the elements of Group I through Group 0: metallic (Li, Be, Na, Mg, Al), giant covalent (C, Si), simple molecular (N₂, O₂, F₂, P₄, S₈, Cl₂) and monatomic noble gases (Ne, Ar) — (Part I wording).
  • Describe the periodic variations of melting point and electrical conductivity across each period, not just quote values.
  • Know the mark-scheme melting order for Period 2: “Nitrogen < Lithium < Beryllium < Carbon (graphite)” and the analogous Period 3 shape peaking at silicon (ms keyword bank §9).
  • Conductivity: metals conduct via delocalised electrons; the MS order is “aluminium > sodium > silicon = sulphur” — no mobile electrons in Si/S (ms keyword bank §9).
  • Within the metals, higher ionic charge and more delocalised electrons → stronger metallic bond (Mg vs Na: “must see BOTH charges”) (mark-scheme 2-mark pattern).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Terminology is where marks die: “lithium molecules”, “ionic bonding in Li or Be metal”, “graphite is simple molecular” are all recorded wrong answers (CP 2013 Paper 1B context). Name the structure type first, then say what is overcome on melting — never “bonds break” for simple molecular substances.

Topic XII (a) Explaining the variations

Interpret the variations in melting point and electrical conductivity in terms of bonding and structures — metallic, covalent and molecular structures

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Build the three-sentence explanation: structure type → particle doing the holding → energy statement (“only weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting” is the MS template sentence) (ms keyword bank §9).
  • Giant covalent peak: “large amount of energy is needed to break millions of strong covalent bonds” — the silicon (and carbon) sentence (ms keyword bank §9).
  • Rank the molecular tail by molecule size: P₄ < S₈ (stronger van der Waals’ forces in the bigger molecule); Cl₂ and monatomic Ar sit lowest (structure-comparison pattern, ms keyword bank §2).
  • Electrical conductivity sentences: metals — mobile delocalised electrons; Al > Na “the number of delocalised electrons of aluminium is more than that of sodium” (ms keyword bank §2).
  • Communication skills: extended periodicity answers carry a communication mark — chain the ideas in full sentences (ms keyword bank §9).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Reactivity is not a conducting-power argument: “sodium has a higher electrical conductivity than aluminium because sodium is more reactive…” is a recorded wrong explanation (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q14). The general comments in CP 2012 and CP 2013 both blamed candidates who “confused the types of chemical bonding with intermolecular forces” — keep the two vocabularies apart in every sentence.

Topic XII (b) Oxide bonding & formulae

Describe the nature of bonding and stoichiometric composition of the oxides of elements from Na to Cl

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Learn the named seven — Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, Cl₂O — exactly as printed; no others are examinable under this strand (Part I wording).
  • Bonding type across the series: ionic (Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃) → giant covalent (SiO₂) → simple covalent molecular (P₄O₁₀, SO₂, Cl₂O) (mark-scheme 2021: SiO₂ — “Si and O linked by strong covalent bonds”, NOT accept Si/O ions).
  • Derive formulae from combining capacity / charge balance and recite the molecular formula P₄O₁₀ (the mark schemes also print the empirical P₂O₅ form of its water equation) (ms keyword bank §4).
  • Draw electron diagrams by type: ionic Na₂O needs brackets and 2− on O²⁻ — a “covalent-like” diagram was recorded wrong (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10); covalent Cl₂O needs the lone pairs on O shown (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Wrong outermost-shell electron count or charge on O²⁻ voids the ionic diagram (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10). “Silicon dioxide consists of molecules” and “SiO₂ contains ions” are both rejected labels — it is giant covalent, and the same structure label earns its Topic II marks too.

Topic XII (b) Oxides + water · acid-base

Describe the variation in behaviour of the oxides Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, Cl₂O in water; recognise the variation of acid-base properties as exemplified by Na₂O, Al₂O₃ and SO₂

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Water behaviour ladder: Na₂O reacts vigorously → strongly alkaline; MgO reacts only slowly / sparingly soluble → weakly alkaline; Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ insoluble, no reaction with water; P₄O₁₀ reacts vigorously → H₃PO₄(aq); SO₂ and Cl₂O dissolve → acidic solutions (Part I wording; ms keyword bank §4).
  • Products: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH (never H₂ gas — recorded error) (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10); Cl₂O + H₂O → 2HClO — writing HCl was recorded wrong (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
  • State both solubility and acidity/basicity when asked for behaviour in water: “soluble” alone was reported insufficient for Cl₂O (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
  • Acid–base character via the test reactions: basic oxides + dilute HCl → salt + water; Al₂O₃ is amphoteric — reacts with acids and alkalis to form salts and water; acidic oxides + NaOH → salt + water, including SiO₂ which “can neutralise alkalis to form salts and water” despite being insoluble in water (ms keyword bank §4).
  • The pattern to recite: from Na to Cl oxides change basic → amphoteric → acidic (Part I wording, exemplars Na₂O / Al₂O₃ / SO₂).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Insoluble in water” is not “no reaction with alkalis” — SiO₂ dissolves neither way but does neutralise alkalis (molten / hot concentrated). For the amphoteric mark, say “reacts with both acids and alkalis to form salts and water” — a bare “amphoteric” without evidence earns nothing when the question says “describe”.

Topic XII (c) Transition metals

General properties of transition metals — coloured ions, variable oxidation states, catalytic properties (plus position and importance)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Identify the position of the transition metals in the Periodic Table — the d-block between Group II and Group III (Sc–Zn in Period 4) (Part I wording).
  • Recognise that most aqueous ions of transition metals are coloured: learn the named trio Fe³⁺(aq), Cr³⁺(aq), Cu²⁺(aq) — and bank Fe²⁺(aq) pale green, MnO₄⁻(aq) purple, Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) orange from the reaction schemes (mark-scheme keyword bank; master summary §3.2/§8.3).
  • Variable oxidation states on the named examples: Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ (“the oxidation number of iron changes from +3 to +2”) and Mn²⁺ / MnO₂ / MnO₄⁻ (+2 → +4 → +7) (Part I wording; ms keyword bank §6).
  • Catalysis: transition metals and their compounds “can be used as catalysts” — the finely divided iron of the Haber Process is the bank’s worked example; a catalyst “provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy” (Part I wording; ms keyword bank §7).
  • Importance: catalysts across industry / medicine, health roles of transition metal ions, haemoglobin’s iron–oxygen binding stated in the topic overview; stainless steel alloying “carbon and chromium / nickel / manganese” (Part I overview; ms keyword bank §3).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Some candidates failed to give specific examples to illustrate their answers” and misspelt ‘Haber Process’ — named species and correct spelling are cheap marks (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10). Colour answers need the reason too: “stated the colour… without giving a reason” was recorded; link colour change to the oxidation-number change (CP 2014 Paper 1B Q11). Block-2 / Group-1 compounds are the colourless contrast (white Na₂O, MgO; Fe²⁺ blue precipitant trap vs K₃[Fe(CN)₆] CP 2014 Paper 1A Q21).

XII (a)

The Li→Ar landscape

$$\text{Li}\to\text{Ne}\ (\text{Period 2}) \\ \text{Na}\to\text{Ar}\ (\text{Period 3})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Strand (a) spans Li to Ar — both full periods, assessed “from Group I through Group 0” (Part I wording).
  • Two structural storylines: Period 2 (Li, Be metals · C, B giants · N₂ O₂ F₂ Ne molecules) and the identical-shape Period 3 (Na Mg Al · Si · P₄ S₈ Cl₂ · Ar).
  • Assessed skills: describe the variations, then interpret them from bonding and structures — description without explanation, or vice versa, caps the mark.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The question often hands you data (melting points, conductivities) and asks you to deduce the structure type — the reverse direction is tested too (ms bank: “1 mark for correct structure type identification”, “1 mark for explanation linking structure to property”).
  • Learn the eight Period-3 structures cold — the whole topic hangs off them.
XII (a)

Bonding across a period

$$\text{metallic} \to \text{giant covalent} \\ \to \text{simple molecular} \to \text{monatomic (Ar)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Part I wording to honour: “the presence of metallic structures, covalent structures and molecular structures”.
  • Metals: giant lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ register — see Topic II for the model itself).
  • Giants: C (graphite/diamond) and Si — every atom covalently bonded to neighbours.
  • Molecules: N₂, O₂, F₂, P₄, S₈, Cl₂ — strong covalent bonds inside, van der Waals’ forces between; Ne and Ar exist as single atoms.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Between the molecules” takes van der Waals’ forces; “between the atoms in the lattice” takes metallic / covalent bonds. The 2012–2013 general comments recorded exactly this swap as a mass error.
  • Phosphorus is P₄ and sulphur is S₈ when the explanation needs the molecule size — write the molecular formula, not just “P” and “S”.
XII (a)

“Melting overcomes…” — the wording ladder

$$\text{``only weak van der Waals' forces} \\ \text{need to be overcome in melting''}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Three tiers, one sentence each (all MS wording): molecular → weak van der Waals’ forces overcome; metallic → stronger/weaker metallic bonds overcome (charge + delocalised-electron count); giant covalent → “large amount of energy is needed to break millions of strong covalent bonds”.
  • Classic exam ladder (mark-scheme pattern): simple molecular < metallic (compare charges) < giant covalent.
  • The N₂ template — “N₂ has the lowest melting point as it has a simple molecular structure, only weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting” (ms bank §9) — is reusable verbatim for Cl₂, Ar, P₄, S₈ after a one-word swap.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never write “covalent bonds break when phosphorus melts” — covalent bonds inside P₄ survive; intermolecular forces go (CP 2012 & 2013 general comments).
  • “Weak bonds” is ambiguous — name the force type. Bonds hold particles together inside molecules or lattices; forces act between particles.
XII (a)

Na < Mg < Al — why metals climb

$$\text{Na} < \text{Mg} < \text{Al} \ll \text{Si}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mark-scheme 2-mark pattern for Mg vs Na: M1 — the ions are Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ (“must see BOTH charges”); M2 — Mg has more delocalised electrons per atom → stronger attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons → higher melting point.
  • Same logic completes the row: Al³⁺ with three delocalised electrons per atom is stronger again.
  • The pair Be < B, Li < Be in Period 2 works identically (“Both Li and Be have metallic structure, metallic bond in Li is weaker than that in Be” — ms bank §9).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Values here are textbook-rounded (≈) — the order is examinable, the decimals are not.
  • Do not explain the metal–metal step by “more electrons in the atom” — state ion charge and number of delocalised electrons (the MS keywords), then say stronger metallic bond.
XII (a)

Silicon — the melting-point peak

$$\text{Si: giant covalent} \Rightarrow \\ \text{``break millions of strong} \\ \text{covalent bonds''}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Every Si atom is joined to four others by strong covalent bonds across the whole lattice — melting means snapping bonds, not slipping particles past each other.
  • Carbon (graphite/diamond) sits the same seat in Period 2 — the MS’s highest-melting example sentence is about “Carbon (graphite)… giant covalent structure… millions of strong covalent bonds” (ms bank §9).
  • The peak position — Group IV — is the pattern statement: the element with maximum covalent connectivity melts highest.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Write “giant covalent structure” — “giant molecular” is legacy wording; “macromolecular” is safe as a synonym but keep giant covalent as the headline label.
  • Never say SiO₂ here unless asked about oxides: silicon is the element, silicon(IV) oxide the compound (Engine B).
XII (a)

P₄ · S₈ · Cl₂ — the molecular tail

$$\text{P}_4(44\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}) < \text{S}_8(115\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}) \\ \text{Cl}_2(-101\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}) \ll \text{the metals}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • All three are simple molecular — low melting points, because only van der Waals’ forces are overcome on melting.
  • The tail is not monotonic: S₈ (bigger molecule, more electrons) has stronger van der Waals’ forces than P₄, so sulphur melts above sodium’s 98 °C even though the metals precede it in position — rank by structure first, molecule size second.
  • The size argument is MS-sanctioned: “van der Waals’ forces between CS₂ molecules are stronger than those between CO₂ molecules” (greater molecular size — ms bank §2); port it to S₄-type reasoning as “larger molecules → stronger van der Waals’ forces”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Say molecular size (or number of electrons), not “relative molecular mass is bigger” — mass alone is not the awardable reason in the bank’s comparisons.
  • “Phosphorus melts lower than sodium” only becomes sensible once you have labelled both structures — position in the period explains nothing by itself.
XII (a)

Argon — the lowest melting point

$$\text{Ar: monatomic} \Rightarrow \text{weakest} \\ \text{van der Waals' forces} \Rightarrow \text{lowest mp}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Argon exists as single atoms — smallest “particles”, fewest electrons → the weakest van der Waals’ forces of the whole period → lowest melting point (below Cl₂).
  • The MS template applies with “atoms” swapped for “molecules”: “only weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting” (cf. the N₂ sentence, ms bank §9).
  • Group 0 trend for context (Topic II): boiling point rises down the group with electron count — argon above neon.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not say “argon has no intermolecular forces / no bonds, so it barely melts” — it condenses and melts precisely because van der Waals’ forces between its atoms, though tiny, are not zero.
  • “Ar is monoatomic because it has a full outer shell (2,8,8)” is the stability reasoning Topic II taught; here use it to justify the particle choice in the force comparison.
XII (a)

Period 2 set-piece: N₂ < Li < Be < C

$$\text{N}_2 < \text{Li} < \text{Be} < \text{C (graphite)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • This four-line ordering with its four explanation lines is a preserved mark scheme (2013 Paper 1) — learn the full table: N₂ simple molecular → weak vdW; Li and Be metallic, Li’s metallic bond weaker; C(graphite) giant covalent → break millions of strong covalent bonds.
  • “1 mark for correct order, 1 mark per correct explanation (structure → property link)” — the mark allocation itself (ms bank §9).
  • Graphite is the giant covalent member — the recorded wrong answers were “graphite has a simple molecular structure” and “lithium molecules are present in lithium metal” (CP 2013 Paper 1B context).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Extended periodicity answers attract a communication mark (ms bank §9) — organize as order line, then one explanation per substance.
  • Boron sits between Be and C in reality (giant covalent, very high mp); if a stem inserts it, reason from structure — never from group number arithmetic.
XII (a)

Electrical conductivity of the metals

$$\text{Al} > \text{Na}\ \text{(both conduct;} \\ \text{solid and liquid)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mechanism sentence: metals conduct because of (delocalised) electrons that can move through the lattice — solid and liquid alike (4-mark conductivity pattern, master summary §2.5).
  • The MS ranking sentence: “The number of delocalised electrons of aluminium is more than that of sodium so that electrical conductivity of aluminium is higher” (ms bank §2).
  • Mg sits between by the same count: 1 vs 2 vs 3 delocalised electrons per atom for Na vs Mg vs Al.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “It has electrons” is NOT ACCEPT — every atom has electrons; the awardable phrase is mobile / delocalised electrons (master summary: “NOT accept ‘free electrons’ alone” for graphite wording as well).
  • Contrast with solutions later in the course: electrolyte conduction is by mobile ions — never let electrons “swim” through the liquid (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q7; CP 2016 Paper 1B Q2).
XII (a)

Why silicon = sulphur in the ranking

$$\text{aluminium} > \text{sodium} > \\ \text{silicon} = \text{sulphur}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • For this comparison the MS treats both as non-conductors: “Silicon has giant covalent structure and its electrons are not mobile” · “Sulphur has simple molecular structure and its electrons are not mobile and cannot conduct electricity” (ms bank §2).
  • Graphite is the Period-2 exception: one non-bonding (delocalised) electron per carbon moves along the layers (Topic II model) — so “all covalent structures insulate” is false.
  • Si’s semiconductor behaviour (conductivity rising with temperature) is a textbook aside — in this MS comparison write “poor / does not conduct”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The reason silicon does not conduct like a metal: its electrons are localized in covalent bonds — no mobile electrons. That, not “it is a metalloid”, is the mark.
  • CP 2016 Paper 1B Q14 records candidates who could order the list yet still mislabelled the structures — the ordering mark and explanation marks are separate (ms bank §9).
XII (a)

Reactivity ≠ conductivity (the recorded fallacy)

$$\text{“Na conducts better} \\ \text{because it is more reactive”} \Rightarrow 0$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • A reported wrong answer: “…sodium has a higher electrical conductivity than aluminium because sodium is more reactive, and the outermost shell electron of a sodium atom loses more easily…” (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q14).
  • Conductivity is a physical property of the solid — how easily electrons move, decided by structure; reactivity is a chemical property — how readily the element reacts.
  • The physical trend is explained by mobile-electron count, the chemical trend (reaction of the metals with water, oxygen) by electron-loss tendency: two registers, never mixed inside one sentence.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • If a sentence about conductivity contains the word “reactive”, delete it and restart from the structure.
  • The CP 2013 note about “interparticle attraction… is ionic bond” shows the same disease in another dress — label the attraction correctly first (metallic bond), then compare strengths.

Atomic radius context — outside the columns

$$\text{Na} \to \text{Cl: same shell,} \\ \text{rising charge} \Rightarrow \text{shrinking radius}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Strand (a) lists only bonding, melting point and electrical conductivity (Part I wording) — atomic/ionic radius comparisons are not named in the columns of Topic XII and are assessed, where taught, under Topic II’s electronic-arrangement logic.
  • Use the one-line reasoning as a scaffold only: across a period the added protons pull the same shell tighter, so atoms shrink; the shell count jumps at Na (new period).
  • Never let radius do the explaining that structure must do: radius is not the mark-scheme sentence for melting point.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Card labelled “outside columns” deliberately — do not spend revision time on radius lists here.

The melting-point curve explained by structure

Period 3 from Na to Ar; bar heights ∝ √(mp + 200) so the low end stays readable. Values are textbook-rounded (≈ °C) — you are examined on the shape and the sentences, never the decimals.

Climb · Na → Al

Na⁺ · Mg²⁺ · Al³⁺: rising ion charge and more delocalised electrons per atom → stronger metallic attraction → mp rises. Name both charges (MS 2-mark pattern).

Peak · Si

Giant covalent: melting must “break millions of strong covalent bonds” — the MS’s own highest-tier sentence, learned for carbon, reused for silicon.

Tail · P₄ → S₈ → Cl₂

All simple molecular → low mps; the S₈ > P₄ bump is larger molecules → stronger van der Waals’ forces. Only “weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting”.

Foot · Ar

Monatomic — the smallest, fewest-electron particles of the period, so the weakest van der Waals’ forces and the lowest melting point of all of Na→Ar.

Multi-blank Quiz — Physical Trends Li→Ar

1. Strand (a) covers the elements from Li to Ar — i.e. Parts of Group I through Group 0 — and lists three physical variables: the nature of bonding, melting point and electrical conductivity (Part I wording).
2. The mark-scheme Period-2 melting order is Nitrogen < Lithium < Beryllium < Carbon (graphite). N₂ is lowest because it has a simple molecular structure and “only weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting” (ms bank §9).
3. Carbon (graphite) is highest because it has a giant covalent structure: a large amount of energy is needed to break millions of strong covalent bonds (ms bank §9).
4. Both Li and Be have metallic structure; the metallic bond in Li is weaker than that in Be (ms bank §9) — the same sentence structure, with Na⁺/Mg²⁺ charges named, explains Na < Mg in Period 3.
5. Mg vs Na, mark 1: name the ions Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ (both charges must appear); mark 2: Mg has more delocalised electrons → stronger attraction → higher melting point (mark-scheme 2-mark pattern).
6. The Period-3 melting-point peak is silicon because it is giant covalent; the two metals before it rank Na < Mg < Al by ion charge and delocalised-electron count.
7. P₄, S₈ and Cl₂ all melt low because they are simple molecular; S₈ melts above P₄ because its larger molecules give stronger van der Waals’ forces (bank pattern: greater molecular size).
8. Argon has the lowest melting point of the period because it exists as single atoms — the smallest particles with the fewest electrons, so the weakest van der Waals’ forces.
9. The MS conductivity ranking of the four elements tested in 2016 is aluminium > sodium > silicon = sulphur (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q14; ms bank §9); aluminium wins because “the number of delocalised electrons of aluminium is more than that of sodium” (ms bank §2).
10. Silicon does not conduct like a metal because “its electrons are not mobile” — its electrons are locked in covalent bonds of the giant structure (ms bank §2); metals conduct in solid and molten states through mobile delocalised electrons.
11. The recorded wrong explanation “sodium conducts better than aluminium because sodium is more reactive” mixes a chemical property into a physical one (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q14).
12. For extended answers the bank allocates 1 mark for the correct order, one per correct structure → property explanation, and a communication mark (ms bank §9).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Physical Trends

“Lithium molecules” vs lithium atoms/ions

Recorded zero-scoring phrase: “lithium molecules are present in lithium metal” (CP 2013 Paper 1B context). Lithium metal is Li atoms — a giant metallic lattice of positive ions in delocalised electrons; no Li₂ molecules in the bulk.

Metallic bond vs ionic bond (in Li / Be)

“The interparticle attraction in elemental lithium or beryllium is ionic bond” was a recorded error (CP 2013 Paper 1B context). Ionic bonding needs two kinds of ion from two elements — a pure metal has only its own cations plus delocalised electrons: metallic.

Graphite: giant covalent vs simple molecular

“Graphite has a simple molecular structure” — recorded wrong (CP 2013 Paper 1B context). Each C is covalently bonded to three others across endless sheets; the weak forces are only between the layers. Layers sliding ≠ molecular solid melting.

Bond breaking vs forces overcoming

Melting P₄ overcomes van der Waals’ forces; melting Si breaks covalent bonds; melting Na overcomes metallic bonds. The CP general comments (2012, 2013) flag swapping these three vocabularies as the topic’s top offence.

Reactivity vs conductivity

Ease of losing the outer electron explains reaction with water/chlorine (chemical). Conductivity is mobility of delocalised electrons in the solid (physical). “More reactive → conducts better” earned nothing (CP 2016 Paper 1B Q14).

Semiconductor talk vs MS comparison

In the ranking “silicon = sulphur” the MS wants “electrons are not mobile” for both (ms bank §2, §9). Bringing up doping or band theory answers a question nobody asked and risks contradicting the given order.

S₈ > P₄: size vs “number of bonds”

The bump is larger molecules → stronger van der Waals’ forces (bank wording uses “greater molecular size”), not “more covalent bonds in sulphur”. Inside every molecule the covalent bonds stay put during melting.

“Position explains it” vs structure explains it

“Melting point increases because the group number increases” is not an explanation — the awardable chain is structure type → holding particles → energy needed. Position only tells you which structure to look for.

vdW by “molecular mass” vs by “molecular size”

“Some candidates wrongly explained the strengths of the forces in terms of the molecular masses” (CP 2017 Paper 1B context); the same slip re-recorded for other molecules (CP 2022 Paper 1B Q4) and on the oxides themselves (CP 2023 Paper 1B Q13). The banked reason sentence is “Molecular size: S₈ > Cl₂; stronger van der Waals’s forces” (CP 2024 Q13) — size drives strength; mass is a correlate, not the cause.

Giant solids have no “molecules” — and slips survive a decade

“SiO₂ molecules are held by strong covalent bonds” was recorded in 2021, “wrongly stated strong covalent bonds between SiO₂ molecules” again in 2023 (CP 2021 Paper 1B Q3; CP 2023 Paper 1B Q13); in 2022 KI/HI got “giant ionic”/“giant covalent” labels swapped (CP 2022 Paper 1B Q1). A giant lattice contains no molecules: name the particles (atoms in SiO₂, formula units in KI) before naming the forces.

The Seven Oxides Inspector — Na₂O → Cl₂O

Bonding type, formula, behaviour in water, acid–base character — the exact columns the mark scheme reads. The set is closed: Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, Cl₂O (Part I wording).

Pick an oxide / idea

XII (b)

Sodium oxide — Na₂O

$$\text{Na}_2\text{O(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to \\ 2\text{NaOH(aq)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ionic (Na⁺, O²⁻), giant ionic lattice; formula from charge balance 2(+1) + (−2) = 0.
  • Reacts vigorously with cold water → strongly alkaline solution of the only soluble Group-1 hydroxide in this row; no hydrogen gas — the oxide is already fully oxidised (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10 recorded exactly that error).
  • As a basic oxide it also neutralises acids: Na₂O(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Exemplar No. 1 of the acid–base strand: when asked for the basic member, name Na₂O and its NaOH(aq) outcome (Part I wording “as exemplified by Na₂O…”).
  • One ionic oxide with a peroxide twin (Na₂O₂) exists in the literature — only Na₂O is in this strand; do not volunteer Na₂O₂ in answers.
XII (b)

Magnesium oxide — MgO

$$\text{MgO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to \\ \text{Mg(OH)}_2\text{(s)}\ \text{(slowly, sparingly)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ionic (Mg²⁺, O²⁻), high-melting giant ionic lattice; white solid (“magnesia”).
  • Behaviour in water is the contrast with Na₂O: reacts only very slowly and the product Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble — suspension is weakly alkaline (pH ≈ 9–10).
  • Basic oxide proof: dissolves in dilute acid — MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l), the equation as it appears, with states, in the CP-quoted 2013 Paper 1A Q15 option list (CP 2013 Paper 1A Q15).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “MgO is insoluble so it does not affect water’s pH” undersells it — slight reaction + slight solubility → weak alkalinity; say all three parts if the verb is “describe”.
  • Do not write Mg(OH)₂ as a strong alkali — strong vs soluble are different claims; limewater-style wording belongs to Ca(OH)₂ in Topic IV.
XII (b)

Aluminium oxide — Al₂O₃, the amphoteric one

$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 6\text{HCl} \to 2\text{AlCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{NaOH} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaAl(OH)}_4$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ionic (Al³⁺, O²⁻; charge balance 2(+3)+3(−2)=0); white giant ionic solid, very high melting (it is also the protective layer on aluminium — “impermeable / impervious to water and oxygen”, ms bank §3).
  • Behaviour in water: insoluble, no reaction — like SiO₂ this must be stated, not skipped.
  • Amphoteric: forms salts and water with both acids (→ AlCl₃) and alkalis (→ sodium tetrahydroxoaluminate, NaAl(OH)₄ — older texts write sodium aluminate, NaAlO₂; follow your school’s equation, same verdict) (master summary E.4: “Al₂O₃ | Amphoteric | Reacts with both acids and bases”).
  • Exemplar No. 2 of the acid–base strand (Part I wording).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The definition mark demands “reacts with acids and alkalis to form salt(s) and water” — “reacts with acids and bases” without products is thinner; “dissolves in both” alone can lose the mark.
  • Al₂O₃ needing hot concentrated alkalis / fusion to react is a textbook depth note; the exam asks only for the amphoteric verdict and the two reactions.
XII (b)

Silicon(IV) oxide — SiO₂ (silica)

$$\text{SiO}_2 + 2\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{SiO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{(hot concentrated)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Giant covalent — the mark-scheme triple: “giant covalent structure / network”, “Si and O linked by strong covalent bonds”, NOT accept Si/O ions; acidic oxide; insoluble in water (mark-scheme bank, 2021).
  • Water: insoluble, no reaction — but insoluble ≠ inert: it neutralises alkalis “to form salts and water” (sodium trioxosilicate(IV), Na₂SiO₃; the basis of water glass) (ms bank §4).
  • Does not react with acids — its acidic character is shown only on the alkali side.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • The trap pair “insoluble, therefore no reaction with base” is the classic data-table error — for this strand SiO₂ is the acidic oxide that never touches litmus because it never dissolves.
  • Name it “silicon(IV) oxide” in prose (IUPAC-style as HKDSE uses it); “silica / sand” acceptable as description, “silicon dioxide” appears in the bank itself.
XII (b)

Phosphorus(V) oxide — P₄O₁₀

$$\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 4\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Simple molecular (white solid, P₄O₆/P₄O₁₀ cages) — the syllabus formula is P₄O₁₀, molecular; the mark schemes also print the empirical form: “P₂O₅ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄ / P₂O₅ + 3H₂O → 2H₃PO₄” (ms bank §4) — both are the same chemistry; match the formula the question gives.
  • Reacts vigorously with water → phosphoric(V) acid, “phosphoric acid that can give hydrogen ions” is the bank’s own explanation line (ms bank §4).
  • Acidic oxide: with alkalis → phosphates (via the acid: neutralise H₃PO₄(aq) with NaOH(aq)).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Names to pair: P₄O₁₀ = phosphorus(V) oxide (empirical name “phosphorus pentoxide” survives in old banks); the acid is phosphoric(V) acid H₃PO₄ — “phosphorous acid” H₃PO₃ is a different compound (Topic IV salts).
  • The product of P₄O₁₀ + water is H₃PO₄ — writing HPO₃/H₄P₂O₇ alternatives complicates a clean mark.
XII (b)

Sulphur dioxide — SO₂

$$\text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3 \\ \text{SO}_2 + 2\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Simple molecular covalent gas (pungent); S is +4 — the oxide named by the strand (no SO₃ required here, though it behaves the same way with water → H₂SO₄).
  • Dissolves in water to an acidic solution — sulphurous acid; turns blue litmus red; with alkalis → sulphites (Na₂SO₃) + water.
  • Exemplar No. 3 of the acid–base strand: the acidic member is “recognised… as exemplified by SO₂” (Part I wording).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “SO₂ is acidic because it contains a non-metal” is not the sentence the mark wants — the evidence is H₂SO₃(aq) / litmus / reaction with base.
  • SO₂’s bleaching and reducing behaviour is real but belongs to Topics VII/XI — in this strand keep the answer on acid–base rails.
XII (b)

Chlorine(I) oxide — Cl₂O

$$\text{Cl}_2\text{O(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to 2\text{HClO(aq)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Simple molecular covalent (Cl–O–Cl, bent at O — angles are Topic VI, don’t go there). It is the oxide named in this strand; Cl₂O₇ and other chlorine oxides are extension.
  • Dissolves giving an acidic solution of chloric(I) acid (HClO) — NOT hydrochloric acid: “wrongly stated that the reaction of Cl₂O with water would give HCl(aq) or a mixture of HCl(aq) and HOCl(aq)” is a recorded error (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
  • Answer both halves: soluble AND acidic — a quarter wrote only solubility and lost the acid–base mark (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Oxidation number bookkeeping keeps you honest: Cl stays +1 going to HClO — no redox happens on dissolving; HCl would be −1, a giveaway the equation is impossible.
  • In its electron diagram show the two lone pairs on O — “omitted the lone pair electrons on the oxygen atom” was reported (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
XII (b)

Amphoterism, word-perfect

$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{HCl(aq)}} \text{AlCl}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{NaOH(aq)}} \text{NaAl(OH)}_4$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • In this topic the word belongs to exactly one oxide: Al₂O₃ (amphoteric hydroxide Al(OH)₃ is met in Topic III/IV — same logic, different compound).
  • Bank table wording: “Al₂O₃ | Amphoteric | Reacts with both acids and bases” (master summary E.4).
  • The proof pair to recite: with dilute HCl → AlCl₃(aq) + water; with NaOH(aq) → NaAl(OH)₄(aq) (school form NaAlO₂ acceptable) + water — salts both times, that is what makes it amphoteric, not mere dissolving.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not call the SiO₂–alkali reaction “amphoterism” — SiO₂ has no acid-side reaction; it is simply acidic.
  • MgO/Na₂O “react with acids” too — basic oxides do; amphoterism requires both sides, hence the word for Al₂O₃ only.
XII (b)

The acid–base band from Na to Cl

$$\text{basic} \to \text{amphoteric} \to \text{acidic} \\ \text{Na}_2\text{O},\text{MgO}\quad \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\quad \text{SiO}_2\text{–}\text{Cl}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The recognisable pattern the strand asks for: oxide character walks basic → amphoteric → acidic left to right (Part I wording: “recognise the variations… as exemplified by Na₂O, Al₂O₃ and SO₂”).
  • Water behaviour walks alongside it: vigorous → slow/slight → insoluble → insoluble → vigorous → dissolves → dissolves.
  • pH of the resulting solutions (where one forms): ≈13–14, ≈9–10, —, —, strong acid, weak acid, weak acid.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Data-table questions expect the band as a sentence: “the oxides change from basic through amphoteric to acidic across the period”.
  • If a stem shows pH values of oxide–water mixtures, rank them, don’t recite the trend backwards.
XII (b)

Stoichiometric composition — building the seven formulae

$$\text{Na}_2\text{O},\ \text{MgO},\ \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \quad|\quad \text{SiO}_2 \\ |\quad \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10},\ \text{SO}_2,\ \text{Cl}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ionic side: balance charges to zero — Na⁺O²⁻ needs 2:1; Mg²⁺O²⁻ 1:1; Al³⁺O²⁻ → Al₂O₃. The lowest whole-number ratio IS the formula unit.
  • Covalent side: read from combining capacity / oxidation number — Si(IV) → SiO₂; S(IV) → SO₂; Cl(I) → Cl₂O; P(V) → P₄O₁₀ (empirical P₂O₅).
  • The empirical/molecular fork appears only at phosphorus: the strand prints P₄O₁₀, banks print P₂O₅ equations — know they are one substance.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never “simplify” ionic formulae beyond the ratio (NaO is wrong; Na₂O), never “molecularise” them (no Na₂O molecule — it is a lattice of ions).
  • Charges from the electronic arrangement (Topic II logic): Na loses 1, Mg 2, Al 3; P/S/Cl share 3/2/1 pairs with oxygen — that is where the subscripts come from.

Multi-blank Quiz — Oxides of Na→Cl

1. The seven oxides named by strand (b) are Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂ and Cl₂O (Part I wording).
2. Na₂O + water gives the solution NaOH(aq) — a gas is not produced; the “hydrogen gas” version is a recorded error (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
3. MgO reacts with water only very slowly / slightly, forming sparingly-soluble Mg(OH)₂ — the suspension is weakly alkaline; with acid: MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) (mark-scheme equation).
4. The oxide that is amphoteric is Al₂O₃: it forms salts and water with acids and alkalis — the syllabus’s middle exemplar (Part I wording).
5. SiO₂ is giant covalent (“Si and O linked by strong covalent bonds” — the mark scheme rejects any ionic description), it is insoluble in water yet “can neutralise alkalis to form salts and water” (mark-scheme bank, 2021; ms bank §4).
6. P₄O₁₀ reacts vigorously with water: P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄; banks also print the empirical form P₂O₅ + 3H₂O → 2H₃PO₄ (ms bank §4).
7. SO₂ dissolves forming sulphurous acid (H₂SO₃); with NaOH it gives Na₂SO₃ + H₂O — the acidic exemplar of the strand (Part I wording).
8. Cl₂O + water gives HClO(aq) — chloric(I) acid, chlorine stays at +1; writing HCl(aq) was a recorded wrong answer (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
9. Across the period the oxides’ character runs basic → amphoteric → acidic; the bonding runs ionic → giant covalent → simple molecular.
10. In a water-behaviour question about Cl₂O a quarter of candidates stated only its solubility, never its acidic property — name both (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).
11. Charge-balance the ionic formulae: Na⁺ with O²⁻ gives Na₂O; Mg²⁺ with O²⁻ gives MgO; Al³⁺ with O²⁻ gives Al₂O₃.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Oxides

Na₂O + water: NaOH vs NaOH + H₂

Sodium metal + water makes H₂; sodium oxide + water only makes NaOH(aq). The reported candidate equation threw in hydrogen gas — impossible without changing oxidation numbers (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).

Cl₂O→HClO vs Cl₂O→HCl

Dissolving is not reduction: Cl is +1 in Cl₂O and +1 in HClO. HCl(aq) means Cl(−1) — something would have to be oxidised in plain water. Recorded wrong answer (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).

“Soluble” vs “soluble and acidic”

Behaviour-in-water = physical + chemical outcome. Stopping at solubility forfeits the acid–base half of the mark — a quarter of candidates did exactly that for Cl₂O (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).

SiO₂: insoluble vs unreactive to alkalis

“Insoluble in water” and “neutralises alkalis to form salts and water” are both bank statements about the SAME oxide (ms bank §4). Dissolving in water is not a prerequisite for acid–base character.

P₄O₁₀ vs “P₂O₅”

Molecular formula (the strand) vs empirical formula (older bank equations). One white solid; balance whichever formula the question prints. Calling the acid H₃PO₃ (phosphorous) pairs the wrong oxidation state (+3 ≠ +5).

SO₂ vs SO₃ · Cl₂O vs Cl₂O₇

The examinable set is the named seven — SO₂ and Cl₂O. SO₃/Cl₂O₇ follow the same acidic pattern (→ H₂SO₄ / HClO₄) but only appear as extensions; answering “SO₃” to a strand-(b) question is simply the wrong formula.

Amphoteric vs “just dissolves”

Amphoterism = reaction with acids AND alkalis giving salts and water both times (master summary E.4). Physical dissolving, or reacting on one side only, does not qualify — SO₂ dissolves and is merely acidic.

Ionic diagram vs covalent diagram

Na₂O: brackets, charges, full oxide ion (2,8) — “covalent-like” drawings lost marks (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10). Cl₂O: shared pairs + lone pairs, molecule has no charge. Decide bonding type FIRST, draw second. 2025 reprise: “wrongly considered aluminium oxide to be a covalent compound”, “wrong number of electrons on ions”, “wrong number of ions / charges” (CP 2025 Q1) — Al³⁺ loses all three outer electrons, O²⁻ gains to 2,8.

Amphoteric is decided at the acid and alkali tubes — never at the water tap

“Wrong stated that Al₂O₃ is amphoteric when added in water” (CP 2022 Paper 1B Q13): water tests neither side of amphoterism (Al₂O₃ simply does not react with water). Spell it — “failed to give the correct spelling of ‘amphoteric’ … wrongly stated that Al₂O₃ is an ‘atmospheric’ oxide” (CP 2018 Paper 1B Q14).

Labels are not evidence — name both products

“Only mentioned Al₂O₃ can react with acids and bases without further referring to the reactions that occurred or product formed” (CP 2024 Q13). The proof pair is salt + water twice: AlCl₃ + H₂O from HCl(aq); NaAlO₂ + H₂O (or NaAl(OH)₄) from NaOH(aq) — the bank’s own expected lines, H₂O included (“Omitted H₂O for the formation of AlCl₃ + H₂O” was a recorded slip). And “Al₂O₃ is acidic because it can react with HCl(aq)” (CP 2024 Q13) reverses the polarity: reacting with an acid evidences BASIC behaviour.

XII (a,b)

Reading an equation properly

$$\text{Na}_2\text{O(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to 2\text{NaOH(aq)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Four registers: formulae correct → balanced → state symbols (s/l/g/aq) → matching observation language if asked.
  • State symbols are markable: banks award “1 mark for correct … equation with state symbols”-style splits — species without (s)/(aq) shown can cap the mark (ms bank §6 practice; general marking conventions).
  • “aq” means dissolved in water, not liquid water itself — H₂O is (l), NaOH made in the water is (aq).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Formula slips are the most expensive errors — “NaO”, “MgO₂”, “AlO” lose everything downstream; build from charges first (Engine B card 9).
  • Some banks “neglect physical state” for pure ionic equations, but for these topic equations show states — you are never penalised for including them.
XII (b)

Making the metal oxides (combination with O₂)

$$4\text{Na(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{Na}_2\text{O(s)} \\ 2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{MgO(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Aluminium completes the trio: 4Al(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Al₂O₃(s) — same method, 2:3 oxygen ratio.
  • Observation line to know: “Magnesium burns with a bright white light” — verbatim from the bank (ms bank §9); leave the matchstick burning in air, freshly cut sodium tarnishes at once (oxide film).
  • Cut surface + air: sodium and magnesium oxides form cold as a coating; burning is the vigorous version.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • In balancing these, oxygen comes as a diatomic molecule — “Na + O → Na₂O” is unbalanceable nonsense; write O₂ first, then fix numbers.
  • The peroxide temptation: Na₂O₂ is real chemistry but off-strand — the oxide of this syllabus is Na₂O unless the question hands you peroxide data.

Making the non-metal oxides

$$\text{Si(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{SiO}_2\text{(s)} \\ \text{S(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{SO}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Phosphorus burns in oxygen: 4P(s) + 5O₂(g) → P₄O₁₀(s) (or with limited air, P₄O₆ — not required here). White phosphorus is stored under water — a standard demonstration note.
  • Sulphur: blue flame, choking gas — the SO₂ that the acid–base strand then dissolves. Silicon: forms its giant oxide coat; Si + O₂ → SiO₂ balances at 1:1:1.
  • Chlorine’s oxide Cl₂O is made indirectly (textbook preparations treat it as prepared, not burnt) — “Cl₂ + O₂ → …” is not a route to write.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Keep formulae from the strand list; the coefficients then follow mechanically. P₄O₁₀ balances with 5 O₂ per 4 P — half-integers only if you insist on P₂O₅ (then 2P + 5⁄2 O₂ → P₂O₅, double it).
  • Names: HKDSE prints “sulphur dioxide” (and “sulphur(VI) oxide” for SO₃); keep the “ph” spelling in every answer — the “f” variants seen elsewhere are the same chemistry, just not this paper’s register.
XII (b)

Oxide + water — the five that react / dissolve

$$\text{Na}_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaOH} \\ \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 4\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The five: Na₂O → NaOH (strong alkali); MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ (slow, slight); P₄O₁₀ → H₃PO₄ (strong-acid-strength solution, vigorous); SO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂SO₃; Cl₂O + H₂O → 2HClO.
  • Al₂O₃ and SiO₂: no reaction with water (insoluble) — that is the answer to write, not silence.
  • The “oxide + water → the corresponding acid/base” pairing is the acid-anhydride idea the bank phrases as “phosphoric acid that can give hydrogen ions” (ms bank §4) — no jargon needed.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Two recorded product errors live here: H₂ with Na₂O, and HCl with Cl₂O (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10). Test yourself on products-only flash recall.
  • Add states and the solution pH in one breath: “…giving an alkaline solution, pH ≈ 13” beats a bare equation on “describe” stems.
XII (b)

Basic oxides + dilute hydrochloric acid

$$\text{MgO(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \to \\ \text{MgCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • That equation is reproduced verbatim (states included) from a 2013 Paper 1A Q15 option quoted in the exam reports — balanced 1:2:1:1.
  • Sodium twin: Na₂O(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l).
  • Observation: the solid dissolves, solution becomes colourless; no gas (metal oxide + acid is not carbonate + acid — no effervescence here).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Salt name from acid name: hydrochloric → chloride; the anion charge picks the ratio (Al³⁺ → AlCl₃, 6 HCl per Al₂O₃).
  • This is the acid-side evidence that an oxide is basic — pair the equation with the verdict sentence when asked to “show that”.
XII (b)

Al₂O₃ answering both sides

$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{(s)} + 6\text{HCl(aq)} \to \\ 2\text{AlCl}_3\text{(aq)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Alkali side, hydrated form: Al₂O₃(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 3H₂O(l) → 2NaAl(OH)₄(aq) (sodium tetrahydroxoaluminate); older form Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O — pick one and balance it; mixing the two without re-balancing is the classic slip.
  • Both reactions make a salt and water — that is why the verdict is amphoteric (master summary E.4).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A stem asking “design a test to show Al₂O₃ is amphoteric” wants both treatments (add dilute HCl; add NaOH(aq); solid dissolves in each) — one tube proves nothing.
  • Watch the word: NaAl(OH)₄ is “sodium tetrahydroxoaluminate”; if your school calls it “sodium aluminate”, write the formula your school marks.
XII (b)

Acidic oxides + sodium hydroxide

$$\text{SO}_2 + 2\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{Cl}_2\text{O} + 2\text{NaOH} \to 2\text{NaClO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The general sentence: acidic oxide + base → salt + water only — that is the definition of an acidic oxide (cf. “Silicon dioxide can neutralise alkalis to form salts and water”, ms bank §4).
  • Sulphur keeps its +4: the salt is sodium sulphite Na₂SO₃, not sulphate. Chlorine keeps +1: sodium chlorate(I) NaClO.
  • P₄O₁₀ route: it dissolves first to H₃PO₄, which the alkali then neutralises — writing one net equation (→ Na₃PO₄ + H₂O) is fine, the two-step story is clearer.
  • SiO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SiO₃ + H₂O under hot concentrated / molten conditions (Engine B card 3).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • No redox sneaks in: check oxidation numbers before and after — same numbers prove a plain neutralisation.
  • Salt-formula logic: Na⁺ with SO₃²⁻ → Na₂SO₃; with ClO⁻ → NaClO; with PO₄³⁻ → Na₃PO₄; with SiO₃²⁻ → Na₂SiO₃.
XII (a)+III

Na and Mg with water / steam — keep the oxide story straight

$$2\text{Na(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \to 2\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)} \\ \text{Mg(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \to \text{MgO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Group pattern (reactivity-series table, master summary §3.3): K/Na/Li — cold water, violent → hydroxide + H₂; Ca steady; Mg — steam only → MgO + H₂; Zn/Fe steam; Cu/Ag/Au nothing.
  • General alkali equation: 2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g) for Group I.
  • This card exists to separate the metal story (H₂ yes) from the oxide story (H₂ never) — the exact confusion CP recorded (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10). The deeper metal-with-water chemistry is Topic III.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Product depends on partner: Mg + cold water barely reacts; Mg in steam burns to MgO with H₂ — the (g) state on water is a real condition, not decoration.
  • Observation vocabulary: “fizzes / effervesces, solid disappears, lilac? — no: colourless solution; Mg: burns with a bright white flame in steam”. Keep the bank’s “bright white light” for burning in oxygen.

Balancing technique for this bank

$$\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 4\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4 \\ \text{P }4{=}4\ \checkmark\quad \text{H }12{=}12\ \checkmark \\ \text{O }16{=}16\ \checkmark$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Order of work: balance atoms that appear in fewest species (P, Al, Si), then hydrogen, and check oxygen last — it usually falls into place.
  • Even/odd trick for O₂ combination reactions: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO; if you get “4Mg + 2O₂” reduce to lowest terms.
  • The P₄O₁₀ hydration counts (6 water, 4 acid) come straight from H₃PO₄ needing 3 H and keeping 1 P each.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP-adjacent bank warning: balance failures are a reported general weakness across papers (“candidates were weak in balancing chemical equations”, CP 2013 Paper 1B context) — practise the bank’s ten equations until each takes under ten seconds.
  • Never alter formulae to balance (MgO₂, NaOH₂): change coefficients only.

Reaction routes — element → oxide → solution

Every (b) equation sits on one of four routes. Learn the routes and the equations stop being ten isolated facts.

Route 1 · Combination with O₂

$4\text{Na}+\text{O}_2\to 2\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ · $2\text{Mg}+\text{O}_2\to 2\text{MgO}$ · $4\text{Al}+3\text{O}_2\to 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ · $\text{Si}+\text{O}_2\to\text{SiO}_2$ · $4\text{P}+5\text{O}_2\to\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ · $\text{S}+\text{O}_2\to\text{SO}_2$

Route 2 · Oxide + water

Five go (Na₂O, MgO slowly, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, Cl₂O); two openly refuse (Al₂O₃, SiO₂ — insoluble, no reaction). Product keeps every oxidation number unchanged.

Route 3 · Oxide + dilute HCl

The three ionic oxides answer: Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃ → chloride salt + water. Acidic oxides ignore it. Equation evidence for the basic/amphoteric verdict.

Route 4 · Oxide + NaOH(aq)

Al₂O₃ (→ NaAl(OH)₄) plus the acidic four (SiO₂ hot conc., P₄O₁₀, SO₂, Cl₂O) → salt + water with O.N. unchanged. The verdict line for amphoteric/acidic.

Multi-blank Quiz — Equation Bank

1. Balance the burns: 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O; 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO; 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃; 4P + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀.
2. Magnesium burning gives the bank’s observation phrase “burns with a bright white light” (ms bank §9); sulphur burns to the choking gas SO₂.
3. P₄O₁₀(s) + 6 H₂O(l) → 4 H₃PO₄(aq); the same chemistry with the empirical formula reads P₂O₅ + 3H₂O → 2H₃PO₄ (ms bank §4).
4. MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) — the paper-printed form; the oxide + acid pair makes salt + water only, with no effervescence (CP 2013 Paper 1A Q15 option).
5. Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O; with NaOH(aq) the hydrated product is NaAl(OH)₄ (school alternative: NaAlO₂).
6. SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O — sulphur stays at +4, so the salt is a sulphite, never a sulphate.
7. Cl₂O + 2NaOH → 2NaClO + H₂O; chlorine’s +1 is untouched — chlorate(I), not chloride or chlorate(VII).
8. For Group-I metals with water the general equation is 2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g); magnesium needs steam and gives MgO + H₂ (master summary §3.3).
9. The two oxides that show no reaction with water are Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ — but SiO₂ still neutralises alkalis “to form salts and water” (ms bank §4).
10. Balancing discipline: change only coefficients, never the formulae; check the oxygen count last, and keep the equation printed with its state symbols.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Equations

Metal + water vs oxide + water

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂, but Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH with no H₂. The CP-recorded equation “sodium oxide … would give sodium hydroxide and hydrogen gas” fuses these two (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10).

Sulphite vs sulphate outputs

SO₂(+4) + NaOH → Na₂SO₃ (+4). A sulphate product (Na₂SO₄, S +6) is an oxidation that oxygen — not the alkali — must supply. Watch O.N. either side of every arrow in this bank.

NaClO vs NaCl vs NaClO₃

Cl(+1) in Cl₂O → chlorate(I) NaClO. Chloride NaCl is Cl(−1) — impossible without reduction; chlorate(VII)/chlorate(V) salts need higher oxidation states the strand never gives.

Mg + water → Mg(OH)₂? Only cold and nearly

Magnesium + steam → MgO + H₂ (series table). The hydroxide forms from the oxide slowly in cold water. Two different routes, two different formulas — do not cross them.

Aluminate equation versions

Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄ balances. Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O also balances. “Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAl(OH)₄” or + H₂O on the wrong side balances nothing — pick one equation and stay in it.

Cl₂O made by burning chlorine? No route

Chlorine does not burn in oxygen; Cl₂O reaches the paper through its water and alkali chemistry only. Inventing “Cl₂ + O₂ → Cl₂O” as a preparation is a fabrication the examiner can spot instantly.

Catalysis — the word-perfect lines

$$\text{“A catalyst provides} \\ \text{an alternative pathway} \\ \text{with lower activation energy”}$$
🎯 Learning Goals

4. Fe²⁺(aq) is pale green (master summary §F.1); with K₃[Fe(CN)₆](aq) — the main ingredient of rust indicator — it gives a blue coloured substance (CP 2014 Paper 1A Q21).
5. Mn oxidation states on the ladder: MnO₄⁻ is +7 (x + 4(−2) = −1), MnO₂ is +4, Mn²⁺ is +2; the acidified permanganate colour change is “purple to colourless” (ms bank §5, §6; Part I example).
6. Iron’s two common ions: Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺; the bank phrase is “the oxidation number of iron changes from +3 to +2” (ms bank §6).
7. The Haber Process catalyst is finely divided iron; a catalyst works by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy (ms bank §7), and it has no effect on the equilibrium position.
8. Contrast with the left of the table: Group 1 compounds are white / colourless and Group 1 forms one oxidation state only; transition metals are coloured with multiple states (master summary §3.2 table).
9. Stainless steel is iron alloyed with “carbon and chromium / nickel / manganese” (ms bank §3); haemoglobin’s oxygen binding relates to its deep-red iron-containing prosthetic group (Part I overview).
10. A colour answer needs its reason: the 2014 VO₂⁺/SO₂ candidates lost marks for stating only the colour of the final solution without giving a reason — the mark wanted the statement that VO₂⁺ received 2 electrons from SO₂ (CP 2014 Paper 1B Q11).
11. Spell the names right on the sheet: the Haber Process — ‘Habour’/‘Harbour’ are recorded errors (CP 2015 Paper 1B Q10); the precipitate from Fe³⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq) is red-brown iron(III) hydroxide (master summary §8.3).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Transition Metals

“All metals are coloured” vs “most transition-metal ions are coloured”

NaCl(aq), MgSO₄(aq), Al₂(NO₃)₃(aq): colourless. The claim is about aqueous ions of transition metals — with the escape word “most” (Part I). Metal surfaces (Cu reddish, Fe grey) are a different fact.

Fe²⁺ pale green vs Fe³⁺ yellow

Swap them once and every later colour question about iron breaks. Anchor: 2 letters in “pale green” → g… Fe²⁺ (the paler one, lower state); Fe³⁺(aq) yellow, its hydroxide red-brown (§8.3 bank).

Purple salt vs purple ion

“KMnO₄ is purple so potassium is coloured” — no: K⁺ is colourless; the purple is the MnO₄⁻ anion. Transition-metal colour can live on the negative ion too (Cr₂O₇²⁻ orange).

Variable oxidation states vs variable charge in one compound

“Iron changes between +2 and +3 inside Fe₂O₃” — nonsense. In each compound the iron has ONE number; the metal shows different compounds in different states (FeO vs Fe₂O₃; FeCl₂ vs FeCl₃).

Catalyst raises yield? No — rate

“Increases the forward and backward rates to the same extent; no effect on the equilibrium position” (ms bank §7) — a catalyst delivers equilibrium sooner, never more. “Lowered the activation energy” alone is thin: say alternative pathway.

Catalyst “unreactive” vs “not consumed”

Catalysts do take part (surface/intermediate chemistry) and can even be poisoned out of service (ms bank §7); the exam-true phrase is “unchanged / regenerated at the end overall”.

Colour without the number

Stating “solution turns colourless” without naming which ion changed and its oxidation-number move was reported insufficient (CP 2014 Paper 1B Q11); conversely naming the change with no colour forfeits the observation mark. Both halves, every time.

Class sentences about colour — “Transition metals are coloured” fails

Rejected wording, re-recorded across years: “Some failed to use the correct terminology: ‘transition metals have different colours’” (CP 2020 Paper 1B Q12); the same slip in a copper item (CP 2021 Paper 1B Q12); “’Transition metals are coloured’” again (CP 2023 Paper 1B Q13). The awardable forms are per-metal: “Iron forms coloured ions.”, with the named ion and colour — Co²⁺/CoCl₂ pink (CP 2020 Paper 1B Q12), CrO₄²⁻(aq) yellow / Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) orange (writing “green” for either was recorded wrong (CP 2025 Q13)).

Zero is not a variable state — and say whose number it is

“The oxidation number of Cu is 0 in copper metal” offered as a variable-oxidation-state example was rejected (CP 2021 Paper 1B Q12) — the metal is not a compound; cite different compounds (Fe²⁺ vs Fe³⁺; Mn²⁺, MnO₂, MnO₄⁻). Precision of ownership was tested too: “The oxidation number of Fe²⁺ is ‘+2’” lost the mark, where “The oxidation number of Fe in Fe²⁺ is +2, while the oxidation number of Fe in Fe³⁺ is +3” was banked as the Better answer (CP 2023 Paper 1B Q13). And on Mn chemistry: “wrongly stated that MnO₂(s) or KMnO₄(aq) is the catalyst” where Mn²⁺(aq) is the species whose catalytic property the data show (CP 2017 Paper 1B Q14).
