Syllabus Master Overview

Topic VI Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Strands (a)–(e))

Every Students should learn / be able to strand of Microscopic World II, with HKEAA watch-outs from mark-scheme language, Candidate Performance reports and the 2018 Notes-for-Teachers depth caps.

Topic VI (a) Bond & molecule polarity

Polarity of bond and molecule — electronegativity and partial charges

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define the electronegativity of an atom — the ability of an atom to attract the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond towards itself.
  • Describe the general trends in electronegativity of main group elements: increases across a period, decreases down a group — use the trend to identify which end of a bond carries δ−.
  • Explain how the unequal sharing of electrons in covalent bonds leads to polar bonds (different electronegativities) and non-polar bonds (same atoms, equal sharing).
  • Identify the partial charges (δ+ / δ−) on polar molecules such as HF, H₂O, NH₃ and CHCl₃.
  • Explain the non-polar nature of CH₄ and BF₃ with reference to electronegativity, polarity of bonds and molecular shape: “the polar bonds are symmetrically arranged”.
  • Keep the two verdicts apart: polar bonds ≠ polar molecule — the shape decides whether the bond polarities cancel.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Examiners record that candidates “failed to demonstrate a good mastery of the concepts ‘electronegativity’ and ‘bond polarity’” (CP 2013 Q2), and that in a CH₂Br₂ item many discussed the molecule when only the bonds were asked (CP 2016). Use the MS register verbatim: “BF₃ is a non-polar molecule. The three polar B–F bonds are symmetrically arranged on the same plane.” Do not memorise electronegativity scales and do not quote dipole moments — both are capped out (NTF-2018 II-B VI (a)).

Topic VI (b) van der Waals’ · hydrogen

Intermolecular forces — van der Waals’ forces and hydrogen bonding

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Explain the existence of van der Waals’ forces in non-polar and polar covalent substances — they act between all molecules.
  • State the factors affecting the strength of van der Waals’ forces: larger molecular size → stronger (CS₂ > CO₂; O₂ > Ne are the MS pairs).
  • Compare van der Waals’ forces with covalent bonds: the intermolecular forces are far weaker — this is why molecular substances melt and boil easily.
  • Describe the formation of hydrogen bonding as exemplified by HF, H₂O and NH₃: an H atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O or N with lone pairs) is attracted to a lone pair on F/O/N of a neighbouring molecule.
  • Compare van der Waals’ forces with hydrogen bonding: hydrogen bonds are stronger — hence the anomalously high boiling points of water, HF and NH₃.
  • Understand the effect of hydrogen bonding on properties such as boiling point, viscosity and surface tension of water and ethanol.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

Never call a hydrogen bond a covalent bond — it is an intermolecular attraction; examiners punished “hydrogen bond formed between BF₃ and NH₃” (that pair forms a dative bond) (CP 2013 Q2). Sub-classifying van der Waals’ forces into “dipole–dipole”, “dispersion” etc. is not expected — write “van der Waals’ forces” full stop (NTF-2018 II-B VI (b)). In HF explanations “F is electronegative” was insufficient: write “F is a highly electronegative element” (CP 2012).

Topic VI (c) Ice & C₆₀

Structures and properties of molecular crystals — ice and C₆₀

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Describe the structures of ice and C₆₀: ice — water molecules held in an open tetrahedral arrangement by directional hydrogen bonds; C₆₀ — spherical molecules of 60 carbon atoms in a simple molecular crystal held by van der Waals’ forces between molecules.
  • State and explain the properties of ice and C₆₀ in terms of their structures and bonding: ice is less dense than water (open structure ⇒ molecules more closely packed when it melts); C₆₀ has a low melting point compared with diamond and is soft because only weak van der Waals’ forces are overcome.
  • Explain why ice floats: larger volume for the same mass once the open hydrogen-bonded lattice forms.
  • Explain C₆₀’s solubility behaviour: non-polar molecules, only weak intermolecular forces — soluble in some organic solvents, insoluble in water.
  • When a molecular crystal melts or boils, name which force breaks: van der Waals’ forces / hydrogen bonds between molecules — the covalent bonds inside the molecules stay intact.
  • Appreciate the role of models in visualising structures (values & attitudes: buckyballs, graphene, nanotubes as STSE context).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

CP 2013 Q1: stating “ice has an open structure” without correlating it to hydrogen bond formation earned nothing, and candidates could not explain the density of water vs ice. MS stock chain to memorise: “hydrogen bond is directional” → “tetrahedral arrangement / open structure” → “In liquid water, the water molecules have relative motion and this leads to the collapse of the open structure. The molecules become more closely packed.” CP 2014 Q1: C₆₀ described but its solubility in organic solvents left unexplained. Strand (c) has no Part II depth rows — the Part I wording is the whole scope.

Topic VI (d) Non-octet molecules

Simple molecular substances with non-octet structures — BF₃, PCl₅, SF₆

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Recognise the existence of covalent molecules with non-octet structures — the octet rule is a tendency, not a law.
  • Draw the electron diagrams of BF₃ (6 electrons around B — incomplete octet), PCl₅ (10 around P) and SF₆ (12 around S); show every lone pair on the surrounding Cl / F atoms.
  • Explain BF₃ + NH₃ → F₃B←NH₃ with the octet argument: “by accepting the lone pair of electrons from the N atom of NH₃, the B atom attains the stable electronic configuration of neon” (dative bond, bridging Topic II).
  • Count electrons around the central atom to classify a given molecule as octet or non-octet before predicting anything.
  • Link to shapes (strand e): all three syllabus non-octet molecules have no lone pair on the central atom — BF₃ trigonal planar, PCl₅ trigonal bipyramidal, SF₆ octahedral (names only).
  • Appreciate that bonding models advance with new evidence (fullerenes, graphene — values & attitudes).
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

PCl₅ was drawn with wrong outermost-shell electron counts and missing lone pairs on Cl (CP 2016). Do not transfer the octet habit to Period 3 centres — P can carry ten and S twelve electrons here; and do not claim B in BF₃ is unstable: it is stable but electron-deficient, which is exactly why it accepts a lone pair. Strand (d) has no Part II depth rows — Part I wording (“BF₃, PCl₅ and SF₆” named) is the entire examined list.

Topic VI (e) Shapes

Shapes of simple molecules — predict and draw three-dimensional diagrams

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Predict and draw three-dimensional diagrams for (i) molecules whose central atom obeys the octet rule, and (ii) non-octet central atoms with no lone pair (BF₃, PCl₅, SF₆).
  • The syllabus shape list is closed: linear (CO₂, CS₂), trigonal planar (BF₃), tetrahedral (CH₄, CCl₄, CH₂Br₂), trigonal pyramidal (NH₃), bent (H₂O), plus trigonal bipyramidal (PCl₅) and octahedral (SF₆).
  • Route: dot-and-cross diagram → count bonding pairs and lone pairs on the central atom → name / draw the shape in 3-D (wedge and dash convention).
  • Use the MS asymmetry register when shape meets polarity: “the central atom has a lone pair … three polar N–H bonds are not on the same plane.”
  • Shapes feed strands (a) and (b): symmetry decides molecule polarity; molecule polarity decides which intermolecular forces act.
  • Do not explain shapes (no lone-pair-repulsion theory essays) and never state bond angles — predicting and drawing only.
💡 HKEAA Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Stating bond angles for the molecules is not expected”, and explaining shapes, polyatomic ions or molecules with more than one central atom are all capped out (NTF-2018 II-B VI (e)). CP 2016: some gave CS₂ a bent shape (S=C=S is linear) or handed in an electron diagram when a 3-D shape was asked — wrong representation, zero marks. CP 2013 Q2: NH₃ pyramidal drawn well, BF₃ planar drawn poorly; two lone pairs on N instead of one.

🚫 Out of scope for Topic VI (Notes-for-Teachers depth caps — do not test here)

Bond angles for any shape — not expected, never quoted in this studio (NTF-2018 II-B VI (e)). Dipole moment (magnitude of separated charge × distance) — not expected; memorising electronegativity scales — not expected, the general Periodic Table trend is enough to identify partial charges (NTF-2018 II-B VI (a)). Classifying van der Waals’ forces into “dipole–dipole / dispersion / permanent dipole–permanent dipole / induced dipole” types — not expected; state the size factors and differentiate non-polar from polar covalent substances instead (NTF-2018 II-B VI (b)). Explaining shapes (full VSEPR theory), shapes of polyatomic ions, and molecules with more than one central atom — not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VI (e)). Non-octet structures beyond BF₃, PCl₅ and SF₆ are not on the examined list, and the syllabus requires their central atoms to carry no lone pairs. Strands (c) and (d) have no Part II Section B rows — their Part I wording is the full scope.

Engine A · Strand (a)

Bond Polarity & Molecule Polarity Inspector

Electronegativity, partial charges and the “symmetrically arranged” verdict — every polarity mark in the paper lives here.

Pick a concept

VI (a)

Electronegativity — the atom-level pulling power

$$\text{electronegativity} = \text{the ability of an atom to attract the \textbf{shared pair} in a covalent bond towards itself}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Define it exactly as above — the mark sits on “shared pair” and “in a covalent bond”.
  • It is a property of an atom in a bond, not of a lone atom floating free.
  • Use it only as a comparing tool: which of two bonded atoms pulls the pair more strongly.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Define it as attraction of electron pairs, not “electrons” in general — CP records shaky command of the definition itself (CP 2013 Q2).
  • Memorising electronegativity scales is not expected; the question gives what you need, or the trend decides (NTF-2018 II-B VI (a)).
VI (a)

The general trends — across a period, down a group

$$\text{across a period: } \nearrow \text{ electronegativity} \qquad \text{down a group: } \searrow \text{ electronegativity}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Main-group elements: electronegativity increases across a period (nuclear attraction of the shared pair grows) and decreases down a group.
  • Fluorine sits at the top-right corner of the usable table — the most electronegative element; use it only as a trend landmark, never as a scale value.
  • Apply the trend to pick the δ− end: O > H, Cl > H, N > H, Br > C.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Questions state “in terms of the trend” — quoting remembered numerical values is worthless (and capped out). Identify partial charges from the trend instead (NTF-2018 II-B VI (a)).
VI (a)

Non-polar covalent bond — equal sharing

$$\text{H} - \text{H} \qquad \text{Cl} - \text{Cl} \qquad \text{same atoms} \Rightarrow \text{shared pair exactly in the middle}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Two atoms with the same electronegativity (usually the same element) share the pair equally — no partial charges.
  • The bond is non-polar; the molecule may or may not be, but with one bond (H₂, Cl₂) it is non-polar.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Do not write “no charges at all, so nothing attracts” — non-polar molecules still have van der Waals’ forces (Engine B).
VI (a)

Polar covalent bond — unequal sharing, partial charges

$$\text{H}^{\delta+} - \text{Cl}^{\delta-} \qquad \text{shared pair displaced towards Cl}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Different electronegativities → unequal sharing → the closer atom is δ−, the farther δ+.
  • δ is a partial charge — far smaller than a full ionic charge; never write H⁺ Cl⁻.
  • Register from the papers: “C—H and C—Br bonds are polar … C and H / C and Br have different electronegativities.”
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “A polar bond is a weak covalent bond” is wrong — polarity is about unequal sharing, not weakness; H–F is both very polar and very strong.
  • State the cause: “…have different electronegativities” — naming δ without the reason drops the mark (CP 2013 Q2).
VI (a)

Dipole arrow — crossed tail at δ+

$$\text{H} \;\xrightarrow[\text{(crossed tail at } \delta+ \text{)}]{\longrightarrow}\; \text{Cl} \qquad \delta+ \longrightarrow \delta-$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The dipole arrow points towards the δ− end (towards the atom holding the shared pair more of the time), with a small cross across the tail at the δ+ end.
  • Mark δ+ / δ− and the arrow consistently — contradicting yourself voids both.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Dipole moment values are not expected — no charge × distance arithmetic anywhere in this topic (NTF-2018 II-B VI (a)). The arrow is a direction label, not a quantity.
VI (a)

Polar molecules — HF, H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃

$$\text{H}_2\text{O}:\ \text{polar O–H bonds, bent molecule} \qquad \text{NH}_3:\ \text{polar N–H bonds, trigonal pyramidal}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The MS asymmetry register, nearly verbatim: “NH₃ is a polar molecule. The central atom has a lone pair … three polar N–H bonds are not on the same plane.”
  • Diatomic HF: one polar bond ⇒ automatically a polar molecule.
  • CHCl₃: the three C–Cl and one C–H bond polarities are not symmetrically arranged ⇒ polar.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Cite both ingredients for the mark: polar bonds and non-symmetric arrangement — “it has lone pairs” alone is not the argument.
VI (a)

Polar bonds, non-polar molecule — CO₂, CCl₄, CH₄

$$\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O} \ (\text{linear}) \qquad \text{CCl}_4 \ (\text{tetrahedral}) \qquad \text{polar bonds, symmetrically arranged}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The stock MS sentence pattern: “The polar bonds are symmetrically arranged” ⇒ the bond polarities cancel ⇒ non-polar molecule.
  • CO₂: two polar C=O bonds in a linear arrangement; CCl₄ / CH₄: four polar bonds at the corners of a symmetric tetrahedron.
  • CS₂ is S=C=S — linear like CO₂, so non-polar too (the MS pairs CS₂ with CO₂ on van der Waals’ strength, not polarity).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP records “CO₂ is non-polar while CS₂ is polar” as a wrong answer (CP 2016) — identical argument, identical verdict: both symmetric, both non-polar.
  • Never write “the dipoles cancel” without naming the symmetric arrangement — the register examiners reward is “symmetrically arranged”.
VI (a)+(d)

The BF₃ case — the model verdict sentence

$$\text{BF}_3:\ \text{three polar B–F bonds, symmetrically arranged on the same plane} \Rightarrow \text{non-polar}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Word-for-word bank sentence: “BF₃ is a non-polar molecule. The three polar B-F bonds are symmetrically arranged on the same plane.”
  • BF₃ doubles as the non-octet shape example: trigonal planar, no lone pair on B — which is exactly why it is flat and symmetric.
  • Contrast pair for revision: BF₃ (planar, non-polar) vs NH₃ (pyramidal, polar) — same “three bonds”, opposite verdicts.

VI (a)

Two questions, two answers — bond vs molecule

$$\text{Q: “identify the polar bonds”} \neq \text{Q: “is the molecule polar?”}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Bond polarity = a property of one pair of atoms (electronegativity difference). Molecule polarity = a property of the whole shape (arrangement of all bond polarities).
  • Read the stem’s target word: “bond” or “molecule”.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2016, verbatim damage: when asked to identify the polar bonds in CH₂Br₂, “some candidates misinterpreted the question, and discussed whether CH₂Br₂ is a polar molecule” — two thirds named the bonds, below half explained; answer what is asked.
VI (a)

Register drill — “more electron-withdrawing than”

$$\text{Br is more electron-withdrawing than C} \Rightarrow \text{C}^{\delta+} - \text{Br}^{\delta-}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Bank wording for CH₂Br₂-type items: “C—H and C—Br bonds are polar” / “C and H / C and Br have different electronegativities” / “Br is more electron-withdrawing than C”.
  • Copy the causal chain: different electronegativities → unequal sharing → named δ ends.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Electron-withdrawing” is a relative phrase — never “Br withdraws electrons completely” (that language belongs to ionic bonding).

The Polarity Twin Gates — one hub, two questions

Every polarity item, bond or molecule, passes through the same two gates in the same order. Answer gate 2 only in the “symmetrically arranged / not on the same plane” register.

Gate 1 · Is the bond polar?

Same electronegativity (identical atoms) → non-polar bond. Different electronegativities → polar bond: shared pair displaced, δ− on the more electronegative atom, δ+ on the other. The trend (↑ across a period, ↓ down a group) is all you may quote — scales are not expected.

Gate 2 · Are the bond polarities symmetrically arranged?

Use the shape from Engine D: symmetrically arranged → non-polar molecule (CO₂, CCl₄, CH₄, BF₃); not symmetric / “not on the same plane” → polar molecule (H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃). No bonds polar? Molecule is non-polar (Cl₂).

Gate 3 · What follows polarity?

Van der Waals’ forces act in both non-polar and polar covalent substances — what is required is to differentiate van der Waals’ forces in non-polar and polar covalent substances (Part I wording); sub-type names such as “dipole–dipole” are not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VI (b)). If H sits on O/N/F: hydrogen bonding too. Solubility register: “relatively non-polar … attracted by weak intermolecular forces”.

The trap gate · Wrong target / wrong representation

Asking yourself the other question (bonds vs molecule) and drawing an electron diagram when a 3-D shape is required are the two recorded zero-movers (CP 2016). Read the stem, answer its noun.

Multi-blank Quiz — Polarity

1. The electronegativity of an atom is its ability to attract the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond towards itself.
2. Across a period electronegativity increases, down a group it decreases; you are not expected to memorise electronegativity scales, only to identify partial charges from the trend.
3. In a non-polar covalent bond two atoms with the same electronegativity share the pair equally, so there are no partial charges.
4. A polar covalent bond arises from unequal sharing of the shared pair; the more electronegative atom carries δ− and the other δ+. The bond is not a “weak covalent bond”.
5. The dipole arrow is drawn with its crossed tail at the δ+ end, pointing towards the δ− end; calculating dipole moments is not expected.
6. Water is a polar molecule: the O–H bonds are polar and the molecule is bent, so the bond polarities are not symmetrically arranged.
7. Carbon dioxide is non-polar although each C=O bond is polar, because the two bond polarities are symmetrically arranged in the linear molecule.
8. The bank sentence: “BF₃ is a non-polar molecule. The three polar B–F bonds are symmetrically arranged on the same plane.”
9. “NH₃ is a polar molecule. The central atom has a lone pair … three polar N–H bonds are not on the same plane” — draw it trigonal pyramidal, with one lone pair on N.
10. CCl₄ has four polar C–Cl bonds but is non-polar (symmetric); CHCl₃ is polar because its bonds are not symmetrically arranged — the shapes are on the same tetrahedral skeleton.
11. In CH₂Br₂ the C—H and C—Br bonds are polar because C and H / C and Br have different electronegativities; Br is more electron-withdrawing than C.
12. Bond polarity asks about one pair of atoms; molecule polarity asks about the whole shape — misreading which one the stem asks was a recorded CP 2016 slip.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Polarity

Polar bonds vs a polar molecule

Bonds are judged by electronegativity difference; the molecule by the arrangement of all its bond polarities. CO₂ has two polar bonds and zero molecular polarity. Answer the noun the stem uses (CP 2016).

Two roads to “non-polar molecule”

Cl₂: no polar bonds at all. CCl₄: polar bonds, symmetrically arranged. Use the right road — saying “CCl₄ has non-polar bonds” loses the first mark; saying “Cl₂’s dipoles cancel” invents physics.

“Electronegative” vs “highly electronegative”

For the HF hydrogen-bond mark the bank demands “F is a highly electronegative element” or “the covalent bond between H and F is very polar” — bare “F is electronegative” was recorded as losing the mark (CP 2012).

Dipole arrow vs δ labels

Both point the same way: arrowhead lands on δ−. A crossed tail sits at δ+. Mixing the convention with an electron-flow curved arrow (organic mechanism language) is out of scope here — label, don't push.

Trend vs scale values

“O is more electronegative than C (top-right corner of the usable table)” scores; “O is 3.5 and C is 2.5” is memorising a scale — explicitly not expected (NTF-2018 II-B VI (a)).

Polar bonds ⇒ “strong” intermolecular forces?

Molecular forces come next (Engine B): polarity changes which intermolecular forces act, not the covalent bonds’ strength. H–F is a strong covalent bond; boiling HF still breaks intermolecular attractions, never the H–F bond itself.

Engine B · Strand (b)

Intermolecular Forces Inspector — van der Waals’ & Hydrogen Bonding

Existence, strength factors and the boiling-point argument — plus the one naming rule: the paper says van der Waals’ forces, full stop.

Pick a concept

VI (b)

van der Waals’ forces exist in ALL molecular substances

The Force Ladder — which attraction, between which particles?

Every boiling-point or solubility mark is one rung of this ladder. Name the force and the particles it acts between — in this order.

Rung 1 · Inside the molecule — covalent bonds

Strong. Never broken by melting or boiling a molecular substance. The bank line: “only weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting” (N₂). Saying covalent bonds break = recorded zero (CP 2012 Q1).

Rung 2 · Between molecules — hydrogen bonds

Only when H is bonded to F/O/N and a lone pair on F/O/N of a neighbour is available. Stronger than vdW — hence water 100 °C, ethanol 78 °C, HF above HCl. Drawn as a dotted line.

Rung 3 · Between molecules — van der Waals’ forces

All molecular substances, polar or not. Strength grows with molecular size: CS₂ > CO₂, O₂ > Ne, larger homologues boil higher. One name only — no sub-types.

Decision flow · “Rank these boiling points”

① Can either substance hydrogen-bond (H on O/N/F)? That one is anomalously high — argue with the hydrogen bonds. ② Neither can? Bigger molecule → stronger vdW → higher bp. ③ Always say what is overcome: intermolecular forces, not covalent bonds.

Multi-blank Quiz — Intermolecular Forces

1. Van der Waals’ forces exist between the molecules of non-polar and polar covalent substances alike; classifying them into sub-types is not expected.
2. The van der Waals’ forces between CS₂ molecules are stronger than those between CO₂ molecules because CS₂ has greater molecular size — so the higher boiling point belongs to CS₂.
3. Oxygen boils at a higher temperature than neon because the stronger van der Waals’ forces between O₂ molecules are due to their larger molecular size — boiling does not break the O=O covalent bonds.
4. A hydrogen bond forms when an H atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O or N) is attracted to a lone pair on F/O/N of a neighbouring molecule.
5. On the strength ladder: van der Waals’ forces < hydrogen bonds < covalent bonds; a hydrogen bond acts between molecules, never inside one.
6. Water boils far higher than H₂S because hydrogen bonding exists between water molecules but only van der Waals’ forces act between H₂S molecules; HF boils higher than HCl for the same reason.
7. Propane, methoxymethane and ethanol have similar relative molecular masses, so their vdW forces are comparable, yet ethanol boils highest because its molecules form hydrogen bonds through the O–H bond.
8. Methoxymethane cannot hydrogen-bond with itself: it has no hydrogen bonded directly to O, N or F; methane fails for the same reason, since C–H is not polar enough — carbon is not a highly electronegative element.
9. Ethane-1,2-diol dissolves in water because of intermolecular hydrogen bond formation between its hydroxyl groups and water — most candidates could not phrase it (CP 2014).
10. Cinnamaldehyde dissolves in ethyl ethanoate because both are relatively non-polar compounds and their molecules are attracted by weak intermolecular forces — neither liquid can form hydrogen bonds with itself, since neither has an O–H bond (bank sentence).
11. Chitin is strong because of hydrogen bonds formed between C=O and N–H groups of the adjacent chains.
12. When a molecular substance melts, only the intermolecular forces are overcome — “only weak van der Waals’ forces need to be overcome in melting” is the bank wording; naming covalent bonds instead was punished (CP 2012 Q1).

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Intermolecular Forces

Hydrogen bond vs covalent bond

A hydrogen bond is an intermolecular attraction, weaker than any covalent bond, and it is not a kind of covalent bond. “H-bonds are weak covalent bonds” is the headline NOT-ACCEPT of this topic.

“vdW only in non-polar” vs the Part I demand

vdW act in polar substances too (HCl, H₂O). The skill is to differentiate vdW in non-polar and polar covalent substances — not to pretend polar molecules lack them.

Bond strength vs boiling-point cause

HF’s high bp is not because the H–F covalent bond is strong — boiling never touches it. It is the between-molecule hydrogen bonding. Two different columns on every answer sheet.

Molecular size vs “number of bonds”

The vdW factor is molecular size (the bank’s own reason). “More bonds ⇒ stronger” is sloppy: CS₂ (3 covalent bonds) beats CO₂ (3) on size, not bond count.

H-bonding “between BF₃ and NH₃”

BF₃ has no H and no F–H bond — impossible. That pair reacts by a dative bond: B accepts N’s lone pair to attain an octet (CP 2013 Q2).

“Dipole–dipole forces” as an answer

The sub-classification is not expected — writing it in (wrongly) invites contradiction; writing plain “van der Waals’ forces” never does (NTF-2018 II-B VI (b)).

Evaporation vs boiling language

Both overcome intermolecular forces; the fast-evaporating liquid has the weaker ones. Keep “volatile = weak intermolecular forces”, not “weak covalent bonds” — same exam, one-word swap, mark lost.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Crystals & Non-octet Structures

C₆₀ “giant covalent” vs C₆₀ molecular

Inside each sphere the bonds are covalent, but the crystal is molecular: spheres held by vdW. “Melting C₆₀ breaks covalent bonds” and “CS₂/SiO₂ confusion” come from the same wrong swap (CP 2012 Q16; CP 2014; CP 2016).

Ice “bonds break” — which bonds?

Melting ice weakens/breaks hydrogen bonds between molecules; the O–H covalent bonds never do. Write “between molecules” explicitly — it is the phrase that carries the mark.

“Ice is lighter” vs “less dense”

“Lighter” compares masses (meaningless without amounts); the property is density: same mass occupies a larger volume in the open structure, so ice floats. CP 2013 Q1 candidates couldn’t close this chain.

Non-octet = “wrong diagram”

Students “correct” BF₃ to an octet by adding a lone pair to B — then it is no longer BF₃. B is genuinely electron-deficient; P and S genuinely exceed eight here. Draw what is.

Melting point plateau vs decomposing

A plateau means a physical change: same molecules either side. If a substance decomposes, covalent bonds break and the particles change identity — different question type entirely.

“Structure” vs “shape” vocabulary

Ice and C₆₀ have structures (crystal arrangements); NH₃ and BF₃ have shapes (molecular geometry, Engine D). The syllabus keeps the words apart — so do your answers.

$$\text{BF}_3{:} \quad \text{B at the centre of a flat triangle of three F atoms — all on the same plane}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • BF₃ is the bank’s trigonal-planar exemplar — draw it flat on the page: three B–F bonds radiating evenly.
  • Its flatness is the polarity argument: “the three polar B–F bonds are symmetrically arranged on the same plane” → non-polar.
  • It is also non-octet (6 electrons on B, strand d) — the two facts share one diagram.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2013 Q2: NH₃’s 3-D shape was drawn well “but not that of BF₃” — don’t pyramid-ise BF₃ by habit; B has no lone pair to lift it off the plane.
VI (e)

Tetrahedral — 4 bonded atoms, 0 lone pairs

$$\text{CH}_4,\ \text{CCl}_4,\ \text{CH}_2\text{Br}_2,\ \text{CHCl}_3 \qquad \text{C at the heart of a tetrahedron of four bonds}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Four bonded atoms, no lone pair on C ⇒ tetrahedral — always drawn in 3-D: two bonds plain in the plane, one wedged out, one dashed back.
  • Perfectly symmetric when the four corners match (CH₄, CCl₄) → non-polar; mixed corners (CH₂Br₂, CHCl₃) keep the tetrahedral shape but change the polarity verdict.
  • CP’s own figures: “a very high percentage … gave the correct three-dimensional, tetrahedral structure of a CH₂Br₂ molecule” — the shape is the easy mark; don’t flatten it.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • A plus-shaped flat CH₄ is a Topic II-era electron-diagram habit, not a 3-D shape. Wedge and dash are the marks of the trade.
VI (e)

Trigonal pyramidal — 3 bonded atoms + 1 lone pair

$$\text{NH}_3{:} \quad \text{three N–H bonds down to a triangular base, one lone pair on top} \Rightarrow \text{a pyramid}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Draw N above the plane of its three H’s — trigonal pyramidal, and show the one lone pair on N when the diagram asks for electrons.
  • The MS polarity line is the shape line: “the central atom has a lone pair … three polar N–H bonds are not on the same plane” ⇒ polar molecule.
  • Contrast with BF₃ on purpose: three bonds each, different lone-pair count, different shape, different polarity.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CP 2013 Q2: “two lone electron pairs instead of one” on N — N has five valence electrons, one shared trio, one lone pair. Count, then draw.
VI (e)

Bent — 2 bonded atoms + 2 lone pairs

$$\text{H}_2\text{O}{:} \quad \text{H} - \text{O} - \text{H}\ \text{drawn as an angle}, \text{ two lone pairs on O} \Rightarrow \text{bent}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Water is bent (also accepted: V-shaped / non-linear): two O–H bonds + two lone pairs on O.
  • Bent ⇒ bond polarities not symmetrically arranged ⇒ polar molecule ⇒ hydrogen bonding in Engine B and the ice lattice in Engine C all follow from this one picture.
  • The whole Topic VI story on one line: O’s lone pairs shape water, water’s shape makes it polar, its polarity + O–H gives hydrogen bonds, hydrogen bonds give ice.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Never straighten H₂O to “copy” CO₂: both are X–central–Y, but C in CO₂ has no lone pairs — O in water has two, and that is the difference the paper tests.
VI (e)+(d)

Non-octet names — PCl₅ trigonal bipyramidal · SF₆ octahedral

$$\text{PCl}_5\ (5 \text{ bonded Cl, no lone pair}) \Rightarrow \text{trigonal bipyramidal} \qquad \text{SF}_6\ (6 \text{ bonded F}) \Rightarrow \text{octahedral}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • These two names close the syllabus list — with BF₃ they are the only non-octet cases examinable, and their central atoms carry no lone pairs (NTF-2018 II-B VI (e)).
  • PCl₅ drawing aid: three Cl in an equatorial plane + one above, one below (axial) — 5 neighbours, 10 electrons on P.
  • SF₆ drawing aid: an octahedron — 6 neighbours, 12 electrons on S. Name + tidy 3-D sketch, angles not required.
  • Diagram hygiene: every terminal Cl/F keeps its three lone pairs (CP 2016 dropped marks here).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Trigonal bipyramidal” is two words welded, not “bipyramid”, and SF₆ is octahedral, never “square pyramid” — exact naming is worth a whole mark.

The Count → Shape Board — the closed syllabus list

Two counts decide everything. Anything outside this table is not examinable (polyatomic ions, extra central atoms, lone pairs on non-octet centres).

Step ① · Count bonded neighbours of the central atom

Double and triple bonds count as one neighbour each (CO₂: two O neighbours). This is the count of atoms around the centre, not of electron dots.

Step ② · Count lone pairs LEFT ON THE CENTRAL ATOM

From the dot-and-cross diagram. 0 → the shape hugs symmetry; 1 → one lone pair pushes (NH₃); 2 → water bends. Terminal atoms’ lone pairs do not affect the shape (but must still be drawn when electrons are asked for).

Step ③ · Name it from the table below

Seven names exist in this syllabus: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, bent, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral. Spelling is part of the mark.

Step ④ · Draw it in 3-D

In-plane bonds as lines; one bond wedged (towards reader); one dashed (away). Tetrahedral, pyramidal and bent shapes must look non-flat. No angles labelled — stating them is not expected.

Multi-blank Quiz — Shapes

1. The examined demand is to predict and draw three-dimensional diagrams; stating bond angles is not expected.
2. Shapes examinable are molecules with central atoms obeying the octet rule, plus non-octet ones with no lone pair on the central atom — the named three are BF₃, PCl₅, SF₆.
3. CO₂ and CS₂ are both linear — the bank writes CS₂ as S=C=S; a bent drawing for either was a recorded wrong answer (CP 2016).
4. BF₃ is trigonal planar with six electrons around B, and it is non-polar: “the three polar B–F bonds are symmetrically arranged on the same plane.”
5. CH₄, CCl₄ and CH₂Br₂ are all tetrahedral; draw with one wedged and one dashed bond so the picture is truly 3-D.
6. NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal because the central atom has one lone pair — the MS notes the three polar N–H bonds are “not on the same plane”.
7. H₂O is bent: two bonded atoms and two lone pairs on oxygen; bending water straight would wrongly make it non-polar.
8. PCl₅ (five Cl, no lone pair on P) is trigonal bipyramidal; SF₆ is octahedral; their diagrams must keep every terminal atom’s lone pairs visible.
9. In the count→shape route, a C=O double bond adds one neighbour, and lone pairs are counted only on the central atom; shapes of polyatomic ions are not expected.
10. Shape feeds verdicts: linear CO₂ and tetrahedral CCl₄ are non-polar (symmetric arrangement), while pyramidal NH₃ and bent H₂O are polar — and bent water plus its hydrogen bonds build the open structure of ice.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Shapes

3-D diagram vs electron diagram

“Draw the shape” wants sticks and wedges; “draw the electron diagram” wants dots and lone pairs. CP 2016: candidates wrote dot-and-cross pictures where a 3-D structure was asked — wrong representation, no mark.

Linear CO₂/CS₂ vs bent H₂O

Both have a central atom holding exactly two neighbours — same connectivity, opposite shapes: C in CO₂/CS₂ carries no lone pair; O in H₂O carries two. Never copy a shape from a formula’s letters.

Tetrahedral vs “a cross in a square”

A flat plus-sign CH₄ is the 2-D diagram from Topic II; the 3-D tetrahedral must wedge one bond and dash one. CP 2016 says most could do it — be “most”.

One lone pair (NH₃) vs two (H₂O) vs zero (BF₃)

The lone-pair count on the centre is the shape’s switch. CP 2013 Q2 punished “two lone electron pairs instead of one” on N; and BF₃ has none — hence flat.

Shape names vs angle numbers

Naming “tetrahedral” is required; stating a numerical bond angle is not expected — and a misremembered angle is wasted ink at best (NTF-2018 II-B VI (e)). This studio never states an angle.

Explaining vs predicting

You must predict and draw; you need not explain shapes with repulsion theory, and ions/multi-central molecules are out (NTF-2018 II-B VI (e)). If a stem asks “explain”, it wants MS polarity-style wording (“lone pair … not on the same plane”), not VSEPR essays.
